# Memory for Attention: Language-Conditioned Re-Perception with a Vision–Language–Motion Map 

Dibyendu Ghosh 

Abstract — A robot carrying a persistent, behavior-annotated map faces two very different planning questions, and its memory answers only one of them well. The spatial-navigation 

question – how to walk around a room – we address first, and report a negative: building on Vision–Language–Motion Maps (VLMM) [1], a behavior-aware planner cost cuts a planning-time objective by ∼35% over 28 AI2-THOR scenes, but under closed-loop execution the real benefit nearly vanishes ( ∼4%) and an on-demand vision–language model (VLM) does as well. The resource-allocation question is different: under a limited perception budget, what should the robot attend to right now to keep its own map fresh? Framing re-perception as this attention decision – which map elements to re-observe, and how often – we show a persistent map’s memory (change-history, or even just recency of last sighting) yields the best re-perception schedule (held-out), matching an oracle, while the memoryless on-demand VLM prior is a poor scheduler. Because the schedule reallocates the budget toward what matters, memory’s benefit concentrates on the important objects ( ∼1.6 × the mean: +7 .7% vs. +4 .9% 

at high heterogeneity), and on a downstream fetch task it yields up to 5% fewer wasted trips than the prior; the gain grows with per-instance heterogeneity exactly as a Cauchy– Schwarz bound predicts – it equals Var( √λ), the variance of root-volatility. With a real CLIP prior on rendered objects the advantage is +21 –26% , its appearance-independent core being the heterogeneity slice. The distinctive value of the map appears when the task is language-conditioned : told what to keep track of, VLMM grounds the relevant objects (open-vocabulary) and tracks their change (memory), beating even a strong relevance-weighted recency baseline ( +2 .5% ) – so its motion channel adds value beyond a last-seen timestamp – and an on-demand VLM (+8 .9% ); neither language nor dynamics alone suffices. The map earns its keep not by telling the robot how to walk around a room, but by telling it what to pay attention to. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

The environments a robot operates in rarely hold still: objects are moved, doors open, things are set down and picked up. A map that records only what and where things are is therefore perpetually going out of date; to act well, a robot benefits from also knowing how the scene behaves. Open-vocabulary maps ground language to navigation goals – VLMaps [2] localises language-specified landmarks, and ConceptFusion [3], ConceptGraphs [4], and HOV-SG [5] attach features to voxels or object graphs – but assume a 

static world. A separate line adds behavior: Khronos [6] builds a spatio-temporal map, and Dewan et al. [7] clas-sify LiDAR points as non-movable, movable, or dynamic. Vision–Language– Motion Maps (VLMM) [1] combine both, annotating each element with an open-vocabulary feature, a 

movability prior ρ ∈ [0 , 1] , an observed cross-frame motion score o, and a per-element confidence γ.Given such a map, what is it for ? The obvious use is 

spatial : feed the behavior fields to the planner so the robot routes around what might move. We pursue this first and find it wanting. One can encode the map into a planner cost – a route is good not only if short but if it stays valid ,avoiding regions where objects churn – and on a planning-time objective it helps substantially. But under closed-loop execution the benefit largely washes out (§III-I): low-level control and reactive replanning already handle a mover that appears ahead [8], so shaping the global path around possible 

motion buys little, and an on-demand query does as well as the map’s memory. Answering “how do I walk around this room?” is not where a behavior map earns its keep. Its distinctive value lies in a different kind of planning. A robot’s perception is budgeted – it cannot re-observe every object at every step, whether because sensing, travel, or computation is limited. It must therefore decide what to attend to : which map entries to re-check to keep the representation fresh as the scene changes. This is a resource-allocation decision, answering “what should I pay attention to right now?” rather than “how do I move?”, and it is precisely where a persistent map’s memory of what has actually been changing should help – and where an on-demand vision–language model (VLM), which reports only category-level movability, should not. Dynamic and lifelong maps [9]–[11] re-perceive and re-map, but treat when to look as a fixed schedule or a coverage sweep; we ask instead how the map’s own history should direct that attention. Scene-graph memory can even predict where change oc-curs from accumulated observation [12], yet existing dy-namic maps still take when and which to re-observe as given. Deciding where to look is studied in active perception [13], [14], next-best-view and active mapping [15], [16], and persistent monitoring [17], [18], which bounds the weighted latency between observations – but from geometric coverage or known dynamics. The refresh problem itself is classic in web crawling [19] (a value-weighted √·-law optimum), a restless multi-armed bandit [20] whose Whittle index we adopt, and underlies age-of-information scheduling [21], [22] and change-rate estimation [23]. Taking VLMM as the map, we drive the schedule from its own observed per-instance change history and ask when that beats an on-demand category prior.

Where the rate estimate comes from. Two sources com-pete. An on-demand VLM returns the category movability prior ρi – one value per object type . The map’s memory ac-cumulates each element’s observed change history and forms a Bayesian rate ˆλi = ( mi + κρ i)/(ti + κ) from mi observed changes over ti observed time (pseudo-count κ, starting at the prior). We caution that the √·-schedule is optimal for the continuous surrogate of Prop. 1, not necessarily the discrete budgeted scheduler; §III therefore reports a held-out estimate and separates the memory-attributable gain from a schedule-level floor. 

Heterogeneity is what memory buys. By Prop. 2, the prior is exact only when true rates match the category norm; its loss grows with per-instance deviation. We model this with a knob h ∈ [0 , 1] : λi = λmax [(1 − h)ρi + h u i],

ui ∼ U(0 , 1) . At h=0 every instance equals its category prior (memory has nothing to add); at h=1 instance rates are independent of category (only history can know them). Real scenes lie in between – two chairs of the same type differ in how often they are used – so the memory’s value is an empirical question of how much heterogeneity a deployment holds. III. A. Memory directs attention better than an on-demand prior 

Over 951 objects (Fig. 2), the held-out history lowers value-weighted staleness below the on-demand prior by a margin that grows with heterogeneity: 1.7, 1.9, 2.3, 3.4, 6.5% 

at h=0 , 0.25 , 0.5, 0.75 , 1.0. Two caveats bound this. First, 

∼1.7% persists at h=0 , where there is no per-instance vari-ation to learn; this floor is not memory but a suboptimality of the √·-schedule on the discrete problem (a noisy estimate can out-schedule the true-rate one). The memory-attributable 

effect is therefore the heterogeneity-driven increase – up to ∼5 points at h=1 , tapering to nothing when scenes are homogeneous. Second, a naive online estimator inflates the advantage by a further ∼1% by adapting on the same realisation it is scored on; the held-out protocol removes this. Net: the map’s memory earns its place in the attention policy when the decision is per-instance and instances depart from their category norm. 

Memory protects the objects that matter. The √wiλi

schedule reallocates the budget toward important entries, so memory’s benefit is concentrated on them. Restricted to the top-10% -importance objects, the memory-attributable reduction (net of the h=0 floor) reaches +7 .7% at h=1 ,versus +4 .9% on the mean – a ∼1.6 × concentration on what matters. (We report the floor-subtracted figure: the raw top-10% gap is ∼11% already at h=0 , where there is no per-instance information, so it is the schedule artifact of Table II, not memory.) Attention is this reallocation – memory trades freshness on churny unimportant objects to protect the fragile, task-relevant ones.

G. Downstream fetch task: does fresher memory help the robot? 

Staleness is an intrinsic proxy; we put it to a task. On 

25 live AI2-THOR scenes the robot runs an object-fetch loop under the budget: objects relocate at λi, the robot re-observes K entries per its schedule (updating remembered locations), and requests arrive for object j ∼ wj ; a request to a stale entry is a wasted trip, costing the extra travel to the true location – a geometric cost the staleness objective never sees. The result confirms the effect and sharpens the reframe. Memory-scheduling incurs fewer wasted trips than the on-demand prior – +4 .9% at h=1 , matched in excess travel ( +4 .4% , t=4 .4) – so a fresher map does translate to a cheaper task. But at moderate heterogeneity ( h=0 .5)memory ties the prior ( +0 .3% , n.s.), and the memory-light 

oldest-first recency heuristic matches or beats memory on the task ( −4.2% at h=0 .5, tie at h=1 ). The lesson: what beats the memoryless on-demand prior is using the map’s observations at all – as change-history or simply as recency of last sighting; the persistent map earns its keep, but on this generic, importance-random fetch task sophisticated per-instance rate modelling is not needed over cheap recency; the rate channel’s distinct value re-emerges under language conditioning ( §III-F), where it beats a relevance-weighted recency baseline ( +2 .5% , t=7 .4).

H. Real perception: memory needs reliable observation 

The observation model so far is an abstract bit-flip; we now ground it in real AI2-THOR perception. Over 138 

objects in 8 scenes we measure each object’s detectability 

– rendering the scene from many agent poses and running instance detection, then taking the fraction of viewpoints from which the object is detected with a usable crop. It is low and heavy-tailed (mean 0.15 from random viewpoints; small or occluded objects are rarely seen), and we run the re-perception loop with each observation succeeding at its real rate. The memory advantage turns out to be conditional on observation reliability (Table V): scaling the measured detectability to a mean success of 0.3 or above – a robot that navigates to a usable viewpoint – memory beats the prior by 

+2 to +2 .4% , but at the pessimistic random-viewpoint mean of 0.15 it reverses to −2.8% . The mechanism is structural, and matches the observation-noise sweep: memory’s rate es-timate is only as good as its observations, so when perception frequently fails a fixed category prior wins. Observed history helps for re-perception provided the robot can actually re-perceive .

I. Why not path-shaping: the spatial-navigation negative 

For completeness we report the spatial use that motivated the shift. Encoding VLMM’s fields as a planner cost and routing to minimise expected disruptions cuts that objective 

∼35% over 28 live scenes. But the objective is close to what the cost minimises; on an independent closed-loop execution (objects actually relocating, counting real replans) the reduction is only ∼4% at +14% travel, and the map’s observed history does no better than – indeed slightly worse than – an on-demand prior ( 4.6 vs. 4.3 replans). The reason is structural: routing aggregates cost over a region , and summation averages out per-instance deviation, so a coarse prior suffices. The per-instance decision – which element to attend to – is where the prior’s bias is exposed and the memory pays off, exactly as Prop. 2 predicts. This refines rather than contradicts the case for behavior annotation [1]: the movability/motion attribute is evaluated there as a map-quality attribute; its downstream payoff, we find, lies in re-perception, not in path-shaping – a planning-time proxy improves ∼35% but the closed-loop gap is small. IV. L IMITATIONS 

(i) The change model is parametric; effects scale with per-instance heterogeneity h, whose real-world value we do not measure. (ii) We test a real CLIP prior on rendered AI2-THOR objects, but the change process and re-observation are still simulated; a live VLM and real longitudinal scene dynamics (natural, measured heterogeneity) together are the key experiment left to future work. (iii) The √·-schedule (Prop. 1) is optimal for a continuous surrogate, not the discrete scheduler; this creates the h=0 floor, which the Whittle-index reference removes – there memory beats the prior only for real heterogeneity, with no floor to subtract, so our headline √·-law numbers are if anything conservative. (iv) The magnitude is modest ( ∼1–5% memory-attributable on the value-weighted mean; ∼1.6 × that on the important objects the schedule protects) and grows with importance skew and budget (Table III). (v) The rate channel’s edge over a memory-light recency heuristic (oldest-first) is setting-dependent: negligible on the generic fetch task, but small and significant ( +2 .5% , t=7 .4) under language conditioning. The robust claim is that observation-based scheduling beats the memoryless prior; the rate model’s advantage over recency is real but modest. (vi) The advantage is conditional on observation reliability : under real AI2-THOR detectability it holds only above a ∼0.3 success rate and reverses below it, since memory’s estimate is only as good as its observations. V. C ONCLUSIONS 

A behavior-annotated map is asked two questions, and answers them differently. To “how do I walk around this room?” its memory adds little – reactive replanning and an on-demand query suffice. To “what should I attend to right now?” – which element to re-observe, under a budget, to keep the map fresh – the map’s memory of what has actually been changing beats a memoryless on-demand prior, by a margin that grows with per-instance heterogeneity (precisely 

Var( √λ), the variance of the scene’s root-volatility, Cor. 1) and that a Whittle-index reference confirms is real rather than a scheduler artifact. What is non-substitutable is the map’s 

persistence itself: whether as per-instance change-history or merely recency of last sighting, using past observations beats querying perception on demand. And the map’s full combi-nation earns its keep in language-conditioned re-perception – told what to keep track of, it grounds the relevant objects and tracks their change, a task a static open-vocabulary map and a plain dynamics map both fail, and one where tracking per-instance change beats even a relevance-weighted recency heuristic, so the motion channel is more than a timestamp.
