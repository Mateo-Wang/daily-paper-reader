Abstract: Recent Vision-Language-Action (VLA) methods improve generaliza-tion by aligning their representations with 3D scene geometry. However, these methods are fundamentally instruction-agnostic: the representations align the en-tire scene uniformly, neglecting the 3D geometry of the specific target object des-ignated by the language instruction. This causes failures on fine-grained manip-ulation and target occlusion tasks, where success depends on accurate 3D un-derstanding of the target object rather than the entire scene. To address this, we present Mind-VLA , an instruction-aware spatial representation alignment method for VLA models. Specifically, Mind-VLA first obtains the target object speci-fied by the language instruction, then prepares its target-object tri-view and ex-tracts the corresponding VAE and VGGT features. Finally, the latent represen-tation of the VLA model is aligned with these features to enable instruction-aware 3D understanding. Mind-VLA reaches 93.9% on LIBERO and 4.47 on CALVIN with a compact 345M-parameter backbone. On real-robot tasks with tar-get occlusion, Mind-VLA reaches 54% average success, outperforming the best-performing instruction-agnostic method in real-robot comparison by 32 percent-age points. Code will be publicly available. 

Keywords: Vision-Language-Action Models, Instruction-Aware Spatial Align-ment, Robotic Manipulation 

## 1 Introduction 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have become an important approach for generalist robotic manipulation, mapping language instructions and visual observations directly to robot actions [1, 2, 3, 4]. However, many manipulation tasks require the VLA models to go beyond 2D visual recog-nition and reason about object shape, pose, spatial relations, and occlusion. To improve the 3D understanding of VLA models, recent methods use 3D scene information in two main ways. The first (Fig. 1a) [5, 6, 7] provides explicit 3D information as additional model input, such as point-cloud features, RGB-D observations, or rendered orthographic views. These methods can improve spatial reasoning, but they usually require depth sensors, depth estimation, or extra 3D processing during inference, increasing the deployment cost. The second (Fig. 1b) [8, 9] extracts 3D scene features with frozen 3D foundation models and aligns VLA representations to these features dur-ing training. This training-time alignment keeps the model input unchanged at inference, reduces deployment cost, and has therefore become a mainstream paradigm for building 3D-aware VLAs. However, existing 3D-aware VLA methods are still instruction-agnostic. Their 3D input or super-vision is usually built from the whole scene, regardless of which object is specified by the language instruction. This weakens the connection between the instruction and the 3D representation. For ma-nipulation tasks, the most important geometry is often not the whole scene, but the object that the robot is asked to manipulate. When the target object is visually similar to nearby objects, instruction-agnostic supervision may make target discrimination harder. When the target is partially occluded, it may also make the VLA model less reliable, because the model has not been explicitly trained to preserve the 3D structure of the instructed object. To address this problem, we propose Mind-VLA, an instruction-aware spatial representation align-ment method for VLA models (Fig. 1c). Mind-VLA aligns the VLA representation with 3D features of the instruction-specified target object instead of only using scene-level 3D features. Specifi-cally, Mind-VLA first obtains the target object referred to by the instruction. It then prepares the target-object tri-view and extracts the corresponding VAE and VGGT features. Finally, the latent representation of the VLA model is aligned with these features to enable instruction-aware 3D un-derstanding. In practice, VAE latent prediction encourages the model to encode the target object’s geometry, while VGGT feature alignment guides intermediate VLA features toward target-object 3D structure. These auxiliary modules are only used during training and are removed at inference time, so Mind-VLA adds negligible deployment overhead. Our main contributions are as follows: (1) We identify instruction-agnostic 3D modeling as a lim-itation of existing 3D-aware VLA methods. Since their 3D information is built from the whole scene, they do not explicitly emphasize the object specified by the language instruction, which can hurt fine-grained manipulation and target-occlusion tasks. (2) We propose Mind-VLA, which aligns VLA representations with target-object 3D features. Mind-VLA uses target-object tri-view VAE latent prediction and target-object VGGT feature alignment during training, enabling instruction-aware 3D understanding. (3) Mind-VLA achieves 93.9% average success on LIBERO and 4.47 average completed length on CALVIN with a compact 345M-parameter backbone. On real-robot tasks with target occlusion, Mind-VLA reaches 54% average success, outperforming Seer [10] by 32 percentage points in our real-robot comparison.

RGB and language, but offer little explicit pressure to encode the 3D geometry of the instruction-specified object. To supply this geometry, recent work follows two directions. The first provides explicit 3D inputs. Before VLAs, 3D manipulation policies showed the value of explicit 3D representations through voxel [20], point [21], virtual-view [22, 23], and diffusion [24] designs, typically with multi-view RGB-D sensing and narrower task scope. Recent 3D-aware VLAs carry this to inference by feeding point clouds [5, 6, 25], stereo observations [26], or RGB-D ortho-graphic views [7]; while effective, they add depth or 3D preprocessing and build full-scene rather than target-object-specific geometry. The second direction instead supplies 3D information only as training supervision: Spatial Forcing [8] aligns VLA features to VGGT [27] scene features, GLaD [9] distills them across layers, and QDepth-VLA [28] predicts quantized depth tokens. This avoids deployment-time 3D modules, but the supervision remains global: the same scene represen-tation supervises the policy regardless of the instructed object. A related family uses predictive auxiliary objectives to enrich policy representations: DreamVLA [29] predicts motion and semantic cues, PALM [30] uses progress-aware affordances, and Geometry Forcing [31] transfers VGGT features through video generation. Mind-VLA is com-plementary: it keeps inference inputs unchanged while making the supervision instruction-aware, aligning VLA representations with the target-object tri-view and its VGGT features rather than scene-level geometry alone. 

## 3 Method 

This section describes Mind-VLA. We first introduce the base VLA method, its action loss, and the training-time auxiliary-supervision paradigm in Sec. 3.1. We then present Mind-VLA’s instruction-aware supervision in Sec. 3.2, including target-object tri-view prediction, multi-level VGGT align-ment, and the full training objective. The auxiliary branches are used only during training; at infer-ence, Mind-VLA keeps the same RGB, language, and state inputs as the base VLA. 33.1 Preliminaries: Vision-Language-Action Methods 

Existing VLA methods map a language instruction, visual observations, and proprioceptive state to a chunk of future robot actions [1, 2, 4], and recent variants augment this base with training-time auxiliary supervision to inject geometric and predictive signals into the backbone. We adopt this formulation as the base VLA method: inputs are tokenized and processed by a causal transformer, and action queries condition a diffusion action head trained with Lact . We elaborate on inputs and tokenization, action prediction, and the auxiliary-supervision paradigm in turn. 

Inputs and tokenization. At each timestep t, two RGB images Ipt , Iwt ∈ R224 ×224 ×3 from a primary and a wrist camera are encoded by a frozen vision encoder; the language instruction ℓ is en-coded by a frozen CLIP [33] text encoder; and the proprioceptive state st ∈ R8 is encoded by a linear projection. The resulting tokens, together with a group of learnable action queries qat appended to the sequence, are processed by a causal transformer over a context window of K timesteps. We denote the final-layer transformer outputs at the action-query positions as Hat ∈ RTa×d.

Action prediction. Conditioned on Hat , a diffusion action head [32] predicts a chunk of Ta future 7-D actions. Let At = [ at, . . . , at+Ta−1] ∈ RTa×7 denote the ground-truth action chunk. Given a diffusion timestep τ and Gaussian noise ϵ ∼ N (0, I), the head is trained to predict the added noise:

3.2 Instruction-Aware Spatial Representation Alignment 

Mind-VLA adds instruction-aware spatial representation alignment to the base VLA method. It introduces scene queries qst and object queries qtri  

> t

, each decoded by training-only auxiliary heads (Fig. 2). Let m(ℓ) be the target object referred to by instruction ℓ. Object queries are supervised by tri-view latent prediction and multi-level VGGT alignment on m(ℓ) rather than the full scene, while scene queries decode the scene-level auxiliaries Lobs and Ltraj introduced in Sec. 3.1, preserving workspace appearance and short-term dynamics. At inference, the method predicts actions without VAE, VGGT, or auxiliary decoders. 

Target-object tri-view. For each task, we prepare a target-object tri-view with top, front, and side as an offline preprocessing step, not as an online inference module. In simulation, these views are pre-rendered as orthographic projections from the object mesh. For real-robot experiments, each ob-ject is photographed once from three approximately canonical viewpoints, using a handheld camera 4without a calibrated rig or precise orthographic alignment. Each instruction is paired with a fixed target-object tri-view via the task definition; since the views are prepared offline, the supervision is unaffected by viewpoint changes or occlusion at inference.

## 4 Experiments 

This section evaluates Mind-VLA from benchmark performance to real-robot robustness. We first describe the benchmarks, implementation details, and baselines in Sec. 4.1. We then report main re-sults on LIBERO and long-horizon CALVIN ABC-D in Sec. 4.2. Next, Sec. 4.3 studies the contribu-tion of scene-level objectives, target-object tri-view prediction, and target-object VGGT alignment. Finally, Sec. 4.4 evaluates real-robot manipulation under normal and target-occluded conditions.

## 5 Conclusion 

We presented Mind-VLA, a VLA framework that conditions 3D supervision on the language in-struction. The training objective combines target-object tri-view latent prediction and multi-layer alignment with VGGT features extracted from the same tri-view; the corresponding auxiliary mod-ules are removed at inference. With a 345M backbone, Mind-VLA reaches 93.9% on LIBERO and 4.47 on CALVIN, and on a real xArm 6 it degrades less under occlusion than Seer [10]. 

## 6 Limitations 

Mind-VLA assumes a bounded object vocabulary: each target object requires a one-time target-object tri-view setup (rendered from mesh in simulation or photographed from approximately canonical viewpoints on the real robot). This privileged supervision improves occlusion robust-ness (Sec. 4.3) but precludes zero-shot deployment to arbitrary unseen objects. The method also maps each instruction to a single target object; accordingly, Mind-VLA (87.5%) trails DreamVLA (89.5%) on LIBERO-Long, suggesting that chained long-horizon tasks benefit from scene-level modeling. Our real-robot evaluation covers three task categories, two target objects, and 30 trials per condition; broader evaluation remains future work. 8References
