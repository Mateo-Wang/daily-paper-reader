# TRACT: Temporally Routed Action Chunks with Chronological Phase Authority for Contact-Rich Manipulation

Abstract —Action chunking shortens the effective decision hori-zon of robot imitation learning by predicting multiple future actions, while conventional phase conditioning describes the current control instant. When a predicted horizon crosses a procedural boundary, assigning the current phase to the entire chunk creates a structural temporal mismatch. We present TRACT, which factorizes phase-structured action chunking into an accepted current phase and a single CURRENT-to-NEXT boundary inside the future horizon. A task-local graph con-strains chronological phase authority, and a cumulative boundary distribution monotonically routes future queries through phase-specific query and action paths. For contact execution, a causal response-deficit integrator compares policy intent with ACK-eligible subsequent motion, accumulates arm compensation when directional response is suppressed, and decays after confirmed recovery. Across six real-robot variants with ten trials each, full TRACT achieves 10/10 full-sequence success, 99.00 [88.75, 100.00]% median [min, max] wipe completion, zero observed phase ambiguity, and zero stalls. Under the current complete method package and evaluation setting, the routed representation obtains better observed task results than the flat package (6/10 vs. 3/10 success; 77.08% vs. 8.03% median wipe completion). Chronological authority reduces observed phase ambiguity from 8/10 to 0/10, and response integration reduces stalls from 4/10 to 0/10. The package comparison does not isolate routing from other generator-package differences. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Long-horizon contact-rich manipulation requires a robot to acquire a tool, establish contact, sustain task motion, and re-turn the tool while preserving both physical contact and proce-dural order. Similar visual and proprioceptive states can occur during contact establishment, repeated wiping, and release, arXiv:2607.29285v1 [cs.RO] 31 Jul 2026 although their valid next actions differ. The resulting failures can include a delayed action transition after a phase switch, premature phase advance that terminates wiping, or phase-consistent stall under suppressed physical response (Fig. 1). Action chunking predicts a sequence of future actions at each policy query, reducing the effective decision length and producing smooth behavior from demonstrations. Ac-tion Chunking with Transformers (ACT) is a representative approach, combining sequence prediction with a temporal ensemble of overlapping chunks [1]. This future-action repre-sentation raises a separate temporal question: which procedural phase should each query in the predicted chunk represent? Near aprocedural boundary, however, the expert phase sequence can be ct, c t, c t, n (ct), n (ct), . . . , rather than a phase-homogeneous repetition of ct. This exposes two distinct ambiguities. Present-phase ambiguity concerns which phase is valid at the current physical instant. Future-boundary ambiguity concerns whether the predicted chunk crosses into the next phase and where that transition occurs. Current-phase conditioning addresses the former; the latter belongs to the predicted horizon. We introduce TRACT, a temporally routed action-chunk policy that assigns these decisions to their corresponding time scales. A chronological authority accepts the current phase under a task-local transition graph. A structured estimator then predicts a single boundary kt and routes future queries monotonically from the accepted current phase to its unique legal successor. Thus, the current phase is a property of the present control state, whereas the phase boundary is a property of the future action horizon. Fig. 1(b) contrasts this routed horizon with a current-phase-conditioned chunk at the P2 →P3 boundary. We therefore evaluate the complete routed package against a flat phase-conditioned package under matched conditions; the comparison is package-level and does not isolate routing from other generator-package differences. Contact friction creates an additional execution problem: a semantically correct chunk may produce less physical motion than intended. TRACT closes this response loop using only causal execution evidence. An integrator compares model intent with the subsequent response only under a matching ACKed execution lineage, accumulates deficit along the cur-rent policy direction, and decays after confirmed directional-speed recovery. This execution closure uses task-derived nor-malization and phase thresholds without additional demonstra-tions, while preserving phase, route, and gripper semantics. This paper makes three contributions:  

> •

We formulate phase-structured action chunking as two temporally distinct decisions: present-phase acceptance and future-boundary routing.  

> •

We develop chronological phase authority and a graph-constrained, monotone CURRENT-to-NEXT route that assigns phase semantics to future action queries.  

> •

We construct a causal execution closure for persistent contact-response deficit and evaluate six controlled real-robot variants; under the current complete method pack-age and evaluation setting, the routed and flat packages obtain 6/10 and 3/10 observed success, respectively. The evaluation is a mechanism-focused feasibility study on one multi-stage planar-wiping task. Each variant is tested in ten complete trials under matched sensing, control, data, action horizon, and hardware conditions. II. R ELATED WORK 

Action chunks as future representations. ACT predicts action chunks and temporally ensembles overlapping predic-tions [1]; Diffusion Policy generates receding-horizon action sequences through conditional diffusion [2]. Subsequent work improves bimanual interdependence, hierarchical control, test-time decoding, token compression, and parallel action decod-ing [3]–[9]. These methods strengthen sequence generation and coordination. TRACT adds an ordered procedural route to the future sequence, turning a flat action chunk into a temporally heterogeneous object. 

Phase and semantic conditioning. StageACT conditions low-level behavior on task stage to reduce partial observability and behavior aliasing [10]. PHASER combines current-phase conditioning with multimodal phase estimation and recovery logic [11]. These approaches assign procedural semantics to the current state. Semantic action representations also sepa-rate intent from execution detail [12]. MoE-ACT supervises phase-specific experts and predicts phase/expert weights for future actions [13]. TRACT parameterizes future semantics with an accepted current phase, its legal successor, and one boundary variable. The resulting route is an ordered two-segment sequence whose monotonicity follows directly from the representation. 

Chunk execution and physical response. Recent meth-ods modify how a generated chunk is consumed. Real-Time Chunking supports asynchronous generation and inpaint-ing [14]; Legato learns chunk continuation [15]; BID and Temporal Action Selection improve test-time consistency or choose among historical chunks [5], [16]. PACE and dynamic execution-horizon prediction select how much of a chunk to execute [17], [18], while set-supervised diffusion and neural-ODE imitation improve correction learning or multi-skill dy-namics [19], [20]. These mechanisms determine when, how much, or which generated action should be executed. TRACT determines which procedural semantics each future query represents during generation. For physical feedback, Reactive Diffusion Policy couples slow action chunks with fast tactile response [21]. TRACT instead uses the visual–proprioceptive command lineage to estimate intent-aligned response deficit and sustain contact motion. III. M ETHOD

A. Task, Data, and Protocol 

We evaluate one fixed-scene, multi-stage planar-wiping task using a Franka Panda, Franka Hand, and Cartesian impedance control. Multi-view RGB and a 13-D Cartesian robot state form the observation. Cameras acquire at 60 Hz; policy infer-ence and action execution run at 30 Hz; the low-level controller runs at 1000 Hz. The action horizon contains 30 queries, ap-proximately one second. All learned components use the same 50 demonstrations. Training follows the separated curriculum above: routed Nominal training and causal-history refinement precede freezing of the action generator and ordinary TE; supervised Manager fitting and offline phase autoregression are then performed without updating the Nominal path. Integrator action normalization and task-specific phase parameters are estimated only after this freeze. Each of six variants is run for ten complete real-robot trials, with all outcomes scored at the trial level. Thus, the evidence is descriptive feasibility evidence for one fixed task, not a statistical generalization study. Data, hardware, sensor streams, action interface, chunk horizon, and ordinary TE are matched. 

A0 is ordinary ACT. A1 adds accepted current phase as an observation embedding. TP removes the advance posterior and chronological Phase Manager acceptance, using the raw phase predictor for phase switching. TU is a prior-work-style flat phase-conditioned action chunk: it retains accepted current phase and the shared temporal trunk, but uses a separately trained shared query/action path without intra-chunk routing. 

TR is full TRACT with the integrator disabled. TF uses the same TR checkpoint and configuration with only the integrator enabled.

B. Metrics Full-sequence success requires autonomous completion in a valid task order, including any P3 continuation or au-thorized P4 →P2 re-wipe. Wipe completion is the removed fraction of central-board marker pixels between global start and end images. Local black-hat enhancement followed by Otsu thresholding yields start and end areas S and E; the score is (S − E)/S × 100% . We report median [minimum, maximum] over all ten trials. Observed phase ambiguity is a trial-level external review of whether an action semantic pattern is incompatible with the physical procedural context; it does not inspect an internal phase output and therefore also applies to A0. Stall denotes persistent valid commands without task progress or autonomous recovery, eventually requiring intervention or termination. The same predeclared intervention criterion is applied to all variants. 

C. Results 

Full system. TF completes all 10 trials with 99.00 [88.75, 100.00]% wipe completion, no observed phase ambiguity, and no stall (Table I). Fig. 3 provides the corresponding image evidence and completion distribution. 

Intra-chunk routing. The flat phase-conditioned TU ob-tains 3/10 success, 8.03 [0, 98.45]% wipe completion, and 7/10 stalls; routed TR reaches 6/10, 77.08 [64.44, 98.60]%, and 4/10. Under the current complete method package and evaluation setting, the routed representation obtains better observed task results (6/10 versus 3/10 success; 77.08% versus 8.03% median wipe completion). Representative TU execu-tions expose the temporal mismatch illustrated in Fig. 1: after an accepted P1 →P2 transition, stale P1 descent actions can displace the grasped tool; after P2 →P3, stale P2 descent can similarly perturb the tool before wiping. These delayed action transitions degrade contact geometry and can lead to ineffec-tive wiping or stall. This package comparison associates the routed representation with higher observed success and typical progress, but does not isolate routing from other generator-package differences. Ordinary ACT A0 obtains 0/10 success and no wiping, establishing that an unstructured action chunk is insufficient for this task. A1 remains at the same outcome after accepted current phase is appended to the observation, showing that present-phase information alone does not resolve the chunk representation. Together with TU –TR, these results support evaluating phase as an internal future-query routing variable rather than only as an observation feature. 

Chronological authority. Because TP removes the advance posterior and chronological Phase Manager, its raw phase switching produces observed phase ambiguity in 8/10 trials, typically a premature P3 →P4 transition soon after wiping be-gins. Under chronological authority, TR records 0/10 ambigu-ity. Its remaining 4/10 failures are phase-consistent mid-wipe stalls, separating procedural acceptance errors from physical response failures. Thus, TP –TR evaluates chronological phase authority. 

Causal response integration. TR and TF share the same checkpoint; only the causal response-deficit integrator switch changes. Enabling it raises success from 6/10 to 10/10, in-creases median wipe completion from 77.08% to 99.00%, and reduces stalls from 4/10 to 0/10 while phase ambiguity remains 0/10. The result supports intent-aligned deficit accumulation as the execution closure for persistent command–response mismatch under contact dynamics, such as friction or other causes of suppressed motion. Once projected directional speed satisfies the configured recovery condition, rapid decay lets the nominal policy organize subsequent behavior. Thus, TR–TF

evaluates causal response-deficit integration under execution stall. 

Mechanism audit. Frozen replay contains 7,105 crossing chunks among 66,173 horizon-30 samples (10.74%), with monotone routes and 99.80/83.60% crossing precision/recall. Manager acceptance raises framewise phase accuracy from 83.83% to 91.83%, completes 50/50 legal chains, and pro-duces no illegal transition; these diagnostics are separate from Table I’s closed-loop evidence. V. D ISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

TRACT makes the temporal extent of phase semantics explicit: chronological authority governs the present in-stant, while one cumulative boundary guarantees an ordered CURRENT-to-legal-NEXT route over the future horizon. Un-der the current complete method package and evaluation setting, the routed representation obtains better observed task results than the flat package (6/10 vs. 3/10 success; 77.08% vs. 8.03% median wipe completion), but this package comparison does not isolate routing from other generator-package dif-ferences. The runtime-matched TP –TR comparison evaluates chronological phase authority, while the same-checkpoint TR–

TF comparison evaluates causal response-deficit integration under execution stall. Correct phase semantics alone leave 4/10 TR trials stalled when realized motion remains below the policy intent under contact. Using ACKed command–response lineage, the causal integrator supplies temporary intent-aligned compensation and raises same-checkpoint success from 6/10 to 10/10. Routing provides chunk-level semantic consistency; response integra-tion sustains the resulting contact execution. The single-boundary contract assumes at most one transition to the selected successor per generated horizon. Horizons spanning two transitions require multiple boundaries, while branching graphs require successor selection. The registered P4 →P2 re-wipe is a new current-time authority event that resets lineage and starts a fresh P2 chunk. Evidence is limited to one fixed-scene planar-wiping task, one robot, and ten trials per condition. Within this controlled feasibility scope, the complete routed package obtains 6/10 success versus 3/10 for the flat package, while full TRACT with response integration reaches 10/10. These results support explicit factorization of present-phase authority and future-boundary routing, together with causal closure of contact response; they do not establish cross-task generalization or statistical population-level performance.
