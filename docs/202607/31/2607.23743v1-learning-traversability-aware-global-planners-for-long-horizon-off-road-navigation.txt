# Learning Traversability-Aware Global Planners for Long Horizon Off-Road Navigation 

Kasi Viswanath 1, Jason M. Gregory 2, Shaunak Kolhe 1 and Srikanth Saripalli 1

Abstract — Autonomous navigation across large off-road en-vironments remains a challenging problem. Onboard sensors perceive only the immediate surroundings, yet safe and efficient routes depend on terrain features that extend well beyond the sensor horizon. Geo-spatial data sources such as satellite imagery, aerial LiDAR, and vector maps can close this gap, but learning traversability from them is difficult: dense labels are unavailable at scale, and existing methods rely on short-range sensing. We propose an efficient formulation that learns a continuous traversability map from overhead data, supervised directly by human-driven GPS trajectories and shaped by self-supervised geometric priors from LiDAR. Alongside the model, we release a public dataset of 299 scenes spanning ∼1,244 km 2 of diverse terrain, paired with 1,130 km of human driving. In field trials on a Clearpath Warthog across seven routes at two sites, our method achieves trajectories within 3.66% of human path length and reduces operator interventions by ∼85% compared to local-planner-only autonomy. https://huggingface. co/datasets/anony-008/offroad-global-nav 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Navigation in urban environments is inherently hierarchi-cal: global route planners derived from map services produce road-level decisions optimized for travel time, traffic, or energy, which are then continuously refined by reactive local planners that respond to immediate context [1]. While this paradigm has been extensively studied and deployed at scale, its extension to off-road environments remains relatively underexplored. Off-road environments lack well-defined road networks, exhibit significant spatial variability, and often contain noisy or incomplete prior information. Feasible routes may not correspond to visible trails, and existing paths may be suboptimal or impassable. As a result, planning must op-erate directly over terrain, placing a strong emphasis on accurate traversability estimation over large spatial extents. Traversability maps are central to this process, encoding the safety and feasibility of regions while enabling the generation of diverse, goal-directed routes. Despite advances in perception and costmap construc-tion, effectively leveraging these representations for long-range planning remains an open challenge. Existing methods

II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Off-road Datasets 

The development of off-road navigation datasets has largely followed advances in learning-based perception. Early datasets such as RUGD [8] focused on semantic un-derstanding from RGB imagery, while later efforts including RELLIS-3D [9], GOOSE [10], DeepScene [11], and GO [12] incorporated multi-modal sensor suites with LiDAR, IMU, GPS, and RADAR to support tasks such as segmentation, localization, and local navigation. More recently, datasets have begun to incorporate driving trajectories to enable learning of terrain-aware traversability. Works such as DigiForest [13] and TartanDrive 2.0 [14] provide supervision for cost map learning from real-world in-teraction. However, these datasets are inherently constrained by onboard sensing, capturing detailed observations only along traversed paths and offering limited spatial coverage for long-range planning. Geospatial data offers a complementary perspective by providing large-scale coverage of terrain through modali-ties such as satellite imagery, aerial LiDAR, and digital elevation models. Public sources including ArcGIS [15], Sentinel-2 [16], USGS [17], OpenTopography [18], and OpenStreetMap [19] make such data widely accessible. While existing geospatial datasets such as EuroSAT [20], LandSat [21], and FLAIR [22] support large-scale semantic understanding, they are not designed for navigation and lack direct traversability supervision. 

B. Terrain Traversability 

Terrain traversability estimation has traditionally been studied in the context of local navigation. Early systems such as Stanley [2] relied on short-range planning within prede-fined corridors, with cost maps serving as the interface be-tween perception and control. Initial approaches constructed these maps using hand-engineered features and semantic class-to-cost association [3], later evolving to learning-based methods that integrate perception and proprioception to estimate terrain feasibility beyond the immediate sensor horizon [4]. The introduction of learning from demonstration further advanced this paradigm, where cost maps are optimized to align planned paths with expert driving behavior us-ing inverse reinforcement learning and deep reinforcement learning [5], [6]. More recent self-supervised approaches leverage vehicle dynamics and execution feedback to refine traversability [23], [24]. Despite these advances, such meth-ods remain inherently local and depend on predefined global routes for long-horizon navigation. In contrast, global traversability estimation in off-road environments remains relatively underexplored. Early ef-forts [25] utilized aerial imagery and LiDAR with simple models, limiting their ability to generalize across diverse terrain. More recent approaches, such as OVerSeeC [26], leverage vision-language models on satellite imagery but primarily rely on semantic reasoning, often lacking explicit geometric grounding. Trailblazer [27] integrates satellite imagery semantics, aerial LiDAR for cost map generation, but semantic abstrac-tion discards raw visual information and reliance on differen-tiable planners like Neural A* [28] constrains gradient flow. Overall, prior work either excels at local traversability estimation with limited spatial extent or leverages large-scale data with indirect supervision. Enabling long-horizon off-road navigation requires reasoning over multi-modal geo-spatial inputs while directly learning traversability aligned with human driving behavior. Our approach addresses this gap by learning dense traversability maps from satellite im-agery, LiDAR, and map priors using human demonstrations. III. D ATA COLLECTION AND PROCESSING 

Effective off-road navigation requires reasoning over het-erogeneous terrain cues that no single sensor can fully capture. High-resolution satellite imagery provides rich vi-sual context, vegetation, trails, and water bodies yet cannot resolve elevation or slope, which aerial LiDAR recovers directly. OSM complements both by supplying vector anno-tations of highways, trails, and waterways as strong semantic priors for the scene. We identified candidate off-road sites spanning a broad range of terrain types such as deserts, grasslands, ev-ergreen forests, mines, and mountainous regions, cross-referenced them with public GPS traces available through OpenStreetMap. After a feasibility study, we selected regions where all three modalities were jointly available. The U.S. Geological Survey (USGS)[17] distributes overhead LiDAR point clouds, covering more than 85% of the U.S. land area with a vertical accuracy of 10 cm (4 in). These point clouds are dense with 5–27 points per m2 and include semantic classification in many regions. Data from the USGS LiDAR Explorer [17] is divided into tiles , swaths , or projects 

typically organized in 1 km or 1.5 km grid units. For each selected region we downloaded the point cloud, then used its geographic bounds to extract co-registered 30 cm/pixel satellite imagery from ArcGIS [15], along with OSM vector features and public GPS traces. Data acquired are spread across seasons to capture natural variation in vegetation and surface conditions. The resulting dataset comprises 299 unique scenes span-ning approximately 1,244 km 2, paired with 1,130 km of human-driven GPS traces. Having established the data sources in Section III, we now describe how these heterogeneous geospatial inputs are trans-formed into a learned traversability representation. We first detail the preprocessing pipeline that aligns and rasterizes the inputs into grid-maps, then present the architecture that fuses them into path-likelihood and traversability map predictions. 

A. Data pre-processing 

LiDAR point clouds often contain tens of millions of points, so informative features must be extracted while com-pressing the representation with minimal information loss. We therefore design a preprocessing pipeline that rasterizes three complementary channels namely height, slope, and intensity into a grid map, capturing both geometric and radiometric structure relevant to traversability. The grid resolution Gres is chosen from the local point density (points/m 2) to ensure at least 15 points per cell, the minimum required for stable surface-normal and slope estimation. The point cloud is voxelized at 0 .25 · Gres to regularize point distribution and improve normal robustness. Slopes are computed as s = arccos (Nz), where Nz is the vertical component of the surface normal. LiDAR intensity acts as a helpful visual guide: vegetation and water absorb strongly and return low intensity, while trails, roads, and barren ground return high. To align this channel with the “higher value ⇒ less traversable” conven-tion used for height and slope, we normalize raw intensity 

I ∈ [0, 1] and store its complement ˜ I = 1 − I on the grid map. The three channels are rasterized at Gres , with each cell storing the mean of all enclosed points. OpenStreetMap features (highways, trails, waterways) are rasterized onto the same grid to provide semantic priors. Corresponding 30 cm /pixel satellite imagery is cropped and reprojected to match the LiDAR extents under a common datum (NAD83 UTM), preserving spatial consistency and precise cross-modal alignment. GPS traces from each scene are augmented to generate trajectories ranging from 200 m to 1 .5 km in length. Each trajectory is rasterized onto a grid map of resolution Gres ,with occupied cells set to 1 and background cells to 0. Start and goal points are subsequently extracted to construct a separate start–goal grid map. 

B. Model Architecture 

An overview of the proposed architecture is shown in Fig. 2, which is a dual-stream, multi-modal network that jointly processes satellite imagery and LiDAR-derived grid maps to produce a traversability map and path logits. All fu-sion is performed at a reduced resolution of 256 ×256 (stride 4), with predictions upsampled to the original 1024 ×1024 for loss computation and inference. 

1) Satellite Image Encoder: Satellite imagery is encoded by DINOv3-SAT, a ViT-L/16 pretrained on SAT-493M [29]. Final-layer patch tokens are reshaped into a 64 ×64 grid (p=16, d=1024). The encoder is kept frozen, as its satellite-domain pretraining provides semantically rich land-cover, vegetation, and geometry features that are difficult to recover from task-specific supervision alone. 

2) Feature Pyramid Network: : A FPN decodes the DINO features to the 256 ×256 fusion resolution. After a 1 ×1projection to 128 channels, a bottom-up pyramid of Ndown =3stride-2 stages is followed by top-down lateral merges with 3×3 GroupNorm–ReLU smoothing. The finest level is pro-gressively upsampled and compressed to 64 channels, pre-serving both fine spatial detail and abstract semantic context at fusion. 

3) LiDAR Encoder: The rasterized LiDAR grid maps of height, slope, intensity, and an OSM raster channel are encoded by a three-stage CNN (5 ×5, 3 ×3, 3 ×3 convolutions with GroupNorm–ReLU), yielding 64-channel features at full resolution. Learnable per-channel input scales are applied prior to encoding with the features then average-pooled to fusion resolution. 

4) Multi-Modal Fusion via FiLM: LiDAR and satellite features are fused via spatially-varying Feature-wise Lin-ear Modulation (FiLM) [30]. Modulation parameters γ, β ∈

RC×H×W are predicted from the DINO features by a Conv– GN–ReLU–Conv block and applied as: 

Ffused = FLiDAR ⊙ (1 + γ) + β (1) γ ∈ (−1, 1) is tanh-bounded to prevent degenerate scaling. A post-fusion Conv–GN–ReLU block further refines the output. Unlike the original scalar FiLM, the spatial maps let the network reweight LiDAR features according to local semantic context. 

5) Goal Conditioning: Start and goal positions are pro-vided as spatial heatmaps, encoded by a two-layer Conv– GN–ReLU CNN into 32-channel goal features, and concate-nated with the fused features to form a 96-channel represen-tation. This conditioning allows the network to produce goal-directed path hypotheses rather than scene-level traversability estimates alone. 

6) Post-Fusion Refinement and Output Heads: The 96-channel representation is refined by a shallow CNN (96 →64 →32 channels, 3 ×3 convolutions with GroupNorm– ReLU and Dropout2d ( p=0.15)). It then feeds two indepen-dent heads: a Path Logits Head and a Traversability Head ,each comprising Conv(3 ×3, 32 →16)–GN–ReLU–Dropout2d followed by a 1 ×1 convolution to a single output channel. The traversability map is sigmoid-normalized to [0, 1]. Both outputs are bilinearly upsampled to 1024 ×1024 prior to loss computation.

Offline benchmarking alone cannot capture the system-level effects that dominate real-world off-road deployment— hardware integration, inter-module coordination, and edge-case robustness. We therefore evaluate on a physical plat-form: a Clearpath Warthog equipped with the multi-modal sensor suite shown in Fig. 4. Field experiments were con-ducted at speeds up to 2 m /s. 

Fig. 4: Sensor suite integrated on the Warthog platform Our algorithm serves as the Long Range Planner (LRP) ,generating globally-consistent waypoint sequences from the robot’s current pose to a user-specified goal. These waypoints are consumed by ARL’s Phoenix autonomy stack, which handles short-range planning and control: Direct LiDAR-Inertial Odometry [33] for state estimation, and Model Pre-dictive Path Integral (MPPI) control [34] as the Short Range Planner (SRP) , operating over local costmaps from Terrain-Net [35] under the Warthog’s kinematic-bicycle constraints. The LRP thus provides the globally-aware plan while the SRP handles reactive obstacle avoidance from live onboard perception. 

A. Global Planner 

For downstream planning, the traversability map is trans-formed into a cost representation via an inverse mapping 

C = 1 − T , aligning high traversability with low planning cost. Given a goal, the LRP queries the resulting cost map to compute K-shortest paths using Yen’s algorithm [36], enabling route diversity beyond a single optimum. Each path is generated via A ∗ search [31] with a weighted objective that balances traversal cost ( α) and path length ( β ), allowing the planner to trade off safety and efficiency. The selected path is transformed from the map frame to UTM for global consistency and then discretized into waypoints. The resulting waypoint sequence is streamed to the SRP for reactive execution. VII. E XPERIMENTS AND INSIGHTS 

Through our experiments, we address the following ques-tions: 1) Does the learned costmap sufficiently reflect terrain characteristics derived from satellite, LiDAR, and map-based inputs? 2) Does the long-range planner yield trajectories that are measurably safer, shorter, or more reliable than those produced by local-planner-only autonomy? 3) Is the learned costmap expressive enough to yield multiple distinct yet feasible trajectories between the same start–goal pair? 4) How does the planner handle outdated costmaps when encountering unseen obstacles or terrain changes? Real-world experiments were conducted at two sites: a 

Levee site and an off-road site at Texas A&M’s RELLIS Campus. The Levee site features a 250 m levee embankment, an adjacent lake, and a segment of a runway (Figure 5a, 5b, 5c), and was selected to evaluate algorithm performance around slopes and water bodies. The off-road site spans more than 9 km 2 and encompasses diverse terrain types like grassland, quarry, trails, lakes, and deciduous forest (Figure 5d, 5e) enabling comprehensive evaluation across varied conditions. 

Fig. 5: Overview of the Levee test site (a, b), showing the 250 m levee embankment, adjacent lake, and runway segment, with its learned costmap (c) used to evaluate slope and water-body avoidance. Overview of the off-road site (d) and its costmap (e), with routes used for experiments. (f) Zoomed view demonstrating the model’s ability to identify traversable corridors through dense vegetation. 

Does the costmap sufficiently capture terrain features? 

We evaluated the learned costmap across diverse terrain, uphill slopes and water bodies at the Levee site, and dense vegetation and deep quarries at the off-road site. At the Levee site (Fig. 5c), the costmap correctly assigned high cost to the embankment slopes and adjacent water, guiding the robot through the levelled corridor between them. On Route 1, the flat region beyond the levee was initially occluded from the SRP’s local perception, causing it to climb the embankment and approach the water dangerously close, a failure mode the LRP avoided by routing around the levee with safe water-body clearance. At the off-road site, the LRP followed established trails as low-cost corridors while excluding quarries, negative obstacles that are difficult to detect from onboard sensors but clearly identifiable from overhead LiDAR geometry. The costmap also resolved traversable corridors through wooded areas (Fig. 5f). We note, however, that the costmap tends to be over-conservative in transitional zones such as grass-to-brush boundaries and drainage edges, contributing to the modest path-length overhead discussed next. This is consistent with our PU-supervised loss, which lacks explicit negative exam-ples of “traversable but unusual” terrain. 

Does the global planner outperform local-planner-only autonomy? 

We use human teleoperation tracks as the baseline, in which an operator manually navigates the robot along a pre-ferred route while avoiding obstacles. We then compare full-stack autonomy (LRP) against SRP-only navigation across identical routes. SRP-only runs were conducted on a limited subset of routes (Table III), as SRP relies on a pure-pursuit global guidance mechanism that poses unacceptable safety risks near hazards such as quarry edges or long horizon missions. How does the planner handle outdated costmaps with unseen obstacles? 

When the platform encounters an obstacle absent from the global costmap during execution, the SRP detects the blockage and halts the robot. This event triggers a replanning request to the LRP: a high-cost region is injected at the obstacle’s estimated location, and an alternative route is computed from the current position to the goal. During testing on Route 6, the platform encountered an unmarked sand heap (Figure 6); the LRP successfully replanned around it and reached the goal without further intervention. VIII. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

In this work, we presented a dual-stream multi-modal framework that learns continuous traversability maps from satellite imagery, aerial LiDAR, and OpenStreetMap priors, supervised directly by sparse human trajectories and self-supervised geometric priors. We demonstrate that the learned costmaps enable globally-aware, terrain-sensitive navigation that measurably outperforms local-planner-only autonomy in both path efficiency and operator intervention rate. However, the LRP–SRP interface remains sensitive to lookahead dis-tance and handoff timing, and static costmaps cannot account for transient obstacles or seasonal terrain change. Future work includes online costmap refinement fusing the global prior with onboard perception, and tighter LRP–SRP co-design which are prerequisites for reliable high-speed off-road autonomy. IX.
