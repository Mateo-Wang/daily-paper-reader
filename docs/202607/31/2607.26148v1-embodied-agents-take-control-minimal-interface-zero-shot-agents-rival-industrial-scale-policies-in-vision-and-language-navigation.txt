Abstract: Autonomous embodied agents must sustain a long decision-making loop that involves perceiving, acting, verifying, and self-correcting over many steps. Current systems sustain this loop through task-specific workflows or embodied policies. We study a third form, agentic embodied control , in which a general-purpose agent holds the loop itself. Using zero-shot navigation as a controlled testbed, we evaluate three software-engineering agent harnesses given only a monocular RGB camera and discrete actions. Under this strictly minimal condition, replicated default-effort configurations reach 70 .7 ± 3.5% success ( opus-5 , mean over three runs), and fable-5 reaches 78% at maximum effort. When a trained waypoint tool is exposed alongside primitives as an optional capability, the hybrid 

fable-5 agent reaches 76 .7 ± 0.6% at default effort, using half the environment steps and less than one quarter of the wall time of the maximum-effort primitive run. Controlled interventions show that capability is primarily model-centered: model choice strongly changes success, harness effects are descriptive, and a forced waypoint interface helps weaker models but can hinder stronger ones. Performance nevertheless falls sharply on longer-horizon tasks, while latency and context growth limit sustained operation. These results show that agentic control is already competitive in zero-shot navigation and that models, harnesses, and interfaces offer complementary paths toward autonomous embodied agents. 

Keywords: Embodied agents, zero-shot navigation, vision-language models 

1 Introduction 

Consider a household robot expected to operate autonomously day after day. It must find the kitchen, pick up a cup, and recover from mistakes without human rescue. Navigation and manipulation require different low-level controllers, but share the high-level challenge of reasoning over extended interaction. We ultimately seek an Autonomous Embodied Agent that can operate over extended interaction without repeated human rescue while managing its state, errors, and computational resources. Two streams of work have dominated progress toward this goal. The ‘trained’ stream internalizes navigation capability in a policy that maps observations to actions, as exemplified by NaVid and its successors [ 1, 2 ]. The ‘zero-shot’ stream keeps a general-purpose model frozen and supplies the missing navigation structure outside its weights. NavGPT and MapGPT, for example, place an LLM inside fixed workflows for spatial representation and planning [ 3, 4]. The first stream puts capability in the weights, whereas the other assembles it through scaffolding. More recently, industrial systems have combined the two ideas through slow–fast or “dual-brain” architectures. ABot-N1 and InternVLA-N1 couple a slow reasoning model to a trained fast action expert [ 5, 6 ]. These systems are more capable and hierarchical, but their handoff remains fixed. The slow stage emits a subgoal and the fast stage executes it. They are therefore trained workflows, not agentic systems. Architecture has deepened, but control of the interaction loop still lies in human-authored code. This organization now deserves reconsideration. Frontier vision–language models increasingly com-bine perception across turns, spatial reasoning, progress tracking, and long-horizon tool use. Agents developed for software engineering further show that models can sustain extended tool-mediated interaction through generic loops [ 7 , 8, 9]. Connecting successive observations, tracking progress, reasoning about action outcomes, and recovering from errors are also central to embodied behavior. Functions previously supplied by navigation-specific scaffolding may therefore be increasingly avail-able in the model itself. This raises a broader question: can the model take control of the embodied interaction loop? 

We study this question through vision-and-language navigation [ 10 ] because navigation requires language grounding, spatial reasoning, progress tracking, recovery, and stopping over extended interaction, while discrete actions reduce the confounding demands of low-level control. We equip three off-the-shelf agent harnesses originally built for software engineering with a monocular RGB camera and four discrete actions (fig. 1, middle). They receive no navigation-specific training, policy, map, search, or explicit memory. Unlike a policy, the model decides when to observe, which actions to issue, how to recover, and when to stop. Unlike a workflow, these decisions are not prescribed by 2task-specific control code. We call this organization agentic embodied control . Using non-embodied harnesses ensures that the surrounding interaction logic was not engineered for navigation. The answer is surprisingly strong. Under the strictly minimal interface, replicated default-effort runs average 70 .7 ± 3.5% SR with opus-5 and 68 .3 ± 1.5% with fable-5 , and fable-5 reaches 

78% SR at maximum effort. On the same model and harness, an optional hybrid interface averages 

76 .7 ± 0.6% and nearly matches that peak with half the environment steps and less than one quarter of the wall time. These results use the standard zero-shot R2R-CE test set [ 11 ] inherited from Open-Nav and since adopted by SmartWay, AgenticNav, and other zero-shot systems [ 12 , 13 , 14 ]. In context, the minimal result is competitive with engineered zero-shot workflows and recent industrial navigators trained on millions of samples, although the trained results use a different split [15, 5]. Where, then, does the capability reside? With the harness and interface held fixed, model choice changes SR dramatically. With the model held fixed, harness differences show no consistent direction and, given run-to-run variance and unmatched serving paths, are descriptive rather than causal. A forced waypoint interface rescues weaker models but can constrain stronger ones; exposing it as an optional tool instead lets the agent compose coarse and fine control. Together, these results point to a model-centered , but not model-only, organization: the model supplies most of the decision capability, the harness sustains interaction, and the interface shapes how that capability is expressed. The relevant question is therefore not simply how much scaffolding to add, but whether it is imposed or placed under agent control. Strong R2R-CE performance does not amount to general embodied competence. Success falls to 26– 39% on the longer-horizon RxR-CE benchmark, while latency and unbounded context growth make sustained operation impractical. Navigation therefore reveals both a transition and a gap. Agentic systems can already produce capable embodied decisions, but cannot yet sustain the open-ended, resource-bounded autonomy required of an Autonomous Embodied Agent. Our contributions are threefold: • We instantiate agentic embodied control in navigation: unmodified software-engineering harnesses, one monocular RGB view, and four discrete primitives reach 70 .7 ± 3.5% SR replicated and 78% at maximum effort on the standard zero-shot R2R-CE test set, without navigation-specific training, scaffolding, or workflows. • Through single-axis interventions we locate the capability: model choice spans 5–72 SR while harness effects stay within a few points, and a trained waypoint module that rescues weak models when imposed makes the strongest agent better, faster, and cheaper when offered as an optional tool. • We characterize what still separates this from sustained autonomy: success drops to 26–39 SR on longer horizons as context grows unboundedly, failures end in silent, confident claims of success, and deployment on a physical quadruped shows reasoning transfers while body awareness and spatial memory do not. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Policies and Workflows in Embodied AI 

VLN provides a controlled setting for this study. R2R introduced instruction- guided navigation on environment graphs [ 10 ], and R2R-CE extended it to continuous environments [ 11 ]. The trained line primarily develops navigation policies in which a model maps observations and history to an action inside an external environment loop. NaVid established a video-based VLM policy, followed by systems with streaming memory, larger models, and substantially more navigation data [ 1, 16 , 2, 6, 15 ]. Vision–language–action models follow the same policy-centered organization in manipulation [17, 18, 19, 20]. The zero-shot line takes the opposite route by freezing a general LLM or VLM and constructs navigation capability through a workflow. NavGPT and MapGPT initiated this direction with fixed 3prompting loops and external spatial representations [ 3 , 4]. Later systems add panoramas, depth, maps, and waypoints [ 12 , 13 ], structured planning, discussion, and search [21 , 22 ], or memory, backtracking, collision handling, and verification [ 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 ]. The model supplies semantic reasoning, but human-authored code fixes how these components are invoked. A newer trained line combines reasoning and action through hierarchical workflows. ETPNav connects learned waypoint prediction and a cross-modal policy to topological planning [ 27 ]. More recent industrial slow–fast systems couple a deliberative model to a trained action expert. ABot-N1, InternVLA-N1, and Vesta differ in their components and schedules, but all execute a fixed planner/subgoal-to-controller handoff [ 5, 6, 28 ]. Their hierarchy is dynamic in content, but fixed in control flow. These developments expose two orthogonal questions. Navigation capability may come from task-specific training or from frozen-model orchestration, while control may reside in a policy loop, a fixed workflow, or the model itself. We focus on the last case. Rather than adding another workflow [29 ], we remove most navigation-specific scaffolding and give the model authority over observation, action, recovery, and termination. 

2.2 Agentic Control and Embodied Interfaces 

ReAct [ 7] organizes an agent as a repeated observe–reason–act loop over interaction history. Coding agents show that capable models can sustain extended work through small sets of generic tools [ 8,9, 30 ]. Embodied agents provide a related precedent. Voyager combines an LLM, environment feedback, and executable tools with a task-specific curriculum, skill library, and verification loop [ 31 ]. We ask whether a generic harness can support embodied behavior without such task-specific support. Agentic control remains rare in embodied navigation. AgenticNav exposes actions, depth, and memory as tools selected by a frozen model [ 14 ], while the deployed Qwen-RobotNav system lets an upper-level planner repeatedly invoke a trained navigation policy and switch task modes [ 15 ]. These examples show that agentic control can orchestrate either zero-shot tools or trained policies. Our minimal setting isolates the former; the hybrid study then tests whether a trained module becomes more useful when placed under agent control. We call a system agentic when the model, rather than an external environment loop or human-authored control graph, directs the high-level interaction. It chooses when to gather information, which actions to take, whether to revise its approach, and when to terminate. This concerns control authority, not merely the presence of an LLM or VLM. The harness still maintains the session and executes tools, while the interface bounds what the model can observe and express. Agent–computer interface design shows that this boundary can substantially shape behavior [ 9 ]. Accordingly, we separate the model ,

harness , and interface , three factors often changed together in embodied systems. Our models and harnesses are general-purpose only in provenance because none was developed for navigation. Their importance here is to expose agentic control without hiding navigation logic in the surrounding system. 

3 Agentic Embodied Control through a Minimal Interface 

3.1 Model-Directed Interaction 

We replace the navigation scaffolding that zero-shot systems build around a frozen VLM with a general-purpose agent harness. Given the instruction and interaction history, the model decides when to observe, how to move, and when to stop. The harness delivers prompts, executes tool calls, returns results, and maintains the session, but does not prescribe the sequence of interaction. No navigation-specific module intervenes between model and environment. The agent receives no navigation training or in-domain demonstrations. Every episode starts in a fresh session, with no state or experience carried across episodes. There is no external navigation memory, map builder, state estimator, planner, search procedure, learned policy, or verification stage. The only history is that maintained by the native harness. 3.2 A Minimal Perception–Action Interface 

The embodied interface only contains two tools. observe() returns a single 512 ×512 front-facing RGB frame without advancing the environment. step(actions) executes an ordered sequence drawn from four Habitat primitives [ 32 ], consisting of FORWARD , LEFT , RIGHT , and STOP . Forward motion advances 0.25 m, each turn rotates 15 ◦, and STOP terminates the episode. A sequence is limited only by the remaining 500-step budget. The tool reports the number of executed primitives and remaining budget, but no image or collision signal. The model must call observe() to see an action’s effect. These tools and the natural-language instruction form the entire task-specific interface. The agent receives no pose, odometry, depth, panorama, map, waypoint candidates, collision feedback, or privileged simulator state. It must infer landmarks, progress, revisitation, action effects, and the stopping point from its monocular observation history. This deliberately diagnostic interface serves as the controlled baseline for the fixed and optional waypoint interfaces in sections 4.3 and 4.4.

Harness. We compare the open mini-swe-agent with vendor harnesses across six shared model identifiers (fig. 3B). Five pairs differ by 1.7–7.3 SR, while opus-5 reverses direction with a 1.7-point vendor advantage. Given the variance in section 4.1 and unmatched serving paths and product defaults, we treat the comparison as descriptive rather than causal. More importantly, none of these harnesses was designed to support the long-term deployment of an autonomous embodied agent. Our task-only configurations provide neither persistent cross-task state nor embodied recovery and safety mechanisms. Together with the context growth observed on longer runs (section 4.5), this motivates embodied-native harnesses for resource-bounded, sustained operation. 

Interface. We compare primitive control with a waypoint interface on R2R-CE and VLNVerse [ 38 ], holding the model, loop, and episodes fixed within each pair (table 4). The waypoint arm replaces primitives with a choice among at most five candidates proposed by a trained depth-based waypoint predictor [ 13 , 39 ]. Its effect depends sharply on primitive-control ability. On R2R-CE, the gain generally shrinks as primitive performance rises: 37–38 SR for the small Qwen models, 17–22 for qwen3.5-plus and the Codex GPT models, roughly 9 for sonnet-5 and opus-4.8 , and only 0.7–1.3 for the two strongest models. On VLNVerse, the direction reverses: SR falls by 4–6 points, SPL by 20–29 points, and collision rate rises four- to six-fold. The waypoint interface is therefore compensatory rather than uniformly beneficial: it supplies useful spatial priors when primitive grounding is weak, but can impose a mismatched action abstraction when direct control is already effective. This conclusion is specific to the tested waypoint predictor and does not imply that richer interfaces are generally harmful. 

4.4 Hybrid Interface: Better, Faster, Cheaper 

The interface ablation above forces the agent to use one action space throughout. We instead expose primitives and waypoints simultaneously, turning the trained waypoint module from a mandatory control path into an optional capability. 4.5 Long-Horizon Limitations 

The strong R2R-CE result remains a short-horizon result. As the horizon lengthens, a deployment barrier and a capability ceiling emerge together, both associated with a rapidly growing interaction context (table 6). 

Latency. The demonstrated capability is not real-time. Because the minimal loop re-sends its entire observation history on every call, context grows without bound within an episode: across the R2R-CE bare cells the final-turn context reaches a median of 33k tokens (maximum 169k), straining the request path on the longest runs. Across 4,700 logged simulator episodes, median wall time is 206 s, 18.6% exceed ten minutes, and 4.6% exceed twenty, totaling 467 hours. In the matched stress cells, moving from R2R-CE to RxR-CE doubles to triples the median final-turn context and at least doubles wall time. These measurements combine inference, deliberation, and harness overhead and do not isolate a causal component, but they expose the need for bounded context management. 

Horizon. Under the same model, harness, effort, and budgets, primitive SR falls from 75 on R2R-CE to 26 on RxR-CE [ 40 ]. Waypoints improve RxR-CE to 39 but remain 30 points below their R2R-CE result. It is also route-level, not stop-level : only 4 of 25 trajectories ever entered the 3 m success region, so the typical failure diverged early and never returned. The mechanisms are linguistic and spatial rather than perceptual. The largest category binds an ambiguous referent (“the sink”, “the first bed”) to the wrong instance in a scene full of duplicates, and stop-decision failures anchor on the named object rather than the annotated endpoint, though range estimates are usually accurate. The signature cognitive failure is self-diagnosis without self-correction :in at least 10 episodes the reasoning states the correct doubt verbatim and the agent commits to the wrong endpoint anyway. Geometry matters only where the interface hides it, since step() returns no collision or pose signal and a blocked forward still reports executed == requested , so wedges, mirror reflections, and reconstruction holes must be read from pixels alone, and in three episodes are not. 10 4.7 Deployment on a Physical Robot 

We deploy the same minimal agent on a real Unitree Go2 quadruped in an office building, with the same single monocular view and primitive step actions as in the simulator; the robot’s low-level control is wrapped behind this interface. In early trials the agent completed simple landmark-following instructions almost every time (the main exception is the body-clearance issue described below), so plain navigation tells us little. The 31 episodes across two sessions instead probe what a home robot would face—conditional reasoning, counting, multistage routes, fetching an object and returning, and handing an object to a specific person. Hardware gives no ground-truth pose, so each episode is read from its frames and the motion step reports. Section C gives the figures and analysis for each episode. Reasoning and perception carry over. The agent settles a condition—logical or perceptual, looking first when it must—before taking the branch it selects; tells people apart by a fine attribute, delivering to the person in white shoes rather than a bystander; holds state across multi-leg routes such as fetch-and-return; and notices from the reported motion when a turn falls short, correcting at the next look. Embodiment does not. The agent barely senses its own body: leaving a doorway, it turns before the body trailing half a meter behind the lens has cleared, and wedges. Actuation is coarse and step 

reports every request as executed, so a turn that falls short silently sends the rest of the instruction down the wrong heading. Most limiting, it keeps no persistent map, so tasks that integrate views over time break down—counting objects across revisits, picking among similar targets, judging distance. This is the embodied form of the long-horizon limit (section 4.5): the bottleneck moves off language and onto body awareness, calibrated motion, and a memory of space, which is where the next section is aimed. 

5 Discussion: A Bootstrapping Cycle toward Autonomous Embodied Agents 

Taken together, our results expose the gap between episode-scale agentic control and an autonomous embodied agent, while suggesting how the model, harness, and interface may jointly close it. 

Agentic as a compositional substrate. Policy, workflow, and agentic systems are not mutually exclusive forms. An agentic system can expose a trained policy as an action model or tool, and a human-authored workflow as a skill, while letting the agent decide when to invoke, combine, or stop them. The key change is not that policies and workflows disappear, but that they move from holding global control to becoming capabilities under the agent’s control. Agentic systems therefore provide an inclusive substrate on which policies, workflows, maps, planners, and other domain logic can be organized as callable and composable capabilities. Our hybrid-interface cell (section 4.4) shows this substrate in miniature: offered both action spaces, the agent decides per step when the trained waypoint module is worth invoking, and surpasses either fixed interface. 

Model, harness, and interface bootstrap one another. Our experiments identify a distinct devel-opment target in each layer: the model sweep and failure analysis expose limits in spatial reasoning and self-correction; the long-horizon results expose the need for persistent, resource-bounded state management; and the hybrid and real-robot studies show both the leverage and remaining limits of interfaces and action models that connect reasoning to physical control. Neighboring agent domains provide useful precedents: GUI agents have been trained from synthesized and iteratively refined interaction traces [ 41 , 42 ], while coding agents have learned from software evolution and rule-based execution feedback [ 43 ]. We hypothesize that embodied agents could follow a similar cycle: maps, waypoint modules, action models, and engineered harnesses help produce successful and verifiable trajectories; those trajectories support supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning with grounded or verifiable rewards, and distillation [ 44 , 7, 45 , 46 ]; and stronger models in turn change or simplify the scaffolds they need. We do not test this cycle here, and which capabilities should be internalized, retained in the harness, or delegated through tools remains open. 11 6 Conclusion 

We replaced the navigation scaffolding with a generic agent harness, one monocular RGB view, and four primitive actions, and found that this minimal-interface, zero-shot agent reaches 78% SR on the standard zero-shot R2R-CE test set, competitive in context with engineered zero-shot systems and the trained state of the art. Separating model, harness, and interface shows how much capability already comes from the general-purpose model, and how strongly its expression depends on the system around it. The most telling result is what happens when the designer stops choosing: a trained waypoint module that rescues weak models yet constrains strong ones as a forced control path makes the strongest agent better, faster, and cheaper as an optional tool. The same interface carries over to a physical robot; what remains open is deployment beyond the episode—persistent memory, bounded context, and recovery over hours of operation. Here each layer is a direction of progress, and we expect a bootstrapping cycle to emerge across them: scaffolds generating the experience that trains better models, and better models in turn simplifying the scaffolds they need.

B Case Study: All 25 Failures 

Of the 100 R2R-CE episodes in the analyzed run (claude-sdk × fable-5 , default effort, SR 75), 25 fail. We reconstruct every failure from its raw log in two passes. A first pass diagnoses each episode from the text stream alone (the instruction, the model’s summarized reasoning, the tool-call sequence, and the environment feedback) and assigns a primary failure mechanism. A second, independent pass then audits each diagnosis against the archived observations, frame by frame: 18 of the 25 diagnoses were confirmed, 7 were revised in mechanism (none in category), and none was refuted. Figures 4 to 7 reconstruct one representative episode per category in the format of fig. 2: archived observations with the step() call issued from each and the recorded reasoning verbatim, above the episode’s complete action tape. Table 11 groups the 25 failures into four mutually exclusive primary categories and table 12 lists every one of them. Three facts frame them. First, failure is silent : 23 of 25 episodes end with a voluntary STOP (22 of them under an explicit claim of success), and the model’s stated confidence is uncorrelated with its final distance to goal, which spans 3.1–44.6 m under near-identical “well within 3 meters” conclusions; the only two episodes that do not claim success are the two the budget stopped. Second, most failures are route-level, not stop-level : only 4 of 25 trajectories ever entered the 3 m success ball (oracle success), so the typical failure diverged at an early branch and never returned. Third, budget is bimodal : confident failures stop after 33–110 primitives with ≥80% of the budget unused, while search failures run to 233–500. A middle mode (an episode that keeps searching and 

converges) does not occur. 

A: Wrong referent / wrong branch (12/25). R2R instructions name object classes ( the sink, the 

lamp, the first bed, the leftmost door) that have several instances in Matterport houses and museums. The agent binds the referent to the first or most salient instance it sees, treats the match as proof of arrival, and stops. The binding error is often visually verifiable in the archive: the rival sink of ep2 is visible through a doorway the agent announced it would check and never did (fig. 4). ep48’s “two white sofas” never existed (one cream sofa plus an armchair pair). ep80’s disambiguating piano appears in no frame, so a console table was re-labelled to make the route fit. When the evidence contradicts the binding, the agent re-parses the instruction to fit its position rather than returning to the branch point: ep53 flags the contradicting portrait wall four times and stops 32.6 m out.

C: Runaway search (4/25). When the expected landmark fails to appear, the agent widens the search instead of re-examining its committed decisions, and the episode ends only when the budget forces an outcome. ep7 is the purest form: the route was executed correctly on the first attempt, but the terminal room contained a treadmill rather than a bed, so the agent rejected its own correct hypothesis and spent ∼460 primitives clearing the entire house. ep59 ends with the agent quoting the correct diagnosis (the master bedroom is “too far for a standard R2R path”) and stopping at a bedroom gym rack anyway. Dataset priors (“typical episodes are ∼10 m”) are stated in six episodes and acted on in none. 

D: Geometry and simulator traps (3/25). Route reasoning is correct but execution is defeated by scan geometry, amplified by a harness property: step() reports executed == requested whether or not the robot moved, and returns no pose, collision, or displacement signal. Blockage is therefore detectable only by comparing consecutive frames. The archive makes the cost measurable: ep6 spends 

∼70 primitives cycling among four camera poses at the true goal room before re-interpreting the goal noun elsewhere. ep98’s final 140 steps collapse to ∼6 distinct viewpoints, including bit-identical frame pairs 67 steps apart (fig. 7). Reconstruction pathology enters the reasoning chain unflagged: mirrors are parsed as doorways (ep63 chases a reflection of the goal bathroom), clipped meshes read as rooms behind walls (ep7), and two episodes stop on artifacts: ep53 facing an unrendered void, ep66 on a mesh hole misread as a red safety marker, an object that does not exist. 

Cross-cutting observations. (i) Self-diagnosis without self-correction is the signature cognitive failure: in at least 10 episodes the reasoning states the correct doubt verbatim: “that seems too long for a standard episode” (ep12), “I should have stopped right at the hall entrance” (ep54), “perhaps I was facing the wrong direction from the start” (ep80). The agent commits to the wrong endpoint anyway. The frame audit confirms several of the named alternatives were real and reachable. Error detection is present. Error recovery, in the sense of undoing a committed decision, is absent. (ii)

C Deployment on a Physical Robot 

We deploy the agent of section 4.7 on a physical Unitree Go2. Because hardware gives no ground truth pose and no automatic success label, we reconstruct each episode from its archived frames and its per step motion report, in the same format as the simulator case study of section B. Every figure below shows the instruction, a few frames with the model reasoning quoted verbatim and the step 

call issued from each, the outcome we judge from the final frames, and the complete action tape. The runs are qualitative and exploratory rather than a controlled benchmark, so we report behavior rather than a success rate. Read as one whole, they say the same thing the main text does. The reasoning survives the move to hardware and the body does not. 

Reasoning and perception carry over. The clearest positive is that instruction level reasoning transfers intact. Figure 8 hides a logic test inside a navigation goal. The agent turns around, resolves that the stated arithmetic is false, and drives to the yellow tape rather than the purple decoy, with the branch settled before it takes a step. The same behavior handles a perceptual premise, where the agent first looks to check a fact, such as the color of a robot against the wall, and then takes the branch that fact selects. Perception is equally robust. The agent names objects and even a specific fictional character from several meters away, reads its own reflection in a glass door, and tells targets apart by fine attributes. Figure 9 is the strongest case. Told to bring a remote to the person wearing white shoes, the agent separates that seated person from a standing bystander in gray from the first frame and stops in front of the correct one. It also keeps enough state for multi part routes. It can fetch an object and retrace its path to the start, and in fig. 10 it enters a named room and, at the narrow doorway, reads a body catch correctly as its own unseen rear, frees itself with small pivots, and stops inside. 

The body does not. The recovery in fig. 10 is not the norm. Figures 11 and 12 are a matched pair with the same start and the same instruction, and they differ only in when the agent turns. The agent reasons well about what the camera shows and rarely plans for the body the camera cannot see, so it does not leave itself room to turn. Coarse actuation compounds this. A commanded turn of about ninety degrees often lands near ten to thirty degrees on hardware, and because step still reports the request as executed, a short turn silently sends a landmark relative instruction down the wrong heading. In one elevator episode the turn that should have faced the goal executed as roughly ten degrees, and the entire second leg proceeded on the wrong bearing until we stopped the run. 

No memory of space. The deepest gap is the absence of a persistent map, so any task that needs views integrated over time breaks down. Figure 13 asks the agent to pass two pillars and then turn. It reads the corridor correctly but never fixes the count, relabels the second pillar from one frame to the next, and only turns at the far wall after a third pillar has gone by. Figure 14 shows the same weakness over a longer route. After the agent turns around and its frame of reference flips, its memory of a target it saw earlier decays, and it stops in front of a monitor cart that it reports as the robot arm it was sent to find. An open counting task in a room the agent has to survey fails the same way. It runs out its budget without ever committing to a number, because it keeps meeting tables it cannot tell apart. These are the embodied form of the long horizon limit of section 4.5. With no structure that records what has been seen and where, information does not accumulate across views.
