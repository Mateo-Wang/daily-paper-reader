# From Semantic Decisions to Feasible Trajectories: Self-Evolving LLM-Guided Optimal Control for Narrow-Space Parking

Abstract — Autonomous parking in nonconvex and narrow environments remains challenging. Although optimal-control methods can explicitly enforce vehicle dynamics and collision constraints, nonconvexity compromises solver robustness and can cause failures. Large language models (LLMs) exhibit strong semantic reasoning capabilities, but directly generating dense trajectories makes it difficult to guarantee physical fea-sibility. We introduce SE-LLM–OCP, a unified framework in which LLMs make high-level discrete maneuver decisions, while an optimal-control module enforces low-level vehicle dynamics and collision constraints. Online, the LLM proposes sparse maneuver plans, decomposing the parking task into a sequence of short-horizon trajectory-optimization problems. A low-level solver then sequentially solves optimal-control problems. If the solver fails, the LLM aggregates failure evidence from the solver and validation stages to guide replanning. Offline, SE-LLM–OCP automatically evolves a structured decision-making knowledge base from scratch, driven by accumulated online failures. We validate our proposed framework in simulation on a car-like vehicle model and on a differential-drive robot. Our experimental results show that SE-LLM–OCP enables safer autonomous parking in narrow scenarios and demonstrates transfer of the same maneuver representation to a different kinematic platform. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Autonomous parking in cluttered environments is a canon-ical hybrid motion-planning problem. A vehicle trajectory must satisfy nonholonomic kinematics and full-footprint collision constraints, yet planner success often depends on discrete maneuver choices: the approach direction, the or-dering of forward and reverse segments, and the placement of intermediate configurations. Search-based planners such as Hybrid A* (HA*) explore these choices with discrete nonholonomic motion primitives [1]. Optimization-based planners instead optimize continuous paths or trajectories under collision costs or constraints and, where modeled, vehicle kinematics or dynamics [2], [3]. The two paradigms fail in different ways. Search effort depends on the resolution and coverage of the motion primitives, whereas nonlinear trajectory optimization is sensitive to initialization and the selected homotopy class. These weaknesses become pro-nounced in nonconvex and narrow spaces: an unsuitable maneuver structure may lead to excessive search or local optimization failure even though a feasible trajectory exists. Large language models offer a complementary source of high-level structure, especially when paired with visual or structured scene representations. Language-grounded robotic systems have used foundation models to decompose in-structions into admissible actions, ground skill selection in affordances, synthesize executable policy programs, and plan over scene-level representations [4]–[7]. Autonomous-driving studies have likewise explored language-model rea-soning for motion planning and coordinate-based trajectory generation [8], [9]. These advances, however, do not remove the physical-grounding problem. A maneuver can be seman-tically plausible yet fail because of a small terminal-pose error, insufficient clearance, or an infeasible swept volume. Asking the model to generate dense waypoints or controls also assigns it a long sequence of metric decisions that are difficult to verify. The question, then, is how to give an LLM the maneuver-planning role in narrow-space parking while keeping physical feasibility with the solver: semantic decisions must be turned into physically feasible trajectories, and low-level failures must come back as guidance the LLM can act on. This paper answers this question with SE-LLM–OCP (Self-Evolving LLM-Guided Optimal Control Planning), a hierarchical framework for narrow-space autonomous park-ing. In SE-LLM–OCP, an LLM serves as the maneuver-level planner: from the scene, the task, and a parking-decision knowledge base, it decides how the vehicle should maneuver—where to stage, which direction to move, and how to approach the goal—and expresses these decisions as sparse terminal poses and forward or reverse motion modes, each accompanied by a short rationale. It never generates dense trajectories or low-level controls. A deter-ministic optimal-control layer then turns each decision into a short-horizon optimal control problem (OCP), enforcing nonholonomic vehicle kinematics, actuation limits, and full-footprint collision avoidance. Semantic reasoning and metric optimization are thus kept in strictly separate layers, each doing what it does best. Because LLM-proposed poses are approximate, the grounding stage does not demand that they be met exactly: each terminal pose is grounded into a small tolerance region adapted to the surrounding clearance, so minor endpoint errors are absorbed without weakening the kinematic or col-lision constraints. When optimization or validation nonethe-less rejects a maneuver, the failure is made actionable. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Traditional Planning Approaches 

Traditional parking planners primarily employ search-based methods and trajectory optimization. Reeds–Shepp curves provide shortest paths for a car with bounded curva-ture and forward–reverse motion in obstacle-free space [10]. Hybrid A* incorporates vehicle kinematics into heuristic search, retaining continuous vehicle states within a dis-cretized search space and refining the resulting path through numerical optimization [1]. These approaches provide geo-metric foundations and initial paths for parking trajectory generation. Optimization-based methods formulate parking as an op-timal control problem with explicit vehicle-motion and collision-avoidance constraints. Li et al. addressed time-optimal parallel parking through simultaneous dynamic opti-mization [11]. They later introduced safe travel corridors for parking, where a coarse path guides corridor construction and different parts of the vehicle are constrained within corresponding collision-free regions [12]. This representa-tion replaces large-scale collision-avoidance constraints with a reduced-scale OCP whose size is independent of envi-ronmental complexity. A subsequent lightweight iterative framework repeatedly reconstructs the corridor to recover free space discarded by a fixed corridor and improve tra-jectory optimization among irregularly placed obstacles [13]. Corridor-based formulations therefore make continuous opti-mization tractable after a coarse route has been supplied. SE-LLM–OCP addresses the complementary high-level prob-lem: an LLM proposes and revises sparse staging poses and motion modes, while the model-based OCP layer gen-erates continuous trajectories under vehicle-kinematic and collision-avoidance constraints. 

B. End-to-End Parking Approaches 

End-to-end approaches learn parking behavior from scene observations and target information. Yang et al. investigated end-to-end neural parking in the CARLA simulator [14]. ParkingE2E predicts parking waypoints from surround-view images and a target parking slot using imitation learning and an autoregressive transformer [15]. MultiPark extends learned parking to multimodal, multi-segment prediction, using parking queries that encode gear, longitudinal, and lateral behaviors together with goal- and collision-related training objectives [16]. These developments address ma-neuver diversity and long-horizon parking within learned planning architectures. In SE-LLM–OCP, the learned com-ponent instead proposes sparse pose anchors and motion modes. Continuous trajectories are generated by solving model-based OCPs, with vehicle constraints enforced during optimization and complete plans checked before execution. 

C. Grounding and Self-Evolution in LLM-Based Planning 

Grounding connects language-level reasoning to robot capabilities and physical environments. SayCan combines language-model scores with skill affordances to select ex-ecutable actions [5]. AutoTAMP translates language instruc-tions into formal task representations for task and motion planning, using corrective feedback to resolve translation errors [17]. VoxPoser constructs spatial value maps that guide model-based motion planning [18]. For narrow-space parking, the grounding interface must accommodate non-holonomic motion and vehicle-footprint collision constraints. Feedback and persistent memory further support the re-vision of LLM-generated decisions. Inner Monologue incor-porates environment feedback into subsequent planning [19], while Reflexion stores verbal reflections on task outcomes to guide later attempts without updating model parameters [20]. In autonomous driving, DiLu combines reasoning, reflection, and memory to retrieve relevant experiences and revise unsuccessful decisions, but its memory is seeded with man-ually outlined scenarios [21]. Building on these directions, SE-LLM–OCP couples online maneuver refinement with offline revision of parking knowledge through a common optimization and validation pipeline, evolving the knowledge base from an initially empty state without hand-authored entries. Online, solver diagnostics and trajectory-validation failures guide bounded replanning of pose anchors and motion modes. Offline, accumulated failures inform revisions to decision principles; a candidate knowledge update is retained only after the resulting planning process yields a trajectory that passes the same online acceptance checks. This links both decision revision and knowledge retention to the feasibility of the generated parking trajectories. III. METHODS Our framework SE-LLM–OCP comprises an online hi-erarchical pipeline (Fig. 1) and an offline knowledge self-evolution loop (Fig. 2). The online hierarchy allows LLM-based maneuver decisions to guide trajectory optimization while vehicle dynamics and collision constraints are enforced by the OCP solver. Online replanning revises the maneuver for the current task using a fixed knowledge-base version. Knowledge revision is performed separately: accumulated failures guide candidate knowledge-base updates. These up-dates are evaluated through the online pipeline before being used in subsequent planning tasks. 

A. Online Hierarchical Pipeline 

The online pipeline comprises high-level maneuver plan-ning, low-level trajectory optimization, and feedback and replanning. The LLM specifies pose anchors and motion modes that divide the parking task into segments. These decisions are converted into terminal conditions for se-quential OCPs, and the resulting trajectory is checked for feasibility before controller execution. Failed optimization or trajectory validation provides feedback for revising the maneuver within a prescribed attempt budget (three planning attempts per task). 

1) High-level Maneuver Planning: A parking scene is defined by a bounded workspace W ⊂ R2, the union of polygonal obstacle regions O, an initial pose p0 =(x0, y 0, θ 0), and a goal pose pg . The region W \ O is available for vehicle motion. Let F(p) denote the oriented rectangular vehicle footprint at pose p = (x, y, θ ). The collision-free configuration set is 

Cfree = {p | F (p) ⊆ W \ O} . (1) The task is to reach pg from p0 through configurations in 

Cfree while satisfying the vehicle dynamics and actuation limits. The decision-making LLM receives the task specification, scene image, metric geometry, start and goal poses, and vehi-cle dimensions. Relevant knowledge is retrieved by matching the current scene against the accumulated entries, each of which records a scene description together with explicitly allowed and forbidden operations. The retrieved entries guide the selection of staging poses and motion modes. The LLM generates an ordered maneuver plan with N segments: 

Π = {(pi, m i, e i)}Ni=1 , (2) where pi is the terminal-pose anchor of segment i, mi ∈{forward , reverse } specifies its direction of travel, and 

ei is a concise natural-language rationale explaining why the segment moves this way. Each rationale is carried through grounding and optimization; when a segment subsequently fails to solve or validate, its rationale is returned to the LLM together with the failure evidence, so that revisions can build on the original intent of the failed decision. Intermediate anchors specify staging poses for reorientation and goal approach, and the final anchor coincides with the goal, 

pN = pg . Vehicle parameters, objective weights, horizon lengths, and collision constraints remain system-defined. 

2) Low-level Trajectory Optimization: The high-level ma-neuver plan is converted into terminal constraints and penal-ties for sequential OCPs via adaptive anchor grounding. Re-quiring every intermediate anchor to be reached exactly can overconstrain these OCPs, even when nearby staging poses would permit feasible motion. Bounded endpoint adjustments are therefore allowed, with penalties favoring the nominal anchors. The final goal remains fixed. 3) Feedback and Replanning: The complete maneuver is checked after all segment OCPs have been solved. Validation assesses endpoint accuracy, continuity between segments, satisfaction of the terminal regions, and footprint feasibility using a sampled vehicle sweep. Trajectories that pass all feasibility checks are sent to the controller. When OCP optimization fails or the assembled trajectory fails validation, solver diagnostics and validation errors are passed to the feedback LLM together with the rationales of the failed segments. It summarizes potential causes at the maneuver level and provides guidance for replanning. The decision-making LLM uses this feedback, the task inputs, and retrieved knowledge to revise the pose anchors and motion modes. The revised plan undergoes the same grounding, optimization, and validation procedure, with the vehicle model and acceptance criteria held fixed. The online loop terminates when a feasible trajectory is found or the prescribed attempt budget is exhausted. This feedback revises the maneuver for the current task; the associated failure evidence is retained for offline knowledge revision. 

B. Offline Knowledge Self-Evolution Loop 

The offline loop in Fig. 2 uses accumulated planning fail-ures to revise the decision principles retrieved during high-level planning. The knowledge base is initialized empty— that is, zero-shot: every entry is generated automatically, with no parking maneuvers, staging heuristics, or exemplars authored by hand. Prompt structure and revision operations remain system-defined; only the knowledge entries evolve through the automated revise-and-verify cycle below. A fail-ure dossier combines observed failure patterns with relevant existing knowledge entries. The knowledge-revision LLM uses this dossier to propose a knowledge delta comprising one or two operations selected from addition, modification, deletion, and merging. Every generated entry must record the scene it applies to together with explicitly allowed and forbidden operations. Applying the delta to the retained knowledge base produces a candidate version for verification. Each candidate knowledge-base version is evaluated through the online pipeline in Fig. 1, with knowledge re-trieved from that version. The resulting maneuver plans undergo grounding, sequential OCP solution, and trajectory validation. Online feedback and replanning remain active within the prescribed attempt budget. The vehicle model, grounding rules, optimization settings, and acceptance crite-ria are held fixed during verification. A candidate is retained as the new knowledge-base version only when verification produces a feasible trajectory. If verification fails, the retained version remains unchanged and the new failure evidence is appended to the dossier to guide further revision. The online attempt budget limits maneuver attempts during each candidate evaluation; a separate offline revision budget limits candidate knowledge updates. The offline loop terminates when a candidate passes verification or the revision budget is exhausted. The loop thus grows the knowledge base from an empty initialization into a task-specific body of parking expertise, retaining only revisions that demonstrably preserve feasibility. IV. E XPERIMENTS

A. Experimental Setup 

Scenes and vehicle. The simulation benchmark comprises parking tasks in confined environments with nonconvex free space. Each task specifies an initial pose and a goal pose. The car-like model has a 4.50 × 1.85 m footprint, a 

2.70 m wheelbase, and a minimum rear-axle turning radius of 5.00 m. 

Controller and execution. All planners are evaluated on the same simulation tasks. Their reference trajectories are tracked using a Stanley lateral controller and a PID speed controller. Controller settings and the execution model are shared across planners, with acceleration and steering-rate limits enforced during execution. 

Evaluation metrics. Planning success requires the gen-erated reference trajectory to pass trajectory validation. For SE-LLM–OCP, this is the validation stage of the online pipeline. Closed-loop success requires successful planning followed by execution to within 0.50 m of the goal position and 10 ◦ of the goal heading, without obstacle collision or departure from the drivable region. Both success rates use all runs as the denominator. The collision rate is computed only over runs that pass planning validation and proceed to controller execution, including unsuccessful executions. A run is counted once if an obstacle collision or a boundary violation occurs. Planning failures are excluded from this denominator. Planning-quality metrics are averaged over successful planning runs. They comprise the number of gear shifts, mean absolute curvature |κ| (m−1), and maximum absolute spatial curvature gradient |dκ/ds | (m−2), where s denotes arc length. The curvature-gradient entry is the mean of the per-trajectory maxima. Terminal position and orientation errors measure the final pose error of the planned trajec-tory; they are distinct from errors after controller execution. Terminal errors are compared between SE-LLM–OCP and HA*+OCP and are not reported for HA*. 

Evaluation protocol and planning budget. For the plan-ner comparison and ablation studies, all configurations are evaluated under a consistent experimental protocol. Indepen-dent evaluation trials are distinguished from the planning at-tempts performed within each trial. All LLM components use 

gpt-5.6-sol . Unless replanning is ablated, SE-LLM– OCP performs bounded feedback-guided replanning when the current proposal fails validation. Each planning attempt undergoes grounding, optimization, and validation, while the knowledge-base version remains fixed throughout each online trial. 

B. Experiment 1: Planning and Closed-Loop Performance 

We compare SE-LLM–OCP with HA* [1], Direct OCP, and HA*+OCP. HA* searches for a kinematically admissible reference, which HA*+OCP then refines through trajectory optimization. Direct OCP optimizes the maneuver without LLM-generated intermediate anchors. In SE-LLM–OCP, pose anchors and motion modes specify the maneuver struc-ture before the segment OCPs are solved. Table I shows that SE-LLM–OCP achieves the best overall balance between planning feasibility and closed-loop execution. It attains 94 .7% planning success and 84 .2% 

closed-loop success, while also producing trajectories with fewer gear shifts and lower curvature-related metrics than HA* and HA*+OCP. Although HA* reaches a comparable planning success rate, its substantially lower closed-loop success indicates that a geometrically valid reference does not necessarily translate into an easily executable parking maneuver. Adding OCP refinement improves the execution performance of HA*, but HA*+OCP still remains clearly below SE-LLM–OCP, suggesting that continuous trajectory refinement alone cannot fully compensate for an unfavorable maneuver structure. Direct OCP produces no validated tra-jectory in the evaluated nonconvex scenes. Figure 3 explains where this advantage comes from. The key difference is that SE-LLM–OCP determines the maneuver structure before numerical optimization by jointly considering obstacle bypass, staging, and the required goal approach. This allows major heading changes to be com-pleted in regions with more available space and leaves the confined goal region mainly for final entry. In contrast, HA* determines the maneuver progressively through search, while HA*+OCP mainly refines the resulting reference without explicitly revisiting its earlier staging and bypass decisions. Consequently, SE-LLM–OCP supplies the segment OCPs with a more favorable maneuver structure rather than relying on optimization to recover from a poor high-level choice. The OCPs then enforce vehicle and collision constraints within this structure. This division of roles explains why SE-LLM–OCP outperforms the baselines not only in planning and closed-loop success, but also in gear-shift and curvature metrics.

We presented SE-LLM–OCP, a parking framework that bridges LLM-based maneuver reasoning and model-based trajectory optimization. The framework uses a knowledge base evolved from scratch to guide staging poses and motion modes, grounds approximate intermediate anchors through adaptive soft constraints, and converts solver and validation failures into feedback for bounded replanning. Simulation experiments in narrow, nonconvex parking scenes demonstrated the effectiveness of this decision–optimization interface, while a differential-drive robot with a 2D LiDAR further illustrated physical execution of the planned maneu-ver.
