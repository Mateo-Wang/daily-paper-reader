# AnchorVLN: Geometry-Anchored Vision-Language Grounding Reasoning for Open-Vocabulary Navigation 

Long Giang Vu *, Chengkai Yao *, Yuxin Liu, FNU Aryan, Rajath Chandrashekar Aralikatti 

giangvl.cs@gmail.com, chengkaiyao825@gmail.com, yuxin4500@gmail.com, aryanmangal2005@gmail.com, rajathcaralikatti@gmail.com 

Abstract —Vision-Language Navigation (VLN) in previously unseen indoor environments is useful in real-world robotics, where an agent must follow natural-language instructions, locate referred objects, and answer spatial questions without a pre-built map or a fixed object vocabulary. Recently, multimodal vision-language models (VLMs) have shown strong open-vocabulary grounding and zero-shot reasoning, making them a natural semantic front-end for such agents. One common problem is these VLMs’ inability to emit reliable metric quantities such as range, bearing, and comparative spatial relations directly from images. Existing approaches address this by folding geometry into a hand-engineered pipeline, or by asking the model to output waypoints; both require rewriting the control stack for each new robot, task, or vocabulary, which does not scale. This work designs AnchorVLN, an open-vocabulary VLN system built on a single rule ( the VLM proposes semantics; geometry decides metrics ), realised as E MBODIED -N AV -MCP, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server that a VLM agent drives at inference time through a compact set of callable tools. Because no tool accepts a distance in metres or a bearing in radians, the schema itself enforces the boundary between semantics and geometry without rewriting the downstream autonomy stack. We benchmark the server on both tasks of the CMU Vision-Language Navigation Challenge 2026: all 30 instruction-following questions over 15 scenes, and a frozen 45-question object-reference set on the same scenes. The full system achieves 64.4% on instruction following, dropping by 13.3 percentage points without controller modeling (t = 2 .77 , paired over questions). On object reference, geometric anchoring clears the challenge’s overlap threshold on 10 of 45 questions (versus 0 of 45 for direct model coordinate estimation), reducing median center error from 3.37 m to 2.48 m.

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Developing robots that can navigate previously unseen in-door environments is a longstanding problem in robotics. Early systems solved it geometrically: lidar odometry and visual SLAM [1], [2] together with closed-vocabulary detectors [3] built a metric map, and a hand-engineered planner queried that map for goals. The maps are consistent, but the object vocabulary is frozen before deployment, so free-form refer-ring expressions and compositional phrases are out of reach without extra supervision. A. Vision-Language Navigation 

Indoor navigation was first a geometric problem: lidar odometry and visual SLAM [1], [2], [16]–[18] with closed-vocabulary detectors [3] produce a metric map, and a planner queries it for goals. The stack this challenge ships is in that lineage—exploration, route planning, and waypoint following on a vehicle the policy does not own [13]–[15]. Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) [4] recast the same setting as following natural-language instructions on a discrete nav-graph; VLN-CE [5] lifted it to continuous control. Parallel object-goal lines study language-driven search without a full instruction, including zero-shot ObjectNav [19], [20]. Refer-ential 3D benchmarks then made the target an instance, not a class: VLA-3D [21] supplies the annotations this challenge uses, and IRef-VLA [22] studies iterative 3D referring. Clos-est to our platform, SysNav [10] is a three-level ObjectNav system from the same laboratory: it decouples semantic rea-soning, navigation planning and motion control, and reserves the VLM for room-level decisions because a model is too slow to sit in the control loop—a decoupling we adopt and say is theirs. We differ in that we do not own the controller, and we score ordered trajectories with object-level referring expressions rather than ObjectNav arrival.

B. Vision-Language Models for VLN 

Multimodal models changed who does the language. LM-Nav [23] parses landmarks with an LLM and grounds them with CLIP on a pre-built topological map; NavGPT [24] and NavGPT-2 [6] use an LLM as an explicit naviga-tional reasoner. Video VLMs then predicted the next ac-tion from a monocular stream [7], [25], and dual-system designs pair a slow VLM planner with a fast policy the authors train [9], [26]. On a real robot, Xu et al. [27] parse instructions with an LLM, build an online visual-language map, and hand waypoints to a learned local controller. AO-Planner [11] is the nearest neighbour to our metric rule: it refuses to ask the model for 3D coordinates and converts image evidence to metres with camera parameters and depth, but it grounds traversable ground in a space it owns. Open-vocabulary maps—VLMaps [8], ConceptFusion [28], Open-Scene [29], CLIP-Fields [30], NLMap [31]—fuse language features into a reconstruction that can be queried after the fact; we defer vocabulary to query time as a design choice, not a result. Xia et al. [32] argue from benchmarks that 3D perception accuracy saturates; we agree that recognition saturates, and treat ranging as a separate, confident failure. We keep the VLM on language-to-pixel grounding and take metres from lidar, on a controller we cannot change. 

C. Agentic Systems for Real-World Tasks 

Outside navigation, large models have been used as agents that call tools and skills rather than outputting motor com-mands. ReAct [33] interleaves reasoning traces with actions over APIs; Toolformer [34] shows that language models can learn when to invoke those APIs. On physical robots, SayCan [35] grounds an LLM in pretrained skill affordances on a mobile manipulator, Inner Monologue [36] closes the loop with language feedback from the scene, and PaLM-E [37] injects multimodal observations into the same planner. The Model Context Protocol [12] standardises that pattern as a client–server tool schema. We use that pattern; the difference is that every argument is a phrase or a handle, not a number. III. The VLM is called in three places: (1) as the MCP client, which reads the request in natural language and decides which tool to call next; (2) inside ground , the one tool that calls it directly, where it is given 2D images and an object name as the target; and (3) inside the perception process that maintains the object graph , one record per object the robot has seen, the records handles name. expand_vocab and

through rather than to , as between the TV and the bed is satisfied by crossing rather than by arriving. Working through them in order is what lets a late comparative , a phrase that picks its target out by its relation to another object, as in the chair closest to the TV , refer to an anchor that an earlier clause established. Below the interface the server speaks ROS to the platform: it reads the panoramic camera, the registered LiDAR scan, the terrain map and odometry, and writes a single Pose2D waypoint. ground consumes the camera and scan, drive_to writes the waypoint, arrival is read back off odometry rather than reported by the platform, and publish 

is the one tool whose output leaves this loop, so the box the system answers with is geometry the graph committed rather than a coordinate a model authored. 

B. Perception 

In this part we describe perception: the tools that tell the client what is in the scene, none of which moves the robot. 

ground is the load-bearing call. It unwraps the onboard panorama into a few overlapping gnomonic faces, which keeps resolution on small distant objects; the VLM returns a box per candidate; the box centre is unprojected to a bearing; and the 

median range of scan returns in a narrow cone about that bear-ing becomes depth, median so that foreground and background surfaces in one cone cannot average into a plausible wrong number. What comes back is handles, or a refusal. Refusal is a feature, because a confident wrong coordinate is driven to at speed. Three gates abort the lift, each a check between two measurements no model sees: an elevation floor rejects a bearing below the scanner’s lowest ray, where the cone samples floor; an implied-size gate rejects a box whose extent at the returned range contradicts the noun; an empty-cone test rejects a bearing with too few returns to cluster. A gated lift still yields a handle, marked unmeasured, and drive_to on it steps a bounded distance along the known bearing, often enough, since moving changes the geometry that caused the refusal. 

nearest , farthest and between resolve compara-tives. The answer is arithmetic over records the graph already holds, and it is a tool rather than a client judgement because the client is bad at it: asked which candidate is closest to an anchor, a model reliably returns the anchor . Because the operands come from the graph rather than the current image, a comparative ranges over every instance the robot has ever seen. between is the exception in what it returns, not an object but a passage: the accessible midpoint of two handles. 

expand_vocab and verify_present maintain the vo-cabulary the perception process searches for, and through it what the object graph comes to hold. That graph lives in the server: following S YS NAV [10], each LiDAR sweep and the objects recognised in it are folded into persistent records keyed by identity, so an object seen again from a new viewpoint updates one record rather than spawning a second, and its extent tightens as occluded faces come into view. It is where every handle resolves, and where object reference reads the 3D box it answers with. 

C. Motion 

In this part we describe motion: the tools that move the vehicle. drive_to takes a handle, and is where the policy stops owning actuation. Below it sits a controller with an objective of its own, assumed only to treat a waypoint as a request rather than a command and not to report arrival. A policy that can neither command nor read its controller must probe or predict, and probing costs a drive apiece, so the server predicts: it reimplements the controller’s objective against the same map, finds the point whose predicted resting place lies nearest the handle’s coordinate, and publishes that point instead of the one it wants. Porting to another platform replaces this model and nothing above it. Arrival is a distance test on odometry, because the judgement a model makes worst is calling a target reached from a viewpoint where it is merely visible. 

explore takes no handle and commits to no destination, so a client holding nothing can still change what the robot can see. When a phrase will not resolve several attempts running, the fixed goal is dropped and the robot drives toward the frontier of unseen space; fresh detections pull it back to reaching, and a coverage plateau ends a fruitless sweep. Where to go is decided by what the graph lacks, not by a plan the client wrote. orbit walks an arc of viewpoints around a handle already reached. A box measured from one approach is one-sided, far faces occluded, size under-read, orientation a guess, and since every sweep folds into the same record, the extent tightens as hidden faces rotate into view. explore 

grows the set of objects the graph knows; orbit completes the geometry of one it holds. IV. E XPERIMENTS 

A. Experimental Setup 

We benchmark on the CMU VLN Challenge 2026 de-velopment set, using two of the challenge’s task types to exercise different parts of the server. Instruction following (Section IV-B) drives all 30 questions and is scored on the trajectory; object reference (Section IV-C) answers 45 comparative referring questions and is scored on a pub-lished 3D box. The two share only the scenes. The rea-soning client is Claude Opus 5 ( claude-opus-5 ) with extended thinking disabled and decoding left at the provider default (temperature unset). At the frequency of 2Hz the platform exposes: 1920 px ×640 px equirectangular (360 ◦)RGB image, registered LiDAR, terrain maps, odometry. The scene graph is built by an open-vocabulary detec-tor cascaded with a segmenter and lifted against LiDAR, running at 2 Hz throughout the run. YOLOv8x-World v2 (yolov8x-worldv2.pt ) detects on a four-face gnomonic projection of the equirectangular panorama; SAM 2.1 Hiera-Large ( sam2.1_hiera_large.pt ) refines each detection to a mask; each mask is back-projected and lifted to a metric box from the registered LiDAR returns it covers, keeping a cluster only when at least 10 on-surface returns support it. A novel-viewpoint gate ( 0.3 m ) suppresses redundant re-detection from nearby poses. . The detector keeps a box above a confidence floor of 0.4, applies non-maximum suppression at IoU 0.5, and admits the mask only if SAM’s predicted mask-IoU clears 0.8. The detector starts each question from a fixed prior of 100 common indoor classes. The development set supplies 15 indoor scenes-single rooms, apartments, offices, a studio-each paired with two natural-language navigation instructions and, per instruction, a ground-truth trajectory. The robot starts from a fixed pose with no prior map. Every question has a wall-clock budget of 

10 min , of which the last 60 s are reserved for committing the answer, so navigation is cut off at 540 s . Each task is evaluated on its own instrument:

C. Object Reference 

End-to-end, the full loop hits 22% of the 45 referring questions (mean IoU 0.060, Table III), and on a hit the box centre lands a mean 36 cm from truth - modest coverage, but geometrically tight when it commits. Two ablations each remove one half of the rule “the VLM proposes semantics; geometry decides metrics” to isolate where that precision comes from. The tool schema is modest; the tools it wraps are not. The naive arm removes the MCP tool layer entirely: the model navigates and answers in free text, with no explore ,

expand_vocabulary or verify_present to shape the scene graph it draws its answer from. Removing the MCP structure ( naive ) barely moves the metric - 22% →

18%, mean IoU essentially unchanged - so the interface is not itself load-bearing. What it strips are explore ,

expand_vocabulary and verify_present , and their loss shows in kind rather than in score. Without explore 

the naive arm reasons over a starved graph - the full system’s is larger on 30 of 45 questions (mean 52.4 vs. 43.8 objects, Figure 2) - and without vocabulary priming it falls back to a coarser word ( nautilus shell sculpture → ammonite artwork ,Figure 3), so it picks the right label from the wrong set. When the model emits the answer’s (x, y, z ) itself ( model-metric ), bypassing the scene graph at answer time, not one of the 45 answers clears the threshold (0/45, mean IoU 0.000): the arm reports no extent - a default cube - leaving nothing for an overlap metric to credit. The failure is geometric, not semantic. Asked for “the bedroom light above the bed closest to the towel,” the grounded system reasons over coordinates and lands 0.23 m from truth, while this arm merely estimates “about 3.5 m in that direction” and misses by 6.0 m . Same model, same navigation, same scene - only the source of the final box differs.

We have both qualitative and quantitative results from run-ning the system with the challenge as a testbed, and together they suggest that this is a promising way to navigate without a prior map. Several limitations bound what we can claim from them. First, latency is high: a model call and the step it triggers cost roughly half a minute, and this cost is structural rather than incidental, since every tool the client invokes waits on a round trip to a frontier model; deployment outside a simulator would require a faster model in the ground role, a client that commits to several steps per call, or both. Second, every run uses a single frontier model, and the interface was designed against that model’s failure modes, so whether a handle-typed boundary matters more for a weaker model remains untested. Third, our evaluation covers one simulator, one robot, and no real hardware; the controller model inside drive_to is the component most tied to this deployment, and while porting replaces it and nothing above it, that is a claim we make rather than one we have run. A. What this design can resolve 

Two configurations were driven more than once, which makes the spread between repeats of an identical configuration measurable rather than assumed. The full system, re-driven, scores 63.5% and 62.2%; w/o Lift ’s two passes score 57.7% and 63.5%. The larger of those gaps is 5.8 points, from changing nothing at all. A single run’s per-question score has a standard deviation of 1.07/6 about that question’s own mean against 1.84/6 between questions, so roughly a quarter of the variance in a corpus mean is run-to-run noise rather than question difficulty. At 30 questions that puts the smallest resolvable difference between two single-pass arms near 9.4 points. Naive clears that floor and w/o Memory does not, which is what separates the two subsections below: the first reports a result, the second an observation. 

B. Removing both components Naive takes the range from the model and does not predict the controller. It is the configuration a team arrives at before reading the controller’s source, and it exists to test whether the two effects compound. They do not. Removing the lift costs 0.8 points and removing the platform model costs 13.3; an additive account predicts 50 .3 % and the arm scores 54.4%, above w/o Platform Model ’s 51.1%. That the arm with both faults outscores the arm with one is the observation; the gap between them, +3 .3 points at t = 0 .83 , is not resolved and we claim nothing from it. What is resolved is the arm itself: 10.0 points below the full system at t = 2 .38 , on 8 of the 9 questions where the two differ. It was not resolved on the 26 questions available before the last two scenes were unpacked ( 8.3 points at t = 1 .88 ), and the four added questions carried it over rather than changed it — the arm scores 54 .2 % on those four against 54 .5 % on the other 26. The conclusion it supports is the main text’s: what costs marks is not knowing where the waypoint will settle, and it costs them whether or not the range that reached the waypoint was measured. The mechanism the observation suggests is visible in the geometry and does not survive a test. Path length behaves exactly as error cancellation predicts: w/o Platform Model 

drives 0.79 × the reference length, and adding the model’s systematically long range brings that to 1.00 × (t = 2 .61 ,resolved). But the per-question score gain does not track the per-question shortfall ( r = 0 .27 , t = 1 .45 ), and splitting the corpus at the median shortfall gives the same gain in both halves. Correcting the distance is real; that the correction is what earns the marks is not established, and we do not claim it. In metres Naive is 0.27 m further from the referenced destination than the full system ( t = 2 .25 ), also resolved. This is the comparison Section ?? refers to: on the 26 questions driven first it was clear in metres while the score could not see it, and the score only caught up once the last two scenes were added. 

C. Removing the prompt’s memory 

The shipped prompt carries a block listing the poses the robot has already occupied, with an instruction to prefer somewhere it has not stood. Removing it is a prompt-only change: the drive loop’s own refusal to re-visit a spent bearing runs off separate state and is untouched. On the four quantities the block’s own wording predicts, removing it changes nothing measurable. Revisit rate is 27 % 

against 28 % , positional spread 2.64 m against 2.72 m , steps per question 8.9 against 8.8, and the share of steps from which the target is visible 94 % against 94 % , with paired t of −0.10 ,

−0.64 , 0.11 and 0.12 and every sign test near-tied. These are per-step measurements and carry roughly ten times the samples a per-question score does. The score moves the other way: 3.9 points in favour of removing the block, at t = 1 .13 . That gap is smaller than the 5.8-point spread we measure between two passes of an identical configuration (Appendix A), and the ablation perturbs fewer questions ( 6 of 30) than a plain re-drive does ( 8 to 9). Six questions differ at all: three are passage constraints flipping, one for the arm and two against; one is a 17 cm near-miss past the 1.25 m tolerance; one is both runs exhausting the step budget. The remaining one has a legible mechanism that does not reproduce on the other two, and we report it as an anecdote rather than a finding. The defensible statement is therefore narrow and negative: removing the block does not hurt. The organisers’ own ref-erence trajectories double back over 15 % of their length on average, and 21 of the 30 do it at all — returning within 0.5 m 

of a point they left at least a metre of travel earlier — so an instruction to prefer unvisited ground argues against what a correct execution does.
