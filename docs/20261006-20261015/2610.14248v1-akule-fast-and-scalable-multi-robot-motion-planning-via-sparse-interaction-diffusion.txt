Abstract
Diffusion models provide expressive trajectory priors for multi-
robot motion planning (MRMP), but scaling them to large teams
requires coordinating individual motions without expensive joint
computation. We introduce
Akule, a sparse interaction diffu-
sion framework that augments a frozen single-robot prior with
three learned modules: a reusable pairwise correction model 𝑅𝜙, a
contextual selector 𝑈𝜓that determines which corrections to evalu-
ate, and a composer 𝐺𝜔that combines their effects through context-
dependent signed weights. This decomposition separates which
interactions merit computation from how their effects should be
combined into coordinated denoising updates. We train 𝑅𝜙using
matched pairwise trajectory optimizations, then learn 𝐺𝜔and 𝑈𝜓
from physically optimized joint trajectories without requiring a
prescribed ground-truth interaction graph. The resulting joint pro-
posals are passed to a downstream search-based planner for valida-
tion and repair. Across diverse MRMP benchmarks, Akule achieves
competitive planning success, complete-planner speedups of up to
25×, zero-shot population transfer, and successful planning with up
to 256 robots. Ablations further demonstrate that learned selection
and composition reduce pre-repair conflicts and downstream repair
effort relative to heuristic selection and uniform summation. Project
website: https://akule-diff.github.io/

Introduction
Diffusion-based trajectory planners learn expressive motion priors
from offline data and generate goal-conditioned trajectories by
iteratively denoising entire motion sequences [4, 12]. Extending
these models to multi-robot motion planning (MRMP) introduces a
coordination challenge: trajectories that are individually plausible
may become infeasible when planned together. Avoiding inter-robot
collisions therefore requires accounting for interactions between
robots while generating jointly compatible motions [17, 28].
Existing diffusion-based MRMP methods introduce coordination
at different stages. Multi-robot Multi-model planning Diffusion
(MMD) retains single-robot priors and uses conflict-based search to
regenerate constrained trajectories [28]. Simultaneous MRMP Dif-
fusion (SMD) enforces inter-robot constraints during joint sampling
[17], while Discrete-Guided Diffusion (DGD) uses discrete multi-
robot paths to guide constrained generation [18]. These approaches
coordinate through constrained replanning, joint optimization, or
discrete guidance. Yet interaction relevance can differ across robot
pairs and change as planned trajectories evolve. This motivates our
question: can diffusion allocate expensive interaction reasoning to the
pairs that matter during denoising?
One way to achieve this is to retain a single-robot motion prior
and represent the influence of another robot as a reusable pairwise
interaction correction to its denoising prediction, drawing on rela-
tional and compositional modeling [3, 22]. For a team of 𝑁robots
however, evaluating such corrections densely requires considering
𝑁(𝑁−1) ordered interactions at every reverse step. Simply select-
ing fewer interactions is not sufficient either. Corrections that are
individually useful may become incompatible when several robots
are coordinated simultaneously. Our key insight is to decompose
interaction-aware diffusion into two distinct decisions: which pair-
wise corrections are worth computing, and how their effects should
be combined into a coherent joint update. Both decisions depend
on the surrounding team and the evolving trajectory estimates.
We introduce
Akule, which realizes this decomposition
through three learned modules. A shared pairwise model 𝑅𝜙learns
reusable interaction corrections; a contextual selector 𝑈𝜓uses inex-
pensive pairwise descriptors and pooled team information to choose
a directed interaction graph before evaluating 𝑅𝜙; and a composer
𝐺𝜔combines the selected corrections using context-dependent
signed weights. Shared parameters and symmetric aggregation
[37] make the architecture permutation equivariant: relabeling the
robots correspondingly relabels its outputs, allowing the same mod-
ules to operate across variable team sizes. We train these modules
through offline trajectory optimization. Matched pairwise optimiza-
tions supervise 𝑅𝜙, joint trajectory optimization provides composi-
tion targets for 𝐺𝜔, and sparse-support learning trains 𝑈𝜓without
prescribing a ground-truth interaction graph. Complete denoising
rollouts further refine the learned coordination. At inference, diffu-
sion produces a coordinated joint proposal that downstream search
validates and repairs as needed.
We evaluate Akule on obstacle-rich navigation and congested
position-exchange benchmarks [17, 18, 28]. Akule achieves compet-
itive or superior planning success with complete-planner speedups
of up to 25×, alongside zero-shot population transfer across team
sizes and successful planning with up to 256 robots. Ablations fur-
ther show that learned selection and composition reduce pre-repair
conflicts and downstream repair effort relative to budget-matched
heuristic selection and uniform summation.
Contributions. Our main contributions are: (i) A permutation-
equivariant diffusion architecture that coordinates variable-sized
robot teams by selectively evaluating reusable pairwise interaction

Related Work
Prior work addresses inter-robot coupling through classical search,
constrained or conditional diffusion, and compositional models
built from reusable factors. Akule instead learns reusable pairwise
corrections, which ones to evaluate, and how to combine their
effects in the context of the team.
Diffusion trajectory models — Learning-based motion planners
exploit learned sampling distributions and motion priors to acceler-
ate planning [11, 27]. Diffusion-based planners generate complete
trajectories through iterative denoising [1, 12], learning from ex-
pert demonstrations [5], trajectories produced by classical planners
or optimization [4, 21], or navigation data [36]. These approaches
provide expressive single-agent motion priors, but independently
generated trajectories do not account for inter-robot constraints.
Classical multi-robot planning — Inter-robot collision con-
straints couple individual plans, making joint-space search
expensive as team size grows. Decoupled and prioritized planners
reduce computation but can fail on solvable instances [7, 30, 32].
Subdimensional expansion couples robots only when interactions
require it [34], while conflict-based search (CBS) resolves con-
flicts through constrained replanning [29]. Both exploit selective
coupling, but search can remain expensive in heavily interacting
scenes. Bounded-suboptimal CBS variants accelerate search while
preserving cost bounds [2, 16]. Large-neighborhood repair [15],
priority inheritance [26], and lazy constraint addition [25] offer
other scalable strategies with differing guarantees. Discrete plan-
ning followed by continuous refinement [10] and reactive collision
avoidance [33] provide further alternatives. These methods exploit
interaction structure during planning or execution, rather than
learning reusable denoising-time corrections.
Multi-agent diffusion planning — Offline multi-agent diffusion
models learn coordination through cross-agent attention [40] or
sparse coordination graphs [38], but address different control objec-
tives from collision-constrained MRMP. For MRMP, MMD retains
single-robot diffusion priors but resolves coupling through conflict-
driven search and constrained regeneration [28]. SMD enforces
joint constraints through optimization during sampling [17], while
DGD uses discrete MAPF guidance to organize constrained genera-
tion and repair [18]. Other approaches use decentralized neighbor
prediction [19], local conditional denoising [13], or model-based
diffusion with conflict-based search [8]. These methods account
for interactions through search, joint optimization, or conditioning
on other agents’ motions. Akule instead makes the evaluation and
composition of reusable pairwise denoising corrections separate
learned operations.
Compositional diffusion and sparse interactions — Compo-
sitional diffusion constructs complex distributions from reusable
scores or energy-based factors [6, 20]. Graph-structured approaches
organize local models through prescribed graphical relationships
[35, 39]. In planning, related work composes skills and task factors
[22, 23], locally valid trajectory predictions and reusable score fac-
tors [24, 31], or obstacle and map-region potentials [21, 28]. These
approaches demonstrate the value of reusable components, but
their factorization or composition rule is often prescribed by task
structure. Sparse interaction relationships can also be learned for
multi-agent coordination [38] and humanoid control [14], although
these address different planning objectives. Akule instead learns
a time-varying directed support and a context-dependent signed
composition of pairwise corrections to a frozen motion prior.

4.4

than requiring binary labels specifying which interactions are cor-

equally valid behavior, making a single hand-designed binary graph

primitive 𝑅𝜙, then use offline trajectory optimization to generate
high-quality composition targets for 𝐺𝜔, and finally learn which
interactions can be removed through 𝑈𝜓.

The final interaction-aware proposal ˆ𝝉is passed to the downstream

5
Experiments
We ask whether sparse interaction selection preserves planning
quality, whether one coordination model transfers across team sizes,
whether the learned proposal reduces complete-planner computa-
tion, and whether these properties hold across map families and
very large teams.
Benchmarks. Canonical Weave [28] is a high-congestion position-
exchange task (𝑁∈{10, 20, 28}); Basic, Dense, Shelf, and Room
[17] are obstacle maps of increasing clutter (𝑁∈{3, . . . , 18}, 25 in-
stances per cell); Highways and Conveyor [28] are the MMD obstacle
environments (𝑁∈{3, . . . , 20}, 10 tasks per 𝑁); and ScaledWeave
scales the Weave geometry to very large teams (𝑁∈{40, . . . , 256},
10 scenes per 𝑁). In every family, Akule is trained or adapted only
at the largest population (𝑁= 28, 18, 20, and 256, respectively) and
evaluated zero-shot at all smaller populations.
Baselines. MPD [4] applies the same frozen single-robot prior
independently and relies entirely on downstream repair, isolating
the effect of coordination; MMD [28] couples single-robot diffusion
with conflict-based search (we use its strongest variant, xECBS,
rerun on our panels); SMD [17] and DGD [18] enforce inter-robot
constraints during joint sampling and via discrete guidance, re-
spectively. On the map families, baseline values are the published
DGD-benchmark results and are not same-hardware timings.
Protocol and metrics. Akule and MPD share the same hard-repair
planner H (MMD’s obstacle-aware xECBS) and differ only in the
proposal. We report success (validated collision-free plans within
the time limit), planning latency (wall-clock time including repair,
averaged over all runs with timeouts counted at the limit), and
quality (mean path length and acceleration over successful plans).
Full benchmark, timing, and hardware details are in Appendices C
and F.

(KF#3) The learned proposal hands repair far fewer conflicts,
and that is where the speed-up comes from. Fig. 4c counts sampled
collisions in the unary prior’s joint proposal before and after Akule’s
corrections and before any smoothing or repair, on matched scenes
and noise. Across all populations and all seven environments, every
condition remains well below the diagonal by roughly the same
fraction. Conflict-based repair pays for each remaining conflict
with a constraint tree branch and low-level replans, so with an
identical backend, this explains the speed-ups over MPD and MMD
(Tables 1, 3); in the ablation (Table 5), variants leaving more conflicts
take proportionally longer. The reduction is smallest where the
prior already yields few conflicts (Weave, 𝑁= 10) and on Conveyor,
whose narrow lanes leave corrections little room.
(KF#4) The same formulation covers different map families and
scales to hundreds of robots. The learned modules are shared across
robots and conditioned on each robot’s own planning context, so a
new map changes the unary prior; they correct not the coordination
mechanism, carrying from free-space exchanges to cluttered maps,
shelves, rooms, and the MMD environments (Tables 2 and 3). On
ScaledWeave (Fig. 5), from 40 to several hundred robots, planning
time grows more slowly than the team and per-robot time stays
roughly constant, while the search-based baselines hit the time limit
from the second population onward. Pair scoring, the quadratic
part, is cheap; the expensive evaluation and composition scale with
the selected degree, and the discarded share keeps rising with 𝑁
(Fig. 4a). Conflict-based search faces a conflict count that grows
faster than 𝑁and lacks such a lever.

Conclusion and Limitations
We introduced
Akule, which augments a frozen single-robot
diffusion prior with reusable pairwise corrections, learned sparse
interaction selection, and context-aware signed composition for
MRMP. Trained from physically optimized trajectories, these mod-
ules coordinate variable-sized robot teams without prescribed inter-
action graphs. Across diverse MRMP benchmarks, Akule achieves
competitive planning success with 25× computational savings, zero-
shot population transfer, and successful planning with up to 256
robots. Ablations demonstrate the benefits of learned selection and
composition in reducing the coordination burden before hard repair.
Limitations and Future Work. Although sparse selection avoids
expensive pairwise correction evaluations, the selector still scores
𝑂(𝑁2) candidate interactions. Hierarchical candidate screening
could reduce this overhead while preserving context-dependent
selection. Moreover, composed updates remain within the span of
learned pairwise corrections, potentially limiting coordination that
requires more complex higher-order interactions. Extending the
correction basis to capture such interactions is a promising direc-
tion. Finally, learned proposals do not guarantee feasibility, and
8


downstream repair can dominate computation in highly congested
settings. Incorporating repair directly in learning, alongside adap-
tive sparsity, could improve the trade-off between computational
efficiency and planning success.

This supplement specifies the implementation behind the sparse
interaction model, its training dependencies, and the boundaries of
the reported measurements. We retain the main paper’s notation:

A.1
State representation and frozen unary prior
The grouped trajectory tensor is 𝐵× 64 × 𝑁× 4. Its physical chan-
nels are two positions and two stored displacements. They are not
interchangeable with velocities in metres per second. The canonical
codec is an unclipped affine transformation, 𝑥phys = 𝑠⊙𝑥norm + 𝑏,
with 𝑠and 𝑏fixed by the pretrained normalizer. Highways and
Conveyor use a velocity bridge: the native prior’s velocity channels
are multiplied by Δ𝑡= 5/64 to obtain displacement channels and di-
vided by Δ𝑡when encoded again. The conditioning tensor contains
the start and goal four-channel states, hence eight endpoint values
per robot. The start and goal positions and prescribed endpoint
motion channels are reinstated after the reverse updates.
The canonical unary prior is the released MPD temporal U-
Net [4, 28]. It maps 64 × 4 noisy single-robot states to equally
sized noise predictions. The base width is 32 and the channel mul-
tipliers are (1, 2, 4, 8), giving widths (32, 64, 128, 256). Each down
stage has two residual temporal blocks, followed by downsampling
except at the deepest stage; the bottleneck has two residual blocks,
and three up stages concatenate encoder skip features. A residual
block contains two kernel-5 convolutions with GroupNorm and
Mish, an additive time projection, and a channel-matching residual
connection. Downsampling uses a kernel-3 stride-2 convolution;
upsampling uses a kernel-4 stride-2 transposed convolution. The
output applies a kernel-5 block and a 1×1 convolution to four chan-
nels. The 32-dimensional time encoder uses a sinusoidal embedding
and an MLP. The frozen configuration uses epsilon prediction and
the 25-step exponential variance schedule. Highways and Conveyor
load their own released unary checkpoints and normalizers with
the same four-level width configuration.
For SMD maps, the frozen unary model adds goal-conditioned
spatial features to the temporal backbone. A 64 × 64 raster con-
tains occupancy, clipped signed distance, and start/goal Gaussian
heatmaps with standard deviation 0.07 m. Five spatial levels have
widths (16, 24, 32, 48, 64), two kernel-3 convolutions with SiLU per
level, and stride-2 convolutions between levels. Bilinear samples
from all five levels are concatenated and projected from 184 chan-
nels to 32 local features. Mean and maximum pooling of the deepest
level produce 128 features projected to a 32-dimensional global to-
ken. Four exact local geometry features augment the local vector to
36 channels: scaled support clearance, its two-dimensional unit gra-
dient, and scaled clearance at the following segment midpoint (the
final support reuses its own clearance). Learned local and global
projections inject this context into each temporal residual block.
These parameters are adapted before coordination training and
remain frozen during 𝑅/𝐺/𝑈training and evaluation.

A.3

A.5
Equivariance and computational boundary
The specifications in Table 6 are independent of population. No
learned module uses a robot’s array index as a feature. Shared pair
encoders, masked set attention, symmetric reductions, and scatter-
add composition commute with a simultaneous permutation of
robots and their endpoints. The resulting trajectory update and di-
rected mask therefore permute with the input. This is architectural
permutation equivariance, up to floating-point reduction order. It
does not imply rotation invariance, and the physical axes remain
meaningful.
The selector still scores 𝑁(𝑁−1) candidates (the vectorized en-
coder also forms masked diagonal tokens). Sparse admission saves
evaluations of 𝑅𝜙and the admitted-edge composer; it does not make
candidate scoring subquadratic. If no edge is admitted, composi-
tion returns a zero correction. The graph is recomputed at every
interaction-aware reverse step, without a monotonic sparsification
constraint.
A.6
Complete planner and repair interface
For Weave and SMD the complete proposal algorithm is: (1) sample
the saved initial noise and condition endpoints; (2) for 𝑡= 24, . . . , 0,
predict unary clean states, encode selector descriptors, admit di-
rected edges, evaluate centered pair corrections on those edges,
compose signed corrections, and apply the conditioned posterior
with the prescribed noise; (3) decode the final proposal; (4) perform
the benchmark’s static cleanup and hard repair; and (5) validate the
returned complete trajectory. The posterior noise is zero at 𝑡= 0.
For Highways and Conveyor, step (2) is replaced by independent
candidate generation and selection followed by a single 𝑡= 0 interac-
tion correction. Section C specifies this distinction. Akule supplies a
root trajectory to downstream conflict-based search [28]. The repair
backend detects remaining conflicts, branches on constraints, and
regenerates affected robot trajectories. SMD additionally uses tar-
geted static repair with the native geometry and endpoint adapter;
low-level RRT fallbacks are part of that implementation. Successful
proposal generation alone is not complete-planner success, and the
learned model provides no collision-free guarantee before repair.

The canonical teacher corpus contains 25,000 qualified geometries

coefficients for each directed frozen pair field. This is the concrete
implementation of the main paper’s trajectory-space composition

C.1
Scenes, geometry, and physical
representation
Canonical Weave. Robots exchange opposite sides of the fixed
obstacle-free square [−1, 1]2 m. The construction starts on a per-
turbed angular ring and snaps a dominant coordinate to the square-
side boundary; the goal reflects that coordinate. Robot radius is 0.05
m, and the frozen center bound is 0.95 m. The coordination model
is trained at 𝑁= 28 and transferred to 𝑁= 10, 20. Weave’s fixed
construction is retained rather than enlarged for higher populations.
The 𝑁= 28 panel is the predeclared first 512 executed conditions of
a 1,024-condition frozen manifest. The smaller-population panels
contain 512 and 256 scenes, respectively. Learned/unary compar-
isons share deterministic noise; the native MMD path uses the
recorded compatible seed convention.
Highways and Conveyor. These use the released MMD obstacle
geometry, disk radius 0.05 m, and workspace [−1, 1]2 m. Obstacles
are rounded boxes with the exact saved centers, full sizes, and
corner radii, not rectangular approximations inferred from images.
Each of the six populations has ten frozen start/goal tasks per map.
Adaptation is performed at 𝑁= 20 where applicable; the evaluation
does not fit a separate coordination network at each smaller 𝑁. The
environment-specific single-robot prior produces 64 independent
candidates per robot. Its frozen runtime uses 64 supports, 20 guide
steps with guidance starting at reverse index 13, one final noise-
free step, and three noising/three denoising steps for constrained
local inference. The supporting runtime configuration includes
the exact four-channel normalizer limits. Akule batches candidate
generation across robots and selects candidates before applying one
terminal interaction correction. The reference MPD/MMD paths
may generate robot candidates sequentially. In particular, MMD’s
roots already incorporate soft inter-robot guidance; they are not
independent unary trajectories.
Consequently, the map experiments are not 25-step interaction
rollouts. Their priors use the diffusion schedule during candidate
generation, but 𝑈/𝑅/𝐺operate once on the selected candidates
at 𝑡= 0. Candidate batching and guidance change sampler cost
independently of interaction selection. Comparisons of complete
planning time evaluate these implemented pipelines, while the
matched unary-intervention study isolates changes to the same
selected candidates.
SMD maps. Basic, Dense, Shelf, and Room use the released in-
stance obstacle circles [17]. Shelf and Room partitions are repre-
sented by the actual circle unions, not substituted rectangles. A
shared map-conditioned unary and the frozen 𝑅/𝐺/𝑈stack are
trained/adapted using 𝑁= 18 contexts and transferred across the
six test populations. Each final cell contains 25 held-out instances
with radius 0.05 m. Training also includes Empty, but the seven-

families. The figure data include exact layout and scene identities.
SMD representative baseline reruns are kept distinct from any pub-

25-scene estimate.

MMD’s soft-guided roots contain fewer residual conflicts than
Akule’s corrected map proposals: 13.7 versus 85.7 on that condi-
tion. At Highways 𝑁= 3, 6, all ten MMD roots are conflict-free.
These results are consistent with MMD spending computation on
sequential soft coordination before hard search.
Conveyor 𝑁= 20 has only six complete MMD roots. The other
four attempts return incomplete teams and cannot supply full-
team root conflict counts. Their planning failures and recorded
computation remain part of complete-planner accounting, while
root comparisons explicitly restrict to available complete roots. We
do not impute missing agents, zero conflicts, or ten complete MMD
proposals. The independent unary candidates used in Table 9 are all
available and are a different baseline from these soft-guided roots.

Ablation Details
The final ablation panel uses Canonical Weave 𝑁= 28 (30 instances)
and Shelf 𝑁= 18 (25 instances). The variants are inference-time
substitutions with frozen unary and pairwise models, the same diffu-
sion schedule, and the same downstream repair interface. They are
not separately retrained neural architectures. Table 11 reproduces
the final manuscript’s ablation results, including success, pre-repair
conflicts, repair and complete latency, successful-trajectory quality,
and directed-interaction savings.
Composition substitutions. Full Akule uses learned hard selec-
tion and signed contextual composition. Dense Akule opens every
non-self directed edge while retaining the learned composer. Raw
summation retains the learned selector and gives every admitted
primitive coefficient one. Calibrated summation retains the selector
and uses a single constant from {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.5, 2}, selected
on disjoint validation scenes by complete proposal rollouts.
Weave validation lexicographically minimizes mean pre-repair
pair conflicts, then acceleration and path length. The first criterion
is smallest at 𝛼= 1 (94.6875 validation pair-times), narrowly ahead
of 𝛼= 0.75 (95.21875), so calibrated and raw summation are the
same intervention and share measurements. Shelf first minimizes
obstacle-invalid robots, then pair conflicts, acceleration, and path
length. The 𝛼= 0 validation candidates have zero obstacle-invalid
robots, whereas every positive grid coefficient has a positive mean.
The rule therefore selects 𝛼= 0 even though its pair-conflict mean
is 165.5769. This is a physical-feasibility-first validation decision,
not a choice minimizing complete-planner latency. A zero coeffi-
cient removes the composed correction but does not imply that
an unoptimized implementation skipped computing its admitted
residuals.

Table 12 distinguishes negative spline coefficients from negative
evaluated temporal weights. These differ because a support’s weight
interpolates several controls. On Weave 𝑁= 28, 42.96% of admitted
coefficients and 44.46% of evaluated weights are negative. The corre-

interface also uses negative values. Such statistics establish that the
trained model uses sign reversal; they do not establish that sign
freedom is necessary. That causal claim would require a controlled
retrained nonnegative-composer comparison, which is not supplied
by an inference-time absolute-value or clipping intervention.

and checks proposal agreement rather than selecting replays for
favorable timing. The Weave component intervals include its

and guidance, while interaction is terminal. Host overhead and
downstream search are outside these intervals.

timers to synthesize a new complete latency.

exact proportionality.

should remain visible alongside Akule’s batching and proposal-

than inventing a comparable search input. Reduction against an
independent unary proposal, sampler cost, and downstream search

H.1
Safety-preserving smoothing
Saved-path smoothing can improve the regularity of a completed

H.2
Alternative downstream planners
Akule’s proposal interface can be combined with different CBS-
family backends. We evaluate this interface on a fixed small panel
of 20 canonical Weave 𝑁= 28 tasks, using the first 20 frozen
final-test conditions and unchanged pretrained models. Table 16
distinguishes newly executed ECBS measurements from existing
CBS/xECBS records on those condition indices. Each attempt has
a 240 s native planning limit and a 255 s external watchdog. We
do not train or tune the coordination model for the substituted
backend. Success requires the same complete-team, endpoint, and
sampled physical collision checks as the canonical benchmark.
The comparison tests compatibility and sensitivity to the repair
backend. It is a small-panel study, not a replacement for the full
Weave benchmark. Historical and newly collected timers retain
their own execution provenance; the table does not establish a
matched-hardware latency speedup between cohorts. Mean latency
includes all 20 attempts and assigns 240 s to timeouts. Successful-
run quality is reported only where a valid result exists.
Plain CBS times out on all 20 tasks for both proposal interfaces.
Newly executed Akule–ECBS solves 20/20, with mean all-attempt
latency 77.45 s and 28.75 CT expansions; the existing Akule–xECBS
subset also solves 20/20. These outcomes show that useful learned
proposals do not remove dependence on the repair backend. Akule’s
mean physical root conflict count is 31.60 in each backend panel,
whereas the native baseline’s root construction depends on its
planner configuration.
H.3
DropRegion and reproducibility
The available released DropRegion artifacts include trained net-
work weights and model arguments, but the environment-specific
trajectory dataset required by the official inference loader is absent.
The loader reads dataset metadata and successful trajectory tensors
to construct a four-channel limits normalizer. The saved network
and diffusion-schedule tensors do not recover these data-dependent
minima and maxima. Thus the available checkpoint alone does not
establish the original mapping between physical position/velocity
channels and normalized diffusion inputs and outputs.
A reproducible matched evaluation requires the original infer-
ence dataset or the corresponding normalization statistics and meta-
data. Substituting another map’s limits or estimating new limits
would change the pretrained model’s inference protocol. We there-
fore omit DropRegion. This is an unavailable normalization depen-
dency, not evidence of infeasibility or of Akule’s performance on
that environment.
H.4
Method limitations
Candidate scoring. The selector evaluates directed candidates
before hard admission. Its pointwise descriptors and contextual
processing therefore remain quadratic in robot count, even when
expensive pairwise corrections are evaluated sparsely. Savings in
𝑅𝜙evaluations do not imply a linear-time complete sampler or
planner.
Restricted correction span. The composer combines frozen pair-
wise correction directions through a finite temporal basis. It cannot
express every joint multi-robot update, and signed coefficients do
not remove this representational restriction. Our observed sign
statistics describe the learned solution; a retrained nonnegative
model would be needed to assess the necessity of signed weights.
Offline optimization and transfer. Training depends on optimized,
qualified pair and composition targets. Their cost and acceptance
criteria affect the available supervision, and several supports can
meet the same physical objectives. There is no uniquely correct
interaction graph. Transfer to different populations and maps de-
pends on the prior, normalization, geometry, and training contexts;
the evaluated grids do not establish arbitrary out-of-distribution
performance.
Repair and validation. Interaction-aware proposals can retain
robot–robot or robot–obstacle conflicts and still require static
cleanup and hard search. The map measurements show that a
faster batched proposal need not reduce subsequent search effort
relative to a soft-guided MMD root. Complete planning also
remains sensitive to backend choice and time budgets. Finally,
sampled success and ideal-tracking trajectory quality do not certify
continuous-time safety, dynamic feasibility under unmodeled robot
physics, or execution robustness.
