Abstract
Occupancy world models provide a structured, metric repre-
sentation of future driving scenes, making them a promising
basis for end-to-end autonomous driving. However, exist-
ing occupancy-aware planners typically couple occupancy
prediction and action generation through either hierarchical
coupling or parallel multi-task learning. Hierarchical methods
use occupancy either as a fully predicted future preceding
action generation or as an action-conditioned rollout for trajec-
tory evaluation, whereas parallel multi-task methods predict
occupancy alongside planning through a separate branch or
auxiliary objective. These interfaces retain either an explicit
generation order or a role asymmetry, limiting reciprocal inter-
action between world prediction and action generation during
trajectory formation. We propose OccWAM, an occupancy-
native world–action model that jointly generates dense future
occupancy and continuous ego trajectories via flow matching
conditioned on scene representations. Building on this joint
formulation, we introduce scene-to-ego world–action flow
matching, a two-stage formulation. The first stage predicts
scene-centric occupancy together with an absolute ego trajec-
tory, providing a stable representation of future scene evolu-
tion. The same predicted world is then re-indexed into the
future ego frames induced by the initial trajectory, where ego
motion is represented through adjacent pose increments and a
second joint flow refines ego-centric occupancy and motion
for action-aligned planning. Unlike video-based world–action
models, OccWAM preserves explicit semantic geometry for
direct planning reasoning without pixel synthesis. Extensive
experiments on Bench2Drive and nuScenes demonstrate state-
of-the-art performance in closed-loop driving, open-loop plan-
ning, and future occupancy prediction.

Introduction
End-to-end autonomous driving has progressed from direct
waypoint regression toward planning-oriented structured sys-
tems, multimodal generative planners, and vision–language–
action models [1–5]. These advances have substantially im-
proved trajectory generation, task integration, and semantic
reasoning. Yet a fundamental question remains unresolved:
how should an explicitly modeled future participate in action
generation?
A growing line of world-model-based policies and world–
action models (WAMs) incorporates predictive futures into
policy computation. Existing systems range from latent fea-
ture alignment and chained world-to-policy architectures to
joint video–action generation [6–9]. These systems show that
predicted futures benefit action generation but leave open a
representation question: what form should the predicted future
take to provide structured and directly usable information for
planning?

Existing occupancy-based driving systems instantiate sev-
eral recurring world–action interfaces. In the action-to-world
formulation, candidate actions or intentions are generated,
then their future worlds rolled out and evaluated [10, 11]. In
the world-to-action formulation, a predictive occupancy repre-
sentation is constructed before being passed to a downstream
planner [12, 13]. Others reduce the sequential dependency via
parallel multi-task decoding or ordered discrete world–action
token sequences [14–16]. These formulations retain an ex-
plicit generation order, separate output pathways, or discrete
prediction sequences. They do not instantiate the interface
studied here: dense metric occupancy and continuous ego
motion jointly generated through a coupled conditional flow.
Joint occupancy–action generation also introduces a
coordinate-representation challenge. Stable world forecasting
benefits from a scene-centric frame, where environmental evo-
lution is not entangled with coordinate changes caused by ego
motion. Planning, in contrast, benefits from an ego-centric
representation that expresses occupied regions relative to the
vehicle’s intended local motion. Scene-centric prediction has
been shown to separate world evolution from ego-induced
perspective changes [17]. World formation and action-aligned
reasoning therefore favor complementary rather than identical
occupancy representations.
We introduce OccWAM, an occupancy-native world–action
model that jointly generates latent future occupancy and con-
tinuous ego motion through conditional flow matching. World
and Action Experts exchange their evolving states at every
transformer layer, allowing predicted occupancy to influence
action formation while the current action hypothesis informs
future-world prediction. Occupancy therefore participates di-
rectly in the generative planning process rather than serving
only as a completed planning condition, auxiliary objective,
or post-hoc trajectory cost. Unlike video-based WAMs, the
generated world retains explicit semantic geometry, support-
ing direct occupancy supervision and metric planning queries
without pixel-space synthesis.
Building on this joint formulation, we propose scene-to-
ego world–action flow matching. Stage I jointly generates
scene-centric occupancy and an absolute ego trajectory in a
fixed current-ego-anchored frame. The predicted world is then
re-indexed into the future ego charts induced by the Stage-
I trajectory, while the trajectory is converted into adjacent
motion increments. This transformed pair forms the structured
source of Stage II, which applies a second joint conditional
flow in an action-aligned ego-centric representation. Stage
I thus models how the world and ego evolve within a stable
scene frame, whereas Stage II models how the same predicted
world relates to local ego motion.
Both stages share a three-expert Mixture-of-Transformers
comprising Understanding, World, and Action Experts. The
Understanding Expert provides a frozen semantic context
stream, while the World and Action Experts interact bidirec-
tionally throughout both flows. Sharing the backbone across
both stages keeps occupancy and action within one unified
model.
We evaluate OccWAM on complementary closed- and open-
loop benchmarks. Bench2Drive evaluates closed-loop driving
under repeated interaction, while nuScenes separately mea-
sures open-loop planning and future volumetric occupancy,
testing both the generated motion and the fidelity of the pre-
dicted metric world.
Our contributions are threefold: (i) We introduce OccWAM,
an occupancy-native world–action model that jointly gener-
ates dense metric future occupancy and continuous ego motion
through a coupled conditional flow, enabling direct bidirec-
tional interaction between world prediction and action genera-
tion; (ii) We propose scene-to-ego world–action flow match-
ing. A scene-centric flow first generates future occupancy
together with an absolute ego trajectory; the predicted world
is then re-indexed into trajectory-induced ego coordinates and
jointly transported with ego pose increments by a second con-
ditional flow; (iii) We instantiate a concrete WAM through
layer-wise bidirectional interaction with a shared three-expert
Mixture-of-Transformers, following the proposed scene-to-
ego world–action flow matching. OccWAM achieves state-
of-the-art closed-loop driving performance on Bench2Drive
and state-of-the-art open-loop planning and future-occupancy
prediction on nuScenes.

Related Work
World models and world–action models.
World models
predict future observations or scene dynamics for simula-
tion, representation learning, and decision-making: recent
work moves toward joint world–action generation in robotics
[6, 7, 18]. In driving, predicted futures enter action generation:
future volumetric features are forecast to inform planning
[12], intention-conditioned latent futures are scored to rank
trajectory candidates [11], video-generator latents condition a
diffusion planner [8], and perception and planning variables
are denoised in a shared diffusion space [19]. Closest to our
formulation, DriveWAM trains video and action streams under
a joint flow-matching objective over an autoregressive tempo-
ral sequence [9], while generative planners model multimodal
trajectory distributions without an explicit future world [3, 20].
OccWAM retains the joint objective but replaces pixel-space
futures with dense metric occupancy, so the generated world
is independently measurable and geometrically aligned with
the action it conditions.
Occupancy prediction and planning.
Occupancy repre-
sents the future scene on a metric grid, so predictions are
evaluated directly in the space where planning constraints
live. OccWorld tokenizes semantic occupancy and autoregres-
sively generates future scene and ego tokens [15], OccLLaMA
extends this to a unified occupancy–language–action vocab-
ulary [16], and later work improves fidelity and efficiency
through dynamic–static factorization [21], rendering supervi-
sion [22], and next-scale temporal prediction [23]. Connecting
the predicted world to action, a few interfaces recur (Figure 1):
2


rolling out action-conditioned futures and selecting by oc-
cupancy cost (a) [10], conditioning a diffusion planner on a
forecast world (b) [13], decoding occupancy and trajectory
in parallel from shared sparse queries (c) [14], or serializing
scene and ego variables into one discrete autoregressive se-
quence [15, 16]. Each imposes an ordering, separate decoders,
or a discretization. Most relevant to us, COME separates envi-
ronmental evolution from ego-induced coordinate change via
ego-invariant scene-centric control features [17]. OccWAM
resolves this separation generatively: dense occupancy and
continuous ego motion emerge from a single coupled velocity
field (d), with the predicted world re-indexed into the frames
its own trajectory induces, then refined jointly with the ego
motion defining them.
Mixture-of-Transformers.
Mixture-of-Transformers as-
signs modality-specific parameters while preserving cross-
modal exchange through joint attention [24], and couples
understanding and action experts in embodied models [25].
Closest to our backbone, AutoMoT connects Understanding
and Action Experts through layer-wise joint attention for driv-
ing [4], and UniDriveVLA adds a third expert for perception
[5]. OccWAM instead makes the third expert a World Expert,
which generates the future occupancy rather than parsing the
present scene. World and Action Experts jointly parameterize
the conditional flows of both stages on a shared three-expert
backbone.

Method
3.1
Overview
Given the current driving context c, including sensor observa-
tions, ego-motion history, navigation inputs, and benchmark-
specific scene context, OccWAM generates a paired future
world and ego trajectory.
Let T denote the benchmark-
specific number of future horizons. The Stage-I supervision is
represented in a fixed scene frame as (OS,⋆, aS,⋆), where
OS,⋆= {[OS,⋆]k}T

O, Eocc(O) and Docc(zo) denote applying the encoder or de-

As illustrated in Fig. 2, OccWAM combines an occupancy-
native world–action generator with scene-to-ego two-stage
flow matching. The generator uses a three-expert Mixture-
of-Transformers backbone comprising Understanding, World,
and Action Experts. The frozen Understanding Expert sup-
plies semantic driving context, while the trainable World and
Action Experts receive the current occupancy and trajectory
flow states and exchange their hidden representations at ev-
ery transformer layer. The two stages reuse the same expert
backbone but employ representation-specific occupancy and
action velocity heads.
Stage I transports independent Gaussian sources toward
scene-centric occupancy and an absolute ego trajectory in a
fixed frame anchored at the current ego pose. The resulting tra-
jectory defines the future ego frames used to warp the decoded
occupancy and is converted into adjacent pose increments.
This transformed pair becomes the structured source of Stage
II. The Stage-II occupancy target is instead the dataset’s native
future occupancy expressed in the ground-truth ego frame at
every future horizon. Stage II then transports a model-induced
source containing occupancy prediction errors, warp-induced
uncovered regions, and pose misalignment toward a complete
ego-centric world–action target without introducing new Gaus-
sian noise.
Throughout, r ∈{S, E} indexes the scene-centric and ego-
centric flow representations, respectively. In the S representa-
tion, occupancy and cumulative ego poses are expressed in a
fixed current-ego-anchored scene frame. In the E representa-
tion, each occupancy slice is expressed in its corresponding
future ego frame, while each action element is the adjacent
pose increment, whose translational components are expressed
along the fixed axes of a canonical sensor-aligned chart. We
reserve t ∈[0, 1] for flow time and k ∈{1, . . . , T} for the
physical future horizon. The symbol ⋆denotes supervision
targets,˜· the Stage-I output,¯· the transformed Stage-II source,
and ˆ· the final Stage-II output. The operator sg(·) denotes
stop-gradient.

The occupancy autoencoder and
Understanding Expert remain frozen during world–action
training. Stage I is trained from independent Gaussian sources
through LS
FM. During every training iteration, its one-step
output is detached, decoded, transformed through W, and
re-encoded to construct the Stage-II source. Stage II inde-

Beyond the two flow-matching objectives, we introduce an
auxiliary world–action coupling loss that evaluates the pre-
dicted trajectory against the predicted occupancy field. Let
Φ(P, a) denote an occupancy-based trajectory cost that sam-
ples the per-class probability field P along the ego footprint
induced by trajectory a.
In Stage I, the cost is evaluated in the fixed scene-centric
frame, penalizing departure from drivable regions and overlap
with static and dynamic obstacles. In Stage II, the adjacent
pose increments are accumulated to recover the trajectory in
the fixed current-ego coordinate frame. For coupling-cost eval-
uation, the occupancy probability field and the reconstructed
trajectory are expressed in the same coordinate frame, and the
same penalties are applied.
Both coupling terms are evaluated as Φ(sg(P), a), where
sg denotes stop-gradient. This prevents the coupling loss from
directly back-propagating through the predicted occupancy
probabilities, avoiding the degenerate solution of reducing
collision penalties by removing obstacles from the predicted
world.

4.1
Experimental Setup
Benchmarks and metrics.
Bench2Drive is our primary
closed-loop benchmark, where we report Driving Score (DS)
and Success Rate (SR) [40]. On nuScenes [41], we evalu-
ate open-loop planning using average L2 displacement error
and collision rate. We additionally evaluate future volumetric
occupancy on Occ3D using semantic mIoU and geometric
IoU [42, 43]. All results follow the official validation splits
and evaluation protocols. For nuScenes, open-loop planning
uses camera observations, whereas future-occupancy predic-
tion uses four historical 3D occupancy frames; these are task-
specific evaluation configurations, not outputs of one identical
raw-input forward pass (Appendix B.1). Bench2Drive uses
front RGB and LiDAR BEV observations at inference and
does not use ground-truth occupancy as a runtime input.

4.2
Closed-Loop Driving on Bench2Drive
We first evaluate OccWAM on the Bench2Drive closed-loop
benchmark and report the quantitative results in Table 1. Oc-
cWAM achieves the best performance in both Driving Score
and Success Rate, reaching 90.45 and 77.27%, respectively.
It surpasses AutoMoT+ [4], the strongest expert-only base-
line with diffusion-based action refinement (DS 89.42, SR
74.09%), and KnowVal-SimLingo [35], the strongest prior
world-model-based system (DS 88.42, SR 69.03%).
The improvement highlights the benefit of the occupancy-
native world–action formulation. By representing future oc-
cupancy and ego motion within a shared generative state, Oc-
cWAM provides planning with an explicit, metric description
of the evolving scene. This unified representation preserves
the spatial structure of the predicted future throughout action
generation, providing a structured basis for accurate and safe
planning.
4.3
Open-Loop Planning and Future Occu-
pancy on nuScenes
Table 2 evaluates the action and world components under a
fixed driving log. The planning metrics measure trajectory
imitation and geometric collision, while the occupancy met-
rics verify that the World Expert produces an independently
meaningful metric future rather than an unconstrained hid-
den feature optimized only for action prediction. OccWAM
achieves the best average planning L2 and collision rate, to-
gether with the best average mIoU and IoU.
The two evaluations are reported together to examine both
sides of the generated world–action state. Strong planning
without a measurable future world would reduce occupancy to
an opaque internal representation, whereas accurate occupancy
without a planning gain would not establish an action-facing
predictive state. The results show that OccWAM improves
planning while preserving a high-quality explicit future world.

5
Conclusion
We presented OccWAM, an occupancy-native world–action
model that jointly generates dense metric occupancy and con-
tinuous ego motion through coupled flow matching. A shared
three-expert Mixture-of-Transformers enables direct World–
Action interaction, making occupancy an explicit and measur-
able future representation that participates in action generation.
Our scene-to-ego design first predicts scene-centric occupancy
and an absolute trajectory, then transports the transformed hy-
pothesis toward an ego-centric occupancy–motion endpoint
through a second joint flow. Experiments on Bench2Drive and
nuScenes demonstrate state-of-the-art performance in closed-
loop driving, open-loop planning, and future-occupancy pre-
diction.

Supplementary Material
A
Overview
This appendix accompanies the main paper in the same PDF.
It details benchmark-specific occupancy modules, world-state
representations, and training procedures, including the dis-
tinct nuScenes input protocols for planning and occupancy
prediction. We further clarify the coverage conditioning and
supervision used in Stage II, together with the auxiliary world–
action coupling objectives.
B
Additional Implementation Details
B.1
Benchmark-Specific Occupancy Modules
We use the term benchmark-specific Occ Encoder to refer to
the complete occupancy module associated with each bench-
mark. It connects the observations available under the corre-
sponding task protocol to the World Expert and also contains
the mappings between metric occupancy and latent occupancy
states. Depending on the benchmark and evaluation task, its
input-processing pathway converts sensor observations or his-
torical occupancy states into a compact world representation
supplied to the World Expert.
Within this module, Eocc and Docc map semantic occupancy
between the metric grid and the latent flow space. Specifically,
Eocc encodes semantic occupancy targets or transformed oc-
cupancy states into latent representations, while Docc decodes
predicted occupancy latents back into the metric semantic
grid.
Bench2Drive neither provides nor uses historical or current
ground-truth occupancy as model input. Its Occ Encoder takes
one front-view RGB image and one LiDAR BEV represen-
tation and produces the latent world features supplied to the
World Expert. The model predicts six future BEV occupancy
frames at 2 Hz over a 64 m × 64 m ego-centered region with a
spatial resolution of 1 m. Each predicted frame is represented
as a 64 × 64 semantic grid with 11 categorical classes, includ-
ing free space. In the latent flow space, the complete future
occupancy sequence has shape 6×8×8×8 (T ×H ×W ×C),
corresponding to 384 occupancy tokens of dimension 8.
nuScenes uses two task-specific input protocols. For the
planning benchmark, the model receives camera observations,
and L2 and collision are evaluated under this camera-based
planning protocol. For occupancy prediction, the benchmark-
specific Occ Encoder jointly processes four historical 3D oc-
cupancy frames and compresses their temporal information
into a compact set of 625 history tokens. The four-frame his-
tory is therefore represented by 625 temporally condensed
tokens in total, rather than by four separate sets of 625 tokens.
Only these condensed history tokens are supplied to the World
Expert, preventing the Mixture-of-Transformers from directly
processing separate token sets for all four historical frames.
The nuScenes occupancy-prediction target consists of six
future volumetric occupancy frames indexed by (t, x, y, z).
Each frame covers the spatial range [−40, 40] × [−40, 40] ×
[−1, 5.4] m at a voxel resolution of 0.4 m and is decoded into
a dense 200 × 200 × 16 grid with 17 semantic classes plus
free space. The reported mIoU and IoU are computed under
this four-frame historical-occupancy protocol.
Accordingly, planning and occupancy numbers shown in
the same result row should be interpreted as evaluations of
the corresponding task-specific configurations of the same
method under their respective benchmark protocols, rather
than as outputs produced by the same raw-input forward pass.
Within each metric group, all compared configurations use the
same input protocol and evaluation procedure.
The Understanding Expert follows the same training proto-
col on both benchmarks. It is first adapted using benchmark-
specific question–answering supervision and is subsequently
frozen throughout World–Action training.
Bench2Drive spatial route prediction.
Bench2Drive ad-
ditionally requires the prediction of 20 spatial route points
representing the reference-path geometry. These points are
distinct from the temporal ego trajectory generated by the joint
occupancy–action flows. We append a dedicated learnable
route query after the occupancy and action tokens and decode
its final representation directly into the route-point sequence.
The route query is supervised by the benchmark-provided
route points and remains outside the joint flow. It receives
no Gaussian source, flow-time interpolation, or velocity-
matching objective. This benchmark-specific route branch
is used only on Bench2Drive.
B.2
Model and Training Configuration
World–Action training optimizes the World and Action Ex-
perts, four representation-specific output decoders, and the
benchmark-specific prediction modules. The four output de-
coders correspond to the occupancy and action outputs of
the scene-centric and ego-centric flows. They implement the
representation-specific velocity readouts in the main formu-
lation and are distinct from the occupancy decoder Docc con-
tained in the Occ Encoder.
The benchmark-specific trainable modules include the
Bench2Drive route branch, the associated action and trajectory
modules, newly introduced projection layers, and the scene-
prior module used by the Bench2Drive configuration. The
Understanding-Expert context is computed once and reused
across both stages, and each stage requires one World–Action
backbone pass.
Stage I and Stage II are optimized jointly rather than trained
as separate models. In the notation of Eq. (16), we set λII =
0.5, so the aggregate Stage-II objective receives one half of
the weight assigned to its Stage-I counterpart. Equivalently,
the Stage-I and Stage-II aggregate losses are weighted at a
ratio of 2:1. The same weighting is used for both Bench2Drive
and nuScenes.
Bench2Drive uses two training phases. Phase 1 adapts
the Understanding Expert using benchmark-specific question–

Benchmark-specific auxiliary objectives.
Equation (16)
specifies the joint flow-matching and World–Action coupling
objective shared by both benchmarks. In addition to these com-
mon terms, Bench2Drive uses decoded occupancy reconstruc-
tion and trajectory-consistency objectives, while nuScenes
uses an ego-future prediction objective.
The decoded occupancy reconstruction objective supervises
semantic occupancy after the predicted occupancy latent has
been passed through Docc. This decoded semantic supervi-

decoder.
The trajectory-consistency objective provides additional su-

supervision for the nuScenes future-ego representation. These
auxiliary terms complement the latent flow supervision with-

procedure.

Supervision of uncovered source regions.
The coverage
pair characterizes the Stage-II source, not the validity of its
supervision. In particular, M E is not a loss mask and does not
turn uncovered cells into unlabeled targets. The ego-centric
occupancy objective in Eq. (5) uses all available valid na-
tive future-ego occupancy targets, including targets in regions
uncovered by ¯OE. Source coverage and target-label availabil-
ity are separate notions: the same flow-matching objective
applies to covered and uncovered source regions whenever
corresponding supervision is valid. No distinct hole-filling
loss or unobserved-as-free target is introduced.
At an uncovered source location, the free-space value is a
categorical encoding placeholder, not a claim that the future
world is free or that the target label is unknown. A valid future-
ego target, if available, supplies the supervision independently
of the source coverage. Thus Stage II can infer content absent
from the Stage-I transformed source and also correct predic-
tion errors and pose-induced spatial misalignment where the
source is covered. Where the native annotation has no valid
semantic target, the placeholder must not be interpreted as an
additional free-space training label.
Evaluation protocol.
Occupancy evaluation follows the
standard benchmark protocol and does not use M E as an
evaluation mask. The reported mIoU and IoU are computed
over the standard native future-ego occupancy target rather
than only over regions covered by the transformed Stage-I
source.
B.4
World–Action Coupling Objectives
Both stages employ an auxiliary coupling objective between
the generated occupancy and trajectory. In Stage I, the abso-
lute trajectory is evaluated against the scene-centric occupancy.
In Stage II, the accumulated ego-pose increments are evalu-
ated against the completed ego-centric occupancy.
In both stages, the coupling objective penalizes trajectory
regions that depart from the drivable surface or overlap with
static and dynamic occupancy. The occupancy probabilities
are detached when these terms are computed. Consequently,
the coupling objectives update the Action Expert without di-
rectly encouraging the World Expert to remove obstacles from
its predicted future.
