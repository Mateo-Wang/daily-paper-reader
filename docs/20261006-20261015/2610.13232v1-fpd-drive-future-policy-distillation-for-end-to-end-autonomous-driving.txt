ABSTRACT
End-to-end autonomous driving models are typically trained on real-world driv-
ing logs, which record only the trajectory actually executed in each scene. This
provides limited supervision for learning alternative valid behaviors. Multi-target
supervision can mitigate this limitation, but broader trajectory coverage makes
safe candidate selection more challenging. We propose FPD-Drive, an end-to-
end planner that distills privileged future knowledge into a visual driving pol-
icy to improve both multi-trajectory supervision and safety selection. Specifi-
cally, we introduce Future-Privileged Multi-Target Distillation to transfer diverse,
high-quality trajectory targets from a teacher with access to recorded future ob-
servations to the student.
These targets extend supervision beyond the single
recorded human trajectory. For candidate selection, we further introduce Safety-
Aware Score Correction to correct ranking errors between nearby safe and un-
safe candidates and raise underestimated safety scores of safe candidates. At de-
ployment, the student requires neither future observations nor teacher inference.
Our base model achieves 94.9 PDMS on NAVSIM v1 navtest and 55.9 EPDMS
on NAVSIM v2 navhard. The scaled FPD-Drive-Pro improves these scores to
95.5 PDMS and 58.1 EPDMS, respectively, achieving SOTA performance among
the compared methods on both benchmarks. Zero-shot closed-loop evaluation
on HUGSIM further yields an HD-Score of 45.1 and RC of 55.3. Project page:
https://countnemochan.github.io/FPD-Drive/.

INTRODUCTION
End-to-end autonomous driving aims to learn planning policies directly from sensor observa-
tions (Hu et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2023). Advances in visual representation learning and generative
trajectory modeling have enabled planners to produce and evaluate multiple candidate trajectories for
a driving scene (Kirby et al., 2026; Liao et al., 2025; Xing et al., 2025). Effective planning requires
both generating valid alternatives and reliably selecting a safe trajectory for execution. However,
learning these capabilities from human drivers is challenging, because each logged driving clip used
for training typically provides only one executed trajectory and its associated visual observations.
As a result, it provides limited guidance for learning and evaluating alternative trajectories.
A recorded human trajectory represents one choice among several potentially acceptable driving be-
haviors. For example, different deceleration timings or speed profiles may be valid in the same scene,
although only one is recorded. Closeness to the demonstration therefore cannot fully determine an
alternative trajectory’s quality. To supervise such alternatives, recent methods use predefined trajec-
tory vocabularies or teachers pretrained on rule-generated targets (Wang et al., 2026c; Ang et al.,
2026; Chen et al., 2026a). These approaches broaden supervision, but constructing alternatives
that are both diverse and scene-appropriate remains challenging. Predefined vocabularies and hand-
crafted generation rules can restrict the range of candidate behaviors covered by supervision. And
recorded future observations reveal how the scene’s structure and evolution can help guide planning

alternative trajectories. These motivates a privileged teacher that uses these observations to generate
additional trajectory targets.
To benefit from broader trajectory coverage, the planner should also learn to select a safe candidate
reliably. Planning metrics provide supervision beyond imitation to help select candidates, but ac-
curate safety assessment remains challenging (Li et al., 2024; Kirby et al., 2026). Geometrically
similar trajectories can differ sharply in safety metrics, and a scorer may favor an unsafe candidate
over a nearby safe alternative. Safe candidates may also be overlooked if the scorer underestimates
their safety. These two types of scoring errors motivate targeted score correction to distinguish
nearby safe and unsafe candidates and raise the scores of underestimated safe alternatives.
We propose FPD-Drive, a future policy distillation framework that enriches trajectory supervision
with privileged future knowledge while jointly improving candidate selection. The teacher and stu-
dent share a coarse-to-fine visual planner that refines trajectory proposals through residual diffusion.
During training, the teacher uses recorded future observations to guide trajectory refinement and
scoring. We then evaluate its candidates using planning metrics and select high-quality, geomet-
rically diverse trajectory targets. Future-Privileged Multi-Target Distillation transfers these targets
to the student, extending supervision beyond the recorded demonstration. For candidate selection,
Safety-Aware Score Correction uses evaluated safety labels for the student’s own trajectories to cor-
rect scoring errors. It penalizes highly ranked unsafe candidates relative to nearby safe alternatives
and increases confidence in underestimated safe candidates.
The deployed student requires neither future observations nor teacher inference. FPD-Drive achieves
state-of-the-art performance on NAVSIM v1 and v2. Zero-shot evaluation on HUGSIM further
supports its transfer to closed-loop driving. Our contributions are threefold:
• We propose FPD-Drive, which uses recorded future observations to help a teacher generate
additional high-quality trajectories for distilling a visual student. Only the student is needed
at deployment.
• We introduce Future-Privileged Multi-Target Distillation, which transfers diverse, high-
quality trajectories from a future-conditioned teacher to the student, extending supervision
beyond the single recorded demonstration.
• We develop Safety-Aware Score Correction to correct the relative safety scores of unsafe–
safe pairs and raise underestimated safety scores for safe trajectories.

RELATED WORK
End-to-end autonomous driving.
End-to-end autonomous driving learns planning policies di-
rectly from sensor observations. Research on planning representations includes multimodal sensor
fusion (Prakash et al., 2021) and integrating perception, prediction, and planning (Hu et al., 2023;
Jiang et al., 2023). To account for multiple acceptable driving behaviors, probabilistic and gener-
ative planners model distributions over possible trajectories (Jiang et al., 2026; Liao et al., 2025;
Xing et al., 2025). ResAD (Zheng et al., 2026) further improves trajectory representations through
normalized residual modeling to address imbalanced optimization (Zheng et al., 2026). Beyond
generation, metric-based supervision and generalized trajectory evaluation address reliable selection
among alternative behaviors (Li et al., 2024; 2025c; Kirby et al., 2026). World models and world-
action models (WAMs) introduce predictive information by learning how driving scenes evolve.
Visual forecasting (Wang et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2024) and latent prediction (Li et al., 2025a; Wang
et al., 2026b) represent future scene dynamics at different levels. Predicted future states support
candidate evaluation (Li et al., 2025b; Zheng et al., 2025; Zhong et al., 2026), while joint world–
action modeling couples future scene prediction with trajectory generation (Zhang et al., 2025; Liu
et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026d). Vision-language-action (VLA) models draw on pretrained seman-
tic knowledge and language understanding to support planning (Xu et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2024;
Hwang et al., 2025). These methods connect semantic scene understanding with driving actions by
conditioning trajectory generation on visual-language representations (Zhou et al., 2026b; Li et al.,
2026; Wang et al., 2026e). Graph-based visual question answering connects scene understanding
with driving decisions (Sima et al., 2025), while reasoning-augmented policies integrate semantic
reasoning with action generation (Zhou et al., 2025; NVIDIA et al., 2025). To balance planning per-
formance and inference latency, FPD-Drive adopts a coarse-to-fine visual planner as the common

Distillation for autonomous driving.
Knowledge distillation and privileged learning have at-
tracted growing interest in autonomous driving. Teachers with access to ground-truth road structure
and traffic-participant states provide trajectory targets for sensor-based students (Chen et al., 2020;
Zhang et al., 2021; He et al., 2026). Feature distillation also transfers scene and planning repre-
sentations from these teachers (Zhang et al., 2023; Jia et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2025). Recent VLA
methods condition teachers on privileged future information and distill token distributions into stu-
dent driving policies (Cao et al., 2026; Dou, 2026). To broaden trajectory, Curious-VLA (Chen et al.,
2026a) expands training data with trajectories generated by the ReCogDrive-VLA Li et al. (2026).
Some concurrent methods explore multi-target trajectory supervision using predefined trajectory
vocabularies (Wang et al., 2026c; Sun et al., 2026) or teacher models pretrained on rule-generated
trajectories (Ang et al., 2026). Without relying on predefined trajectories or vocabulary, FPD-Drive
combines a future-conditioned teacher with multi-target trajectory policy to distill a student. We fur-
ther introduce Safety-Aware Score Correction to improve safety assessment and candidate ranking.

3.1
COARSE-TO-FINE TRAJECTORY MODELING
We separate trajectory generation into scene-conditioned proposals and residual refinement. As il-
lustrated in Fig. 1, the proposal decoder provides a finite set of starting trajectories, which may
cluster within a limited region of trajectory space. A diffusion refiner then models continuous ad-
justments around these proposals to improve trajectory quality and broaden candidate coverage.
This provides more alternatives for selection under different driving preferences. We adopt this
coarse-to-fine structure as the common backbone for the teacher and student.

Candidate scoring.
We use a transformer-based trajectory scorer with self-attention among can-

driving preferences without retraining (Kirby et al., 2026).
3.2

provides limited supervision for these alternatives. We therefore train a teacher model using recorded
future observations as privileged conditioning. The teacher aggregates observations across multiple
future time steps into a global scene representation. This representation guides trajectory refinement
to produce additional high-quality target behaviors.

3.4
SAFETY-AWARE SCORE CORRECTION
Multi-target distillation provides diverse trajectory supervision, but safe candidate selection also
requires accurate safety scores. Highly ranked unsafe candidates can displace safe alternatives,
while underestimated safe candidates may be overlooked. In performance evaluation, a zero safety
label reduces the overall score to zero, regardless of other metric values. We therefore introduce
Safety-Aware Score Correction, using evaluated safety labels for the student’s own candidates to
correct both errors.

3.5
OPTIMIZATION AND DEPLOYMENT
Training proceeds in three stages. First, we train a base coarse-to-fine visual planner using recorded
supervision. Second, we initialize the teacher from this base and introduce future observations
through the global scene encoder to condition refinement and scoring. Both stages use proposal,
diffusion, trajectory reconstruction, and score losses, with demonstrations as trajectory targets and
evaluated metrics as score labels. Third, we freeze the teacher and cache its selected targets. The
student is initialized from the first-stage base and fine-tuned with multi-target distillation and Safety-
Aware Score Correction:

Ablation studies.
We conduct all ablations using our base FPD-Drive. Component ablations in
Table 4 examine the teacher and student designs. For each teacher, the two PDMS values corre-
spond to selection by the learned scorer and the PDM-Closed evaluator, respectively. PDM-Closed
selects higher-quality trajectories under the evaluation metrics, supporting its use for teacher-target
selection. However, we still train the scorer jointly with the teacher because gradients from met-
ric prediction help improve scene representations for trajectory generation. Future tokens and the
global scene encoder improve the teacher’s trajectory prediction, while residual DiT refinement
benefits both the teacher and student, supporting our backbone design. The student performs best
when Future-Privileged Multi-Target Distillation and Safety-Aware Score Correction are combined,
suggesting complementary contributions to trajectory generation and selection.
The ablation on multi-target distillation in Table 5(a) examines the effect of target budget. Here,
K denotes the maximum target budget per scene, and ¯J denotes the average number of targets re-
tained after quality and diversity filtering. Using multiple targets improves student performance,
with diminishing gains at larger target budgets. The ablation on supervised trajectory selection in
Table 5(b) shows that selecting higher-quality targets with PDM-Closed improves student perfor-
mance compared with learned-scorer selection. Under both selection criteria, distillation from a
future-privileged teacher outperforms self-distillation without future information. Additional abla-
tions on future-token injection locations, token-level distillation policy, distillation target sources
and number of DiT denoising steps are provided in Appendix E.

CONCLUSION
We presented FPD-Drive, a framework that uses recorded futures to expand what a driving policy
can learn from each demonstration. A future-informed teacher provides multiple alternatives, which
are evaluated and filtered into trajectory targets for the student. Targeted score correction helps the
9


student distinguish unsafe candidates from nearby safe alternatives and recognize safe options it
would otherwise undervalue. FPD-Drive achieves state-of-the-art performance on NAVSIM v1 and
v2, while zero-shot closed-loop evaluation on HUGSIM further demonstrates its generalization to
closed-loop driving. Limitations and future directions are discussed in Appendix F.

APPENDIX
A
TRAINING AND INFERENCE DETAILS
Training consists of three stages. In Stage 1, we train the coarse-to-fine planner described in Sec. 3.1,
which serves as the common backbone for the teacher and student, for 30 epochs, starting with a
learning rate of 2 × 10−4. In Stage 2, we initialize the teacher from the Stage 1 model and train
it for 10 epochs with privileged future observations, starting with a learning rate of 2 × 10−4. In
Stage 3, we freeze the teacher and train the student, initialized from the Stage 1 model, using Future-
Privileged Multi-Target Distillation and Safety-Aware Score Correction for 10 epochs, starting with
a learning rate of 2 × 10−5. We conducted all training on 8×H800 GPUs with a batch size of 16,
using the AdamW optimizer and a cosine learning rate decay strategy with linear warmup. We adopt
gradient clipping with global norm 1.0, and the training precision is BF16. Table 6 details the model
configuration.
Following the training-data setup of DrivoR (Kirby et al., 2026), we train our base model,
FPD-Drive, on approximately 106K NAVSIM trainval scenes and 134K SimScale scenes.
NAVSIM (Dauner et al., 2024) provides real-world driving logs derived from nuPlan, including
multi-view images and human driving trajectories; SimScale (Tian et al., 2026) augments recorded
scenes through reactive simulation, providing rendered multi-view observations and pseudo-expert
trajectories. We further train FPD-Drive-Pro with additional controllably generated driving scenar-
ios to broaden behavioral coverage, with generation details and data composition in Sec. B and
Table 7, and the resulting scaling trends in Fig. 3.
At inference, the model takes four camera views from the current frame, the current ego state, and a
navigation command. The refiner starts from Gaussian residual noise and runs 8 DDIM steps (Song
et al., 2021) before adding the residual to each of the 64 trajectory proposals. The scorer then
selects one refined trajectory for execution. The output is an 8 × 3 sequence of future ego poses
(xh, yh, ψh) at 0.5-second intervals, covering the next 4 seconds. All poses are expressed in the
current ego coordinate frame, with positions (xh, yh) in meters and heading ψh in radians.

NAVSIM EVALUATION METRICS
NAVSIM evaluates planning in a pseudo-closed-loop setting. An LQR controller tracks the predicted
4 s trajectory using a kinematic bicycle model at 10 Hz. The resulting ego states are evaluated against
the map, route, and surrounding traffic. Each sub-metric takes a value in [0, 1] and is reported as a
percentage. The composite score combines multiplicative factors with a fixed weighted average of
the remaining metrics. NAVSIM v1 and v2 differ in both their metric sets and scenario organization.
NAVSIM v1 (navtest) uses two multiplicative factors: no at-fault collisions (NC) and drivable-
area compliance (DAC). NC considers only collisions for which the ego vehicle is responsible,
while DAC checks whether the vehicle footprint remains within the drivable area. The weighted
component includes ego progress (EP), time-to-collision within bounds (TTC), and comfort (C). EP
15


measures progress along the centerline relative to the PDM-Closed reference. TTC checks whether
the time to collision with any other vehicle remains above a fixed threshold. C checks whether the
simulated trajectory satisfies bounds on acceleration, jerk, and yaw rate. The composite score is:
PDMS = NC · DAC × 5 EP + 5 TTC + 2 C
12
.
(11)
NAVSIM v2 (navhard two stage) adds two multiplicative factors: driving-direction compli-
ance (DDC) and traffic-light compliance (TLC). DDC penalizes travel against the lane direction
within a 1 s window. Its score is 1 for wrong-way travel below 2 m, 0.5 for travel between 2 m and
6 m, and 0 for travel beyond 6 m. TLC checks whether the ego vehicle crosses a stop line at a red
light.
The weighted component additionally includes lane keeping (LK), which checks lateral deviation
from the centerline using a 0.5 m threshold over a 2 s window. The original comfort metric is re-
placed by history comfort (HC) and extended comfort (EC). HC evaluates the simulated trajectory
together with the recorded past to assess comfort at their junction. EC concatenates trajectories
predicted at two adjacent frames of the same scene and applies the comfort bounds to the com-
bined sequence. This penalizes inconsistencies between successive planning steps. The extended
composite score is:
EPDMS = NC · DAC · DDC · TLC × 5 EP + 5 TTC + 2 LK + 2 HC + 2 EC

.
(12)
NAVSIM v2 also changes how it organizes scenarios and aggregates scores. Its 5,912 scenarios form
225 groups, each evaluated from two start frames separated by 0.5 s. The first stage evaluates the
original recorded frames. The second stage evaluates synthetic continuations in which surrounding
traffic responds to the planned ego behavior. For each start frame, scores are averaged separately
within each stage, and the two stage averages are multiplied. The resulting scores for the two start
frames are then averaged to obtain the group score. The final EPDMS is the mean across all groups.

Token-level versus multi-target distillation.
Inspired by privileged policy distillation in Evo-
DriveVLA (Cao et al., 2026), we compare token-level distillation with our Future-Privileged Multi-
Target Distillation. We adapt their token-distribution matching idea to our diffusion planner by
applying a token-level KL divergence between teacher and student distributions during DiT denois-
ing. The teacher has access to recorded future observations, whereas the student uses only current
observations. The token-level variant underperforms multi-target distillation, as summarized in Ta-
ble 9. This suggests that matching token distributions during denoising is less effective in our setting
than explicitly supervising multiple complete trajectories selected for planning quality and geomet-
ric diversity. Future-Privileged Multi-Target Distillation assigns these alternatives to distinct student
proposals, directly encouraging coverage of multiple valid behaviors in the same scene.

LIMITATIONS
FPD-Drive still incurs inference overhead from iterative DiT sampling. Future work will explore
single-step denoising and refinement, motivated by MeanFuser (Wang et al., 2026a) ’s efficient one-
step trajectory generation. Further evaluation is needed to assess robustness during longer-horizon
interactions. We have not evaluated the model under extreme weather conditions, such as heavy rain
and snow, or deployed it on real vehicles, so we leave these settings for future validation.
