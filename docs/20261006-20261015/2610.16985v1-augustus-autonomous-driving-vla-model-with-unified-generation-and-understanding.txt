Vision-language-action (VLA) models have shown strong potential for end-to-end autonomous
driving by leveraging world knowledge and understanding to achieve great performance in
long-tail scenarios. However, current VLAs suffer from two major limitations: 1) Degeneration
into vision-action (VA) models. To achieve low latency, regression-based and flow-based methods
directly generate actions, yet they do not sufficiently leverage the VLM’s understanding, leading
to unsafe and unexplainable actions. 2) Autoregressive high latency. Autoregressive methods
sequentially generate chain-of-thought (CoT) and action tokens, fully leveraging the VLM’s
understanding, but high latency makes deployment difficult. To enable low-latency deployment
while properly using CoT to assist action generation, we propose AUGUstus1, an Autonomous-
driving VLA model with Unified Generation and Understanding. Specifically, we first unify
diffusion-based action generation and autoregressive CoT understanding in a single model, where
understanding is optimized only to serve generation. More concretely, joint CoT supervision
preserves the VLM’s spatial understanding in the shared representation and keeps it useful
for safer and more accurate trajectories. Second, we propose AUGUstus-mode, which orders
the action generation before the CoT so that the CoT supervises the reliable action during
training while inference needs only the action output, enabling low latency. Finally, we introduce
AUGUstus-GRPO, a unified GRPO algorithm that jointly optimizes the action and CoT, further
improving action performance. Extensive experiments demonstrate that AUGUstus achieves
94.6 PDMS on NAVSIMv1 and 90.7 EPDMS on NAVSIMv2, establishing state-of-the-art (SOTA)
planning performance without scorer module. On nuScenes, our method achieves an average L2
error of 0.27 m and an average collision rate of 0.05%, both the best among the compared methods.
To validate the practicality of AUGUstus, we train it on our in-house dataset using 512 NVIDIA
H800 GPUs for approximately two days, then evaluate it on an internal 1,000 km dataset and
preliminarily deploy it on a real vehicle, achieving 12 Hz operation.

Introduction
Autonomous driving has gradually moved from modular stacks for perception, prediction, and planning
toward end-to-end models to reduce error accumulation, including ST-P3 (Hu et al., 2022), UniAD (Hu
et al., 2023), and VAD (Jiang et al., 2023). Yet their limited model capacity and training data restrict the
world knowledge available to the policy, making long-tail scenes and semantically complex interactions
difficult to handle reliably.
To address long-tail scenarios, many recent studies have explored large vision-language models (VLMs)
as a promising foundation model for autonomous driving, leveraging their large-scale pre-training to
supply broad visual concepts, language priors, and reasoning capabilities (Zhao et al., 2026; Xiong et al.,
2026; Zhou et al., 2026b; Ye et al., 2026b). However, current mainstream autonomous driving VLA
architectures still exhibit three limitations: 1) Regression head: Despite low latency, these methods (Zhao
et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025) produce no action distribution, making RL post-training difficult; moreover,

Diffusion or flow-matching expert: To address the lack of action distribution for RL algorithm, some VLA
methods (Xiong et al., 2026) adopt diffusion or flow-matching experts for action generation, but they still
do not fully exploit the VLM’s spatial understanding, thereby producing unsafe and unexplainable actions

VLM’s understanding with low-latency control. This raises a key question: can we design an architecture that keeps
this action-relevant understanding while remaining low-latency and easy to deploy?
We argue that these limitations lie in the separation between VLM-based understanding and action
generation, rather than treating them as a unified process. Autonomous driving requires understanding
and action generation to work in synergy, where accurate spatial understanding enables reliable action
generation. However, conventional approaches decouple them: direct regression and diffusion/flow
experts degenerate into Vision-Action (VA) models that produce unsafe and unexplainable actions,
whereas autoregressive CoT leverages understanding but serializes reasoning before action, causing
high latency. Accordingly, we reconsider the role of CoT understanding in autonomous driving. Rather
than treating CoT as an independent reasoning stage or a serial prefix to actions, CoT should be jointly
optimized with action generation within a unified architecture, where action generation is the core and
CoT serves to improve it. Such a unified formulation keeps VLM spatial understanding coupled to
and useful for the action policy, while inference only outputs actions, preserving an RL-friendly action
distribution and low latency.
To address above fundamental limitations, we propose AUGUstus, an Autonomous-driving VLA model
with Unified Generation and Understanding (Fig. 1d). Our objective is action-centric: understanding is
not trained for its own sake but to generate a safer and more accurate trajectory. Specifically, (1) we unify
diffusion-based action generation and autoregressive CoT understanding in a single model, where joint
CoT supervision preserves the VLM’s spatial understanding in the shared representation for better action
generation; (2) we propose AUGUstus-mode, an action-first and CoT-second ordering. This forces the
model to learn a CoT that benefits action performance, while inference only needs the action output,
thereby maintaining low latency. We explore various CoT designs and find that a CoT describing the
specific positions and future orientations of other agents in the scene is most effective for action; and (3)
we also introduce AUGUstus-GRPO, a unified GRPO algorithm that jointly optimizes the continuous
action and CoT streams so that reasoning serves planning, further improving planning capabilities.
We extensively evaluate AUGUstus on closed-loop NAVSIM and open-loop nuScenes. AUGUstus achieves
94.6 PDMS on NAVSIMv1 and 90.7 EPDMS on NAVSIMv2, without a scorer module. On nuScenes, our
method achieves an average L2 error of 0.27 m and an average collision rate of 0.05%, both the best among
the compared methods. To validate the practicality of AUGUstus, we train it on our in-house dataset

Related Work
VLA models for autonomous driving. Recent driving VLAs follow three designs. Query-based policies (Zhao
et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026b; Shao et al., 2026) are low-latency but without action distribution for RL
and weakly leveraging VLM semantics, which can yield unsafe and unexplainable actions. External
flow-matching experts (Xiong et al., 2026) model continuous action distributions but keep the generator
outside the VLM, weakening VLM semantics. Autoregressive policies (Zhou et al., 2026b; Ye et al., 2026b;
Wang et al., 2026) expose semantic reasoning but serialize CoT and action tokens, incurring high latency.
These designs struggle to trade off VLM understanding and low latency. AUGUstus unifies continuous
action tokens and CoT understanding in one transformer under AUGUstus-mode, where understanding
is trained to serve action generation rather than to maximize its own output quality, and deployment
outputs actions directly.
Reinforcement learning for driving VLAs. Driving-oriented VLMs increasingly adopt GRPO-style post-
training, such as AutoVLA, AutoDrive-R2, AutoDrive-P3, and RecogDrive (Zhou et al., 2026b; Yuan et al.,
2026; Ye et al., 2026b; Xiong et al., 2026). RecogDrive directly supervises actions but lacks explicit CoT
supervision, making it prone to reward hacking. Other autoregressive methods supervise token-level
outputs, which slows both training and inference. In contrast, AUGUstus-GRPO jointly optimizes CoT
and action under a shared group-relative advantage, with CoT further improving action performance.
3
Methodology
This section introduces AUGUstus framework, which unifies generation and understanding within a single
transformer. First, we unify continuous, flow-based action generation with autoregressive, token-based
CoT understanding in one transformer. Second, we propose AUGUstus-mode, an action-first, CoT-second
training scheme in which the CoT stream is optimized to supervise and improve action generation. At
inference, only the action stream is evaluated, ensuring low-latency deployment. Finally, we introduce
AUGUstus-GRPO, a unified reinforcement learning algorithm that further improves the VLA’s planning
capability.

plan,𝑖= PDMS𝑖while keeping 𝑅pred,𝑖. This encourages assertive
progress and aligns the policy with the benchmark objective. Stage II starts from the Stage-I checkpoint
and sets 𝑅(2)
plan,𝑖= 𝑅end,𝑖+ 𝑅safe,𝑖, preserving the same 𝑅pred,𝑖. It therefore retains the progress learned
in Stage I while refining the trajectory toward accurate endpoints and safe behavior. In both stages, the
shared reward and advantage in Eq. 4 jointly update JaT-GRPO and CoT-GRPO; only planning reward is
different.
4
Experiments
4.1
Benchmarks and Baselines
Public benchmarks. Following (Zhou et al., 2026b; Xiong et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026b), we evaluate on the
NAVSIM (Dauner et al., 2024) and nuScenes (Caesar et al., 2020) benchmarks. For both benchmarks, the
input is a 2-second, 4-frame clip from the front, front-left, and front-right cameras at 2048×512. AUGUstus
predicts 8 waypoints over a 4-second horizon for NAVSIM, and 6 waypoints over a 3-second horizon for
nuScenes. For NAVSIM, we report the official PDMS on NAVSIMv1 and EPDMS on NAVSIMv2. For
nuScenes, following the ST-P3 protocol (Hu et al., 2022), we report L2 error and collision rate.
In-house benchmarks and driving test. To validate the practicality of AUGUstus, we train it on in-house
dataset without using any public dataset, using 512 NVIDIA H800 GPUs for approximately two days
with a global batch size of 2048. We first evaluate it on in-house 1,000 km driving set covering safety,
comfort, efficiency, and compliance metrics, and then deploy the model on a real vehicle for a preliminary
driving test in Guangzhou, China.
Baselines. We use Qwen3.5-2B (Qwen Team, 2026) as the base model. On public datasets, we compare
against mainstream approaches, including end-to-end small planners and representative autoregressive,
regression-based, and diffusion-based VLA planners. On the in-house dataset, we compare AUGUstus
with diffusion-based and regression-based architectures. Note that, considering the real-time requirements
for deployment, unless otherwise specified, inference in this paper refers to 1-step denoising without the
subsequent CoT generation.

Main Results
Public benchmark results. As shown in Table 1, AUGUstus reaches 94.0/94.6 PDMS with 1/3-step
denoising on NAVSIMv1, only 0.2 below human performance (94.8), while only generating a single
trajectory without any scorer module. On NAVSIMv2, as shown in Table 2, AUGUstus obtains the SOTA

and 0.05% average collision rate.
In-house benchmarks and real driving test. Beyond public benchmarks, we evaluate VLM+flow matching,
VLM+regression, and AUGUstus on an internal 1,000 km driving dataset. We report macro-averages
across safety, comfort, efficiency, and compliance, where positive values indicate worse performance and
AUGUstus is fixed at 0%. AUGUstus performs best in safety, comfort, and efficiency, with the largest gaps
in safety and comfort; the flow-matching baseline is slightly better in compliance (Table 4). As shown in

5
Conclusion and Future Work
In this work, we propose AUGUstus, a deployable unified VLA that integrates diffusion-based action
generation and autoregressive CoT understanding in a single transformer, with AUGUstus-mode ordering
for low-latency inference and AUGUstus-GRPO for joint optimization. Experiments on NAVSIMv1/v2
and nuScenes show state-of-the-art performance: AUGUstus achieves 94.6 PDMS on NAVSIMv1 and
90.7 EPDMS on NAVSIMv2 without a scorer module, and on nuScenes it achieves an average L2 error of
0.27 m and an average collision rate of 0.05%, both the best among the compared methods. To validate
9


its practicality, we train it on our in-house dataset with a large-scale GPU cluster, then evaluate it on an
internal driving dataset and preliminarily deploy it on a real vehicle, achieving real-time operation. In
future work, we will conduct more extensive real-vehicle tests to further validate the architecture and
attempt to apply it to robot VLA.

Supplementary Architecture Details
A.1
Backbone and action projections
Action-module data path. Given a normalized noisy trajectory 𝑥𝑡∈R8×3 and flow time 𝑡∈[0, 1], the
module produces a clean trajectory estimate ˆ𝑥0 ∈R8×3 through three stages: an Action Projector, the
shared transformer action-token block, and an Action Decoder. The eight rows always correspond to the
eight future waypoints; all linear maps are shared across waypoint positions, while positional information
is carried by the backbone’s continued token positions.
Action Projector. In the left block of Fig. 8, each waypoint is first mapped from three coordinates
to the hidden width by a linear layer. In parallel, the scalar flow time is encoded with a sinusoidal
sine/cosine embedding. The two representations are concatenated row-wise and passed through a
Linear–SiLU–Linear MLP, yielding action-token embeddings 𝑒act ∈R8×𝐷. Thus the time condition is
available at the input of every action token without changing the dimensionality of the pretrained
language-vision stream.
Shared action-token block. The embeddings 𝑒act are inserted as an eight-token action block after the
multimodal context. In AUGUstus-mode, the action block reads the cached video, ego-state, route, and
language context and is bidirectional within itself; the subsequent causal CoT block may read the action
states, but action tokens cannot read CoT tokens. The action states therefore receive scene information
from the shared trunk while remaining independent of the optional autoregressive decoding path.
During training, this is also the route by which the CoT loss can shape the action representation; during
deployment, only the action block is evaluated.
Segmented input sequence and transformer layer. The center of Fig. 8 expands one forward pass
into its token segments: visual tokens 𝑉, input-language tokens 𝐿, noisy action tokens 𝐴, and causal
chain-of-thought tokens 𝐿. The action segment is a single bidirectional block, while the language and
CoT segments retain their autoregressive masks. Every decoder layer applies the same residual pattern,
AdaRMSNorm→Attention→Add→AdaRMSNorm→MLP→Add, to the shared hidden states. The
time condition is segment-specific: visual and input-language tokens use 𝑡=0, the noisy action tokens use
the sampled 𝑡∈[0, 1], and the CoT tokens again use 𝑡=0. This keeps the diffusion state from contaminating
the language stream while allowing every action layer to adapt to the denoising point.
Action Decoder. The right block of Fig. 8 maps the final action hidden states ℎact ∈R8×𝐷back to waypoint
coordinates. It applies LayerNorm, a linear projection, SiLU, a second LayerNorm, and a final linear
projection to three channels, producing ˆ𝑥0 ∈R8×3. The output is inverse-normalized to metric 𝑥, 𝑦, and
heading coordinates before being sent to the planner. At 𝑡= 1, the 𝑥0-parameterization makes this
decoder output the deployed one-step trajectory directly; for a multi-step ODE/SDE rollout, the output is
re-embedded as the next noisy action state and the same shared module is applied again. No separate
action transformer or scorer is introduced.

C.1
CoT content and supervision formats
C.1.1
What each CoT teaches
Our CoT ablation is a comparison of three alternative supervision targets, not a three-stage pipeline: every
variant is trained on the identical scene and action, and only the content of the single CoT block differs.
We take the three task-oriented CoT definitions from AutoDrive-P3 (Ye et al., 2026b) – Perception CoT (the
2-D boxes of relevant agents), Prediction CoT (each box plus a discretized future-position label, such as
near-front or far-left), and Planning CoT (textual ego waypoints or maneuvers) – and ask which one makes
the action head plan best, since only one of them is emitted per sample. We do not chain them, and the
model never consumes its own perception output to produce a prediction or a plan; the three streams are
competing ways of describing the same scene, and Table 9 reports the outcome (prediction CoT wins).
A fourth variant, reasoning CoT, concatenates the three signals into a compact approximately 100-token
15


explanation: it first names the relevant agents, then states their future bins, and finally gives the resulting
ego maneuver. This is intentionally shorter and more structured than unconstrained free-form reasoning.

Prediction (CoT) reward. Predicted boxes are greedily matched to ground-truth boxes by descending IoU

D.2.1
SFT Training Detail
We adopt Qwen3.5-2B backbone network. The action sequence consists of 8 action tokens with hidden
dimension 𝐻=2048. The ActionEncoder is a 3-layer SiLU MLP with concatenated sinusoidal time
embedding 𝑡. The ActionDecoder follows the structure LN →Lin →SiLU →LN →Lin(3) and uses
a near-zero final initialization. Each layer applies per-norm modulation projections that map 𝐻to
2𝐻, corresponding to a DiT variant with approximately 8M additional parameters. We apply quantile
normalization with (𝑞01, 𝑞99) per action dimension. Training uses DeepSpeed ZeRO-2 with bf16 precision.
The loss weights are 𝜆diff=10 and 𝜆lm=1. The main model uses dimension weights (1, 1, 1).
For every training example we sample a flow time 𝑡and noise 𝜀, construct 𝑥𝑡= (1 −𝑡)𝑥0 + 𝑡𝜀, and run one

D.3
Complete Training and Inference Algorithms
Algorithms 5 and 6 give the full cold-start and deployment procedures corresponding to the contrasts
in Algorithms 1 and 2. Both use the same AUGUstus-mode attention (action before CoT): context is
causal, actions are bidirectional, and causal CoT tokens can read context and actions but not vice versa.
Training uses noisy ground-truth actions and teacher-forced prediction CoT; deployment uses initial
Gaussian noise and deterministic action denoising, with no ground-truth action or CoT required. Action
normalization, time conditioning, and auxiliary module losses follow Appendix A and Appendix D.2.

D.6
Deployment Complexity
Latency. The deployed path is a causal visual prefix followed by one bidirectional eight-token action suffix.
The vision encoder remains eager because the multimodal preprocessing contains host synchronization;
the prefix and denoising suffix are captured in one CUDA graph. Table 10 reports the steady-state
batch-one measurements from the profiling script in fast_inference.md.
The eager profile launches about 3.7k small kernels and performs roughly 200 pageable host-to-device
copies per sample. Graph replay removes this CPU dispatch overhead and reduces the LLM stage from
91.83 to 33.19 ms (2.77×), while leaving the visual stage unchanged. The action suffix contributes only
eight tokens on top of a roughly 2,257-token context. Optional post-hoc CoT is therefore deliberately
disabled in the speed-critical configuration: a measured 166-token explanation takes about 4.5 s at batch
one, whereas the action-only path is below 100 ms.
