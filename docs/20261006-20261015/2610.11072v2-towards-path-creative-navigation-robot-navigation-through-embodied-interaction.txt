Abstract— Autonomous navigation in cluttered and con-
strained environments typically assumes a fixed environment
and searches only for paths within existing free space. However,
a route can be blocked by an articulated structure, a movable
object, or a pedestrian, and reaching the goal may therefore
require appropriate embodied interaction with the environment.
This paper formulates Path-Creative Navigation (PCN), a
navigation paradigm in which the robot recovers free space
by coordinating locomotion and embodied interaction, and
addresses one class of PCN tasks with a mapless framework. The
vision-LiDAR-odometry framework integrates local observations,
obstacle geometry, and robot-state estimates through a unified
navigation system, providing stable semantic and geometric
information for navigation and interaction. The traversability-
aware decision method utilizes visual and LiDAR distance
information to determine whether a blockage is actionable
and whether it can be safely bypassed, enabling the robot
to interact only when necessary while supporting articulated
structure pushing, movable object pushing, obstacle avoidance,

I. INTRODUCTION
Autonomous navigation enables robots to plan routes and
avoid obstacles in cluttered and constrained environments.
However, most navigation systems assume a fixed environ-
ment and search only for paths within existing free space. In
homes, offices, or warehouses, a route may instead be blocked
by an articulated structure, a movable object, or a pedestrian
in a narrow passage. In such cases, reaching the goal may
require changing the environment rather than searching for
another collision-free path. The key challenge is therefore
not only how to interact with the environment, but when
interaction is actually necessary for navigation.
This challenge presents three requirements, particularly in
cluttered and constrained environments where free space is
limited. First, the robot must determine whether an object
truly blocks the route or can be safely bypassed. Interacting
arXiv:2610.11072v2  [cs.RO]  9 Oct 2026


with every detected object is inefficient and unsafe, whereas
treating all obstacles as immutable may leave a recoverable
route blocked. Second, potential blockers impose different
constraints: fixed obstacles require avoidance, movable objects
may be pushed, articulated doors must be operated according
to their constrained motion, and pedestrians may require a
non-contact request. Third, interaction must remain tightly
coupled with the navigation objective as the scene changes.
After interacting with a blocker, the robot must reassess
whether the passage is traversable and resume navigation
when possible. Consequently, the task requires geometric
reasoning about route blockage and bypass feasibility, se-
mantic reasoning about potentially actionable objects, and
closed-loop interaction as the scene changes.
Existing approaches address individual aspects of this
challenge but may not fully satisfy the three requirements.
Conventional navigation cannot recover a route that requires
an environmental change. Navigation among movable obsta-
cles (NAMO) [1] allows a robot to move obstacles and clear
free space, while interactive navigation [2] permits physical
interaction to support goal reaching. These methods focus on
movable-object interaction or predefined manipulation tasks.
Map-based methods [3], [4] rely on pre-built maps for global
planning, but maps provide incomplete path information
when traversable regions are not explicitly represented or
are only partially observable. LLM-based methods [5], [6]
use semantic reasoning to determine whether and how to
interact with objects and plan feasible routes, but LLM-based
reasoning adds communication latency and computational
overhead. Task-and-Motion Planning (TAMP) [7] integrates
navigation and manipulation through explicit action sequences,
typically involving higher-level task planning. In parallel,
humanoid loco-manipulation methods [8], [9] provide the
physical skills required for door opening and object pushing.
This paper focuses on the complementary navigation-level
problem of determining when interaction is required to recover
free space and continue toward the goal.
To address this problem, we formulate Path-Creative
Navigation (PCN), which explicitly treats the restoration of
traversability as part of the navigation problem itself, and
jointly reasons about whether the robot should navigate within
the current free space or change the environment to make a
route traversable. PCN covers a broad range of tasks, and
this paper addresses one class of them, in which the blockage
comes from a movable object, an articulated structure, or a
pedestrian. For this class, we present a mapless humanoid
navigation framework that integrates vision, LiDAR, and
odometry. Visual perception provides object semantics and
distance estimates, while LiDAR and odometry provide local
geometry and robot-pose information. A traversability-aware
decision method jointly reasons about object semantics, route
blockage, and bypass feasibility. The decision uses only local
observations, without a pre-built map or a language model
in the loop.
In summary, the main contributions of this paper are as
follows:
• We formulate Path-Creative Navigation (PCN), a nav-
igation paradigm that reasons over locomotion and
actionable embodied interaction to recover free space
across different types of route blockages.
• We propose a traversability-aware decision method that
considers local observations to determine whether a
blockage is actionable and whether it can be safely
bypassed, triggering interaction only when necessary to
restore route traversability.
• We design a vision-LiDAR-odometry fusion framework
that combines object semantics, local obstacle geometry,
and robot-state estimates within a unified navigation
system, supporting articulated structure pushing, movable
object pushing, pedestrian request, and navigation toward
the goal.
• We evaluate our framework in both simulation and real-
world experiments, demonstrating higher task completion
and more reliable interaction selection while coordinating
navigation and interaction.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Robot Mobile Manipulation
Navigation Among Movable Obstacles (NAMO) relaxes
the assumption that the environment remains fixed during
navigation. Affordance-based NAMO [10] estimates whether
an unknown obstacle can be moved and uses contact-aware
planning to complete an otherwise infeasible navigation
task. Social concerns also apply when a robot changes the
environment. Socially aware NAMO [11] adds a placement
cost so that a displaced object is less likely to block a corridor,
doorway, or other space needed by people. These methods
mainly reason about which objects should be moved and
where they should be placed. In contrast, our framework
does not perform global planning or optimize final object
placements; it uses local semantic and geometric observations
around the robot-to-goal corridor to assess local traversability
and coordinate predefined responses to doors, movable objects,
fixed obstacles, and pedestrians within a unified humanoid
navigation system.
Interactive navigation permits physical interaction to sup-
port goal reaching. Recent methods mainly improve the
decision and control required for path clearing. Yang et al.
[12] use hierarchical policy learning to estimate obstacle
properties and generate pushing actions for a mobile ma-
nipulator. Dai et al. [3] combine adaptive object-dynamics
learning with non-prehensile mobile manipulation. For legged
manipulators, Bi et al. [4] learn an arm-pushing controller
that moves large obstacles in narrow spaces. Manipulate-to-
Navigate [13] combines visual affordances and manipulability
priors to learn feasible end-effector poses for Reach and
Door tasks. These methods show that interaction can make
a blocked route feasible. However, most of these learning-
based approaches train policies or object models for specific
interaction tasks and settings. Our framework applies to new
visual instances within supported categories, such as doors
and boxes with different colors and appearances, without
fine-tuning or retraining.


Task-and-Motion Planning (TAMP) connects discrete action
choices with continuous geometric and kinematic feasibility.
A major challenge is the large search space of long-horizon
tasks. VLM-TAMP [14] addresses this problem by using
a vision-language model to generate intermediate subgoals,
which divide a long manipulation task into shorter TAMP
problems. When a subgoal cannot be refined into a feasible
motion, the system queries the model again and replans.
Sung et al. [15] improve long-horizon TAMP through learned
backjumping heuristics. Unlike general TAMP, our framework
does not search for or revise a long-horizon sequence of task
and motion actions; it makes local traversability decisions.
B. Robot Loco-manipulation
Loco-manipulation provides the physical skills needed to
execute such interactions. Recent work improves whole-body
safety for mobile manipulators in cluttered environments
[16]. DemoHLM [8] generates diverse synthetic trajectories
from one simulation demonstration for each task and uses
them to train high-level manipulation policies on top of
a universal whole-body controller. ULTRA [9] combines
physics-driven motion retargeting with a unified controller
that accepts either dense motion references or sparse task
goals. Our framework performs local observations reasoning
from semantic and geometric information around the robot-to-
goal corridor, coordinating predefined responses to different
blocker types.
Door opening is a related mobility skill, but a door is
an articulated structure rather than a freely movable rigid
object. Its motion is constrained by a hinge, and the robot
must coordinate the arm, body, and door state. Jang et al.
[17] formulate approaching, opening, traversing, and closing
as a coupled planning problem for a mobile manipulator.
Zhang et al. [18] learn a policy that opens and traverses both
push and pull doors with a legged manipulator. These works
demonstrate that changing the state of an articulated structure
may be necessary for mobility. In our work, however, opening
the door is not the task goal itself. The action is selected
only when the current door state blocks goal reaching and
opening it can create sufficient free space.

Consider a mobile embodied robot operating in a potentially
changeable environment Et at time step t. The robot has an

Interacting with blocking elements is enough to make the

IV. METHODOLOGY
A. Overview of Our Framework
We instantiate the class of PCN tasks defined in Sec-
tion III with two modules. The vision-LiDAR-odometry
fusion framework provides stable semantic targets and local
geometric information. The traversability-aware decision
method determines whether the robot should continue normal
navigation, avoid an object, or interact with an actionable
object. The whole framework is shown in Fig. 2.
B. Vision-LiDAR-Odometry Fusion
The fusion framework combines visual semantics and
depth with LiDAR geometry and odometry. Vision and depth
provide semantic object targets in the odometry frame, while
LiDAR and odometry provide local geometric information
for navigation and interaction.

C. Traversability-Aware Decision Method

whether a detected object should be handled by navigation
or embodied interaction. The decision jointly considers the
object’s semantic category, its relation to the task corridor, and
the available LiDAR clearance. Fixed obstacles are handled
by navigation and obstacle avoidance, whereas movable
objects and articulated structures are considered for interaction
only when they block the active route and cannot be safely
bypassed.

D. Embodied Interaction Execution
When NeedInteract(ot,i) = 1, the selected object is used
as the interaction target ot, and an admissible interaction
mode mt ∈M(ot) is executed through aint
t
= (ot, mt). The
execution strategy depends on the semantic category of the
target. Fixed obstacles remain handled by navigation and
obstacle avoidance.
• Physical interaction: For movable objects and articu-
lated structures, the robot first approaches the selected
target and aligns its body with the interaction direction.
It then moves its arms to the required pose and executes
the corresponding physical interaction. gt provides
closed-loop feedback for approach, body alignment,
speed adjustment, and interaction completion. Once the
passage becomes traversable, the robot retracts its arms
and resumes navigation. If the interaction cannot be
completed safely, the robot moves away from the target
and repeats the approach and alignment procedure.
• Pedestrian request: Pedestrians, treated as a special
class of movable objects, are handled through non-
contact interaction. When a pedestrian blocks the ac-
tive route and satisfies NeedInteract(ot,i) = 1, the
robot stops and issues a verbal request to clear the
passage. Navigation resumes once the passage becomes
traversable.

We compare our proposed method against four methods
representing different interaction-selection strategies. DRL-
DCLP [20] is the navigation-only baseline: it avoids fixed
obstacles but cannot modify the environment when the
route is blocked. Always-Interact invokes the corresponding
interaction for every detected actionable object, regardless
of whether the object blocks the route or can be bypassed.
Proximity-Trigger initiates an interaction whenever a detected
actionable object enters a predefined distance threshold,
without explicitly evaluating local bypass feasibility. Oracle-
Selective uses the Simulator Ground Truth (GT) to identify
the objects whose states must be changed to reach the goal.
All methods use the embodied interaction introduced in this
work for door opening and movable-object pushing. Ours
w/o LiDAR removes LiDAR-based geometric information to
evaluate its contribution to decision and interaction, while
Ours w/o NeedInteract removes the traversability-aware
decision module and selects any actionable object detected
in front of the robot. All methods use the same robot, initial
state, goal, navigation policy, and evaluation budget. Each
method is evaluated twice in every scenario, resulting in 140
trials per method.
In this experiment, SPL measures path efficiency. A
selection is a positive decision on a detected target, and
an initiation is a selection that enters the interaction stage,
regardless of eventual success. Recall is the fraction of
required targets selected at least once, and MIR is the fraction
never initiated. Precision is the fraction of selections on
required and not previously selected targets, and F1 score is
the harmonic mean of precision and recall. UIR measures
initiations involving non-required or previously operated
targets divided by all initiations. Recovery retries count
once per selection. Mean Time is the mean total time of
successful trials. Required targets are identified from scene
ground truth provided by the simulator, and Oracle-Selective
is reported separately as a ground truth reference. Required
interactions are identified from scene ground truth provided
by the simulator. Oracle-Selective is reported separately as
a ground truth reference. Additionally, we visualize three
experimental trajectories in Fig. 4.

B. Real-World Experiments Setup and Results Analysis
We further conduct real-world experiments in cluttered
and constrained environments. Our experimental platform
consists of a Unitree G1 humanoid robot equipped with a
Livox Mid-360 LiDAR, an Intel RealSense D435i depth
camera, and an NVIDIA Jetson Xavier NX for onboard
computation. Object detection runs at 15 Hz, and planning
runs at 10 Hz. To increase the difficulty beyond simulation, we
construct cluttered scenes in narrow corridors with limited free
space. The environments contain previously unseen obstacles,
including road barriers, suitcases, and chairs, with some
objects placed close to the corridor walls. Consequently,
unnecessary or incorrect interactions can easily block the
remaining free space, cause the robot to become stuck, or
cause a collision, requiring intervention. We evaluate two
real-world tasks, with 10 trials for each task:


• Manipulation task: we construct a multi-stage environ-
ment that connects two areas. The robot first pushes
open a closed door that blocks access to the next area.
It then continues navigation, avoids static obstacles, and
performs the required interaction tasks. This setting
evaluates whether the robot can maintain goal-directed
behavior across sequential navigation and manipulation
steps.
• Social-interaction task: a pedestrian blocks the path in
front of the robot. The robot stops at a safe distance and
provides a verbal prompt that asks the pedestrian to clear
the passage. The robot resumes navigation only after
the passage becomes available. This setting additionally
evaluates whether the robot can restore access through
non-contact interaction with a pedestrian.
Key parameters are: Ns = 3, η = 0.35, rr = 0.35m,
mc = 0.25m, cth = 0.85m, and the navigation velocity is
0.5m/s. The real-world experiments are illustrated in Fig. 1,
and representative interaction executions are shown in Fig. 6.
As reported in Table II, PCN achieves success rates of 80%
and 70% in the manipulation and social-interaction tasks,
respectively, whereas DRL-DCLP and Always-Interact fail to
complete either task. Proximity-Trigger achieves only 20%
and 10% success rates. Its proximity-based interaction strategy
is unreliable in narrow corridors, where actionable objects
may lie close to the walls without blocking the route, and
unnecessary interactions can reduce the remaining free space,
cause the robot to become stuck, or result in a collision.
In contrast, PCN reduces the intervention rates to 20%
and 30% by explicitly reasoning about route blockage and
bypass feasibility. These results demonstrate the importance of
selectively triggering interaction for navigation in constrained
real-world environments.
VI. CONCLUSIONS
This paper formulates Path-Creative Navigation (PCN),
a navigation paradigm that restores traversability through
embodied interaction. We instantiate PCN with a mapless
vision-LiDAR-odometry framework and a traversability-aware
decision method that determines whether a blockage is action-
able and bypassable. The framework coordinates navigation,
obstacle avoidance, articulated-structure interaction, object
pushing, and pedestrian requests while maintaining progress
toward the goal. Simulation and real-world experiments show
higher task completion than baseline methods, demonstrating
that navigation can recover feasible routes by deciding when
environmental interaction is necessary. In future work, we
plan to integrate whole-body control and extend PCN to
support more challenging navigation and manipulation tasks
in complex environments.
