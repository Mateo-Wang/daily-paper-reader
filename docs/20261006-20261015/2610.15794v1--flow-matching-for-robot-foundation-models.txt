∆Flow Matching for Robot Foundation Models

Flow matching has emerged as a common generative recipe for current robot foundation models.
Vision-language-action models (VLAs) generate action chunks, while world-action models (WAMs)
additionally generate future visual states. Yet both start generation from a fixed Gaussian prior,
despite the strong temporal continuity between past and future states and actions. This process is
costly to train and requires iterative sampling at deployment, especially for WAMs with an additional
visual branch. Exploiting this temporal continuity, we introduce ∆-flow, which constructs the source
from recent history with mild noise rather than from a fixed Gaussian prior. This moves the source
closer to the target, reducing the transport to a small residual ∆that can be learned efficiently and
enables single-step inference. We integrate ∆-flow into the VLA policy π0.5 and the WAM policy
including ImageWAM and Fast-WAM. On LIBERO, ImageWAM reaches 90% success in one epoch
rather than six, and Fast-WAM reaches 95% in two epochs rather than eight. With LoRA fine-tuning,
π0.5 exceeds its baseline’s five-epoch performance after one epoch. Single-step inference gives 1.54×,
2.49×, and 4.45× speedups on π0.5, ImageWAM, and Fast-WAM, respectively. It also preserves
action smoothness, achieving a 4.5× reduction in action jerk compared with one-step Gaussian
sampling. ∆-flow reaches state-of-the-art generalization performance on LIBERO-Plus. Remarkably,
action-history sources can also be applied to pretrained WAM policies without retraining. Replacing
the Gaussian action source at inference largely preserves task success with a single sampling step and
can slightly improve it. We further study how action representation and the use of visual information
in action learning affect history-to-future prediction in ∆-flow. Together, these results show that
∆-flow is simple yet effective, substantially improving the efficiency and generalization of current robot
foundation models.

Introduction
Flow matching has become a standard recipe for continuous action generation in robot policies (Lipman et al.,
2023). In vision-language-action models (VLAs), an action chunk is produced by transporting a sample from
a Gaussian prior to the action distribution, conditioned on the current observation (Black et al., 2025b,a).
More recently, world-action models (WAMs) extend this generative formulation beyond actions to future
visual representations, jointly modeling how the scene evolves and how the robot acts (Ye et al., 2026; Yuan
et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026a; Hou et al., 2026). In both families, generation begins from Gaussian noise.
However, this Gaussian-source formulation is expensive, both to train and to deploy (Hou et al., 2026; Dong
et al., 2026). Even for action-only VLAs, flow-based action generation converges slowly during training (Black
et al., 2025a), and at deployment it requires a large number of function evaluations (NFE) (i.e., inference
steps), which directly increases control latency. WAMs inherit both costs and amplify them. During training,
WAMs learn transports from pure Gaussian noise to both future actions and high-dimensional future visual
states. At deployment, WAMs that jointly sample visual states and actions require computation for both
modalities at each sampling step. Together, these training and inference costs pose challenges for deploying
flow-based policies on real robots.
Most existing work targets the latter, reducing inference cost along three lines. Efficient samplers shorten the
sampling trajectory or distill iterative generation into a single prediction (Lu et al., 2022; Song et al., 2023;

they remain effective on the visual branch of WAMs is still an open question. Some warm-start methods
instead use separately trained predictors to provide informative initializations, followed by a few sampling
steps for refinement (Li et al., 2026a; Scholz and Turner, 2025). Other models like Fast-WAM (Yuan et al.,
2026) and ImageWAM (Zhang et al., 2026a) retain future prediction as a training objective but skip it at
inference, so that its knowledge reaches the action branch only implicitly through the shared representation.
These models thus trade explicit future conditioning for inference speed. Our experiments show that removing
explicit future prediction at inference degrades performance under distribution shift. More fundamentally,
these methods shorten, approximate, or partially bypass the generative transport to reduce inference cost,
rather than reducing the cost of learning the transport itself.
Flow matching, in essence, learns a transport between two distributions (Lipman et al., 2023; Jia et al., 2026a;
Bai et al., 2026). Since both actions and visual signals are naturally continuous, and their executed histories
are directly available from feedback measurements, why not take these histories as the source of the flow? Using
history as the source can simplify the mapping learned during training and shorten the transport path at
inference. Several works in image and video generation indeed construct their sources from existing images or
adjacent frames and learn transports to target images or future frames (Liu et al., 2023b; Davtyan et al., 2023;
Liu and Ritschel, 2025). In robot control, action-to-action (A2A) flow matching uses histories of executed
actions as the source distribution and learns a transport to future action chunks (Jia et al., 2026b). Each
line, however, treats a single modality in isolation. Under the WAM paradigm, and especially at the scale of
growing video and robot data, jointly training these two mechanisms, and identifying the design choices that
make them work together, remains an open question.
In this work, we propose ∆-flow matching for robot foundation models, termed ∆-flow, where the symbol ∆
denotes the difference between the history and the future of a visual state or action. Rather than generating the
future from fixed Gaussian noise, ∆-flow directly learns how the recent visual and action history evolves into
its future, leaving the flow networks responsible only for residuals. In VLAs, this replaces the Gaussian source
of the action branch; in WAMs, it replaces the source of both branches. The future visual representations and
actions of WAMs are modeled jointly, with action generation conditioned on the predicted visual future, and
both produced in a single flow step at inference. This design preserves both action continuity and state–action
coupling, which are identified as important factors for successful trajectories (Yang et al., 2026). Because
∆-flow alters only the source of the flow, it integrates naturally into existing policies, including WAMs such as

ImageWAM (Zhang et al., 2026a) and Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026) and flow-based VLAs such as π0.5 (Black
et al., 2025a), without network architectural modification or extra data.
Figure 1 summarizes the formulation and its training and inference benefits. Experiments show that ∆-flow
is simple yet effective. We evaluate ∆-flow across robot foundation policies on LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023a)
and LIBERO-Plus (Fei et al., 2026). During training on LIBERO, ImageWAM reaches 90% success in
one rather than six epochs, while Fast-WAM reaches 95% in two rather than eight. On π0.5 with LoRA
fine-tuning, one epoch exceeds the baseline’s five-epoch success, giving a 5× reduction in training epochs to
that performance level. At inference, ∆-flow accelerates all three backbones through single-step generation. On
Fast-WAM, it jointly predicts future visual representations and actions 4.45× faster than ten-step Gaussian
sparse-joint inference while improving LIBERO-Plus success. ∆-flow also maintains smooth actions at
one step: on ImageWAM, it achieves a 4.5× reduction in action jerk compared with one-step Gaussian
sampling, with smoothness comparable to ten-step Gaussian sampling. On LIBERO-Plus, it further achieves
state-of-the-art performance without large robot training data, reaching 88.55% success across the seven
perturbation categories. Most strikingly, replacing the Gaussian action source with recent executed actions
enables single-step sampling in pretrained ImageWAM and Fast-WAM without retraining. This adaptation
largely preserves task success and can slightly improve it.
Beyond the main results, we systematically examine how ∆-based flows interact with existing policy and
WAM designs, and several findings are counterintuitive. First, action-space parameterization barely changes
standard LIBERO success but shifts LIBERO-Plus success by over 14 points, so parameterizations that appear
equivalent in distribution can differ sharply under perturbation (Figure 10). Second, predicting fewer future
frames over the same horizon improves control, gaining 5.19 points on LIBERO-Plus over a denser schedule
(Figure 7). Third, as shown in Figure 8, stop-gradient improves future-image PSNR and LIBERO-Plus success.
The action branch still reads visual features, but its gradients do not update the world model. These results
indicate that feature sharing and gradient coupling should be treated as separate design decisions. Finally,
once the source is past-informed and visual supervision is retained, supervising only the one-step endpoint of
the action branch nearly matches the full flow-matching objective, albeit with slower convergence (Figure 9).
We believe these findings are useful beyond the specific setting studied here.

Related Work
2.1
Robot foundation models
2.1.1
Vision-language-action model
VLAs map visual observations and language instructions to robot actions (Zitkovich et al., 2023; Kim et al.,
2025). π0 (Black et al., 2025b) introduces a flow-matching action expert for continuous action generation,
while π0.5 (Black et al., 2025a) further improves open-world generalization through heterogeneous co-training.
FAST (Pertsch et al., 2025) compresses action sequences to reduce autoregressive decoding steps, while
FLOWER (Reuss et al., 2025) reduces action-expert parameters by sharing adaptive normalization across
transformer layers. In flow-based VLA policies, generation starts from Gaussian noise and iteratively transports
it into a future action chunk conditioned on the observation (Black et al., 2025b). Such policies can be costly
to train, and repeated action-expert evaluations increase inference latency (Pertsch et al., 2025; Black et al.,
2025b). Our work instead constructs the source from recently executed actions and learns their transformation
into future action chunks.
2.1.2
World-action models
WAMs couple predictive world modeling with robot action generation. Joint WAMs generate future visual
representations and actions together (Ye et al., 2026; An et al., 2026), while causal WAMs condition action
prediction on predicted future visual states (Li et al., 2026b). To reduce inference costs, Fast-WAM (Yuan
et al., 2026) retains video prediction during training but generates actions from current visual representations
at test time. ImageWAM (Zhang et al., 2026a) replaces video generation with image editing and conditions
action generation on current-image representations. These methods mainly change how visual information
supports action generation. However, both visual and action generation still start from Gaussian noise. Our
3


work instead introduces a unified history-based source formulation for visual and action generation, learning
past-to-future changes in both modalities.
2.2
Past-to-future generative modeling
A few early efforts have begun to start generation from the past. In visual generation, several methods
construct more informative sources from existing images. For example, I2SB (Liu et al., 2023b) learns a
Schrödinger bridge from a source image distribution to a target image distribution, grounding image translation
directly in the input image. Related video generation methods (Davtyan et al., 2023; Liu and Ritschel, 2025)
exploit relationships between adjacent frames to learn the generation of future frames from observed ones.
In robot control, action-to-action (A2A) flow matching (Jia et al., 2026b) uses executed action histories as
the source distribution and learns their transport to future action chunks. Warm-start methods (Chen et al.,
2024; Li et al., 2026a) instead use auxiliary policies or predictors to provide action initializations, followed by
a small number of refinement steps. These studies explore informed sources separately for visual and action
generation, but do not examine how both can be coordinated within the WAMs. Our work unifies past visual
states and actions as generative sources, providing a common past-to-future formulation for future world and
action generation.
Relation to our prior A2A policy. ∆-flow builds on our earlier A2A flow matching (Jia et al., 2026b), which
transports executed action histories to future action chunks, and extends it in four respects. (1) A2A defines
the transport in a learned latent space, whereas ∆-flow operates directly in the action space, so it can be
dropped into an existing policy without an additional encoder or decoder. (2) A2A establishes the idea for
actions only; here we show that the same difference-based source is equally effective for visual generation, and
that the two branches can be coupled within a single WAM. (3) A2A was evaluated on task-specific visuomotor
policies, whereas we test the formulation at the scale of robot foundation models, covering both flow-based
VLAs and WAMs. (4) We report a set of empirical studies absent from A2A, on action representation,
world-action coupling, and knowledge insulation.

5
Evaluation
We evaluate how learning past-to-future changes affects training efficiency, generalization, and inference in
robot foundation policies. We also test whether action-history sources can benefit pretrained WAM policies
without updating their weights. Our evaluation addresses four questions: (1) Does ∆-flow converge faster
than its Gaussian-source counterpart? (2) How does ∆-flow affect generalization under distribution shift?
(3) Can ∆-flow reduce inference latency while maintaining action continuity? (4) Can action-history source

5.2
Training Efficiency
We first compare LIBERO success throughout training with the corresponding Gaussian-source references.
As shown in the top panels of Figure 3, complete ∆-flow on ImageWAM reaches 91.30% after one epoch,
compared with 24.60% for the original model, a gain of 66.70 percentage points. It first exceeds 90% at
epoch one, whereas the original first exceeds this threshold at the evaluated epoch-six checkpoint, giving a 6×
reduction in training epochs to this level. Action-only ∆-flow reaches 90% by epoch four. On Fast-WAM,
∆-flow reaches 72.60% after one epoch, compared with 42.55% for the Gaussian sparse-joint baseline. It
reaches 95.65% by epoch two, a level the baseline first exceeds at epoch eight, giving a 4× reduction in training
epochs. Moreover, applying ∆-flow to actions alone already recovers most of this gain, and adding the visual
source further accelerates learning. With the demonstration set held fixed, training progress is measured in
epochs or cumulative samples processed.
As shown in the top-right panel of Figure 3, action ∆-flow on π0.5 with full-parameter fine-tuning reaches at
least 95% LIBERO success by epoch two and peaks at 99.30%. All ∆-flow models use a single Euler update
at inference. The left panel of Figure 4 shows that, with LoRA fine-tuning (Hu et al., 2022), action ∆-flow
reaches 92.20% after one epoch, 95.50% after two, and 98.00% after five. Its first-epoch result already exceeds
the ten-step baseline’s 91.30% after five epochs, giving a 5× reduction in training epochs to exceed that
performance level.

5.3
Generalization under Distribution Shift
We next evaluate generalization on both WAM and VLA policies using LIBERO-Plus without additional
fine-tuning. Table 1 summarizes their performance on LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus. On ImageWAM, ∆-flow
with a 50% image-latent prior increases LIBERO-Plus success from 82.49% to 88.55%, while maintaining
similar LIBERO performance (97.80% versus 98.00%). ImageWAM with ∆-flow achieves state-of-the-art
performance on LIBERO-Plus, despite using no additional embodied-data pretraining. Fast-WAM-joint with
∆-flow reaches 80.58% on LIBERO-Plus, compared with 77.51% for the Gaussian sparse-joint reference
with the same architecture and one-step sampling budget. On the VLA policy π0.5, action ∆-flow increases
LIBERO-Plus success from 80.70% to 84.53% and LIBERO success from 97.10% to 99.00%.
As shown in the bottom panels of Figure 3, these generalization gains are already visible early in training. On
ImageWAM, ∆-flow reaches 75.36% LIBERO-Plus success after one epoch, compared with 17.12% for the
original model. On Fast-WAM, it reaches 68.55% after two epochs, compared with 47.81% for the Gaussian
sparse-joint baseline. By epoch four, its success reaches 77.81%, already exceeding the baseline’s final 77.51%.
The perturbation-wise results further show where these improvements are most pronounced. On ImageWAM,
the largest gains occur under changes in robot initialization and object layout, where success increases from
52.90% to 73.94% and from 81.31% to 88.39%, respectively. Gains also extend to language instructions and
visual appearance, including background textures and lighting. Improvements under sensor noise are smaller,
while camera-viewpoint performance remains largely unchanged. Taken together, these results show that the
benefits of ∆-flow extend beyond faster learning on LIBERO to stronger generalization under distribution
shift across both WAM and VLA policies. We attribute this generalization to two factors: the residual is
easier to fit than a full transport, and the executed history now enters as the flow source rather than as a state
token concatenated with the visual input, which removes the shortcut that such concatenation often invites.

Training-Free Source Adaptation
Lastly, we test action-history initialization on the original ImageWAM and Fast-WAM policies trained with
Gaussian sources. Both policies are frozen, and only their Gaussian action source is replaced at inference.
The adaptation is a special case of Eq. (3). We set αa = 1 and σa = 0.5, with ba restricting the noise to the
pose dimensions.
As shown in Table 2, replacing the action source at a single sampling step raises ImageWAM’s LIBERO
success from 97.50% to 98.10%. Fast-WAM improves from 95.55% to 96.15% under the same one-step budget.
On LIBERO-Plus, ImageWAM improves from 81.65% to 82.82%, while Fast-WAM decreases from 68.28% to
67.58%. The adaptation is also free at a fixed solver budget, since it adds no network evaluation. Latency
follows the complete local policy-call protocol on an A100-80GB GPU, consistent with Section 5.4 and
Appendix B. These results show that an action-history source transfers to a frozen WAM policy without
post-training, supporting one-step inference while largely preserving task success. It further indicates that
∆-flow can be combined with an already well-trained policy at little cost, yielding immediate inference gains.

Empirical Studies on Policy Design
Beyond the components of ∆-flow itself, four further design choices must be settled before a history-based
formulation can be deployed, and together they define the configuration used in our final models: (1) how
much future visual prediction control actually needs; (2) whether gradients should be isolated between the
world model and the action head; (3) whether the action branch still requires flow-matching supervision
once its source is past-informed; and (4) how the action representation affects generalization. Although we
settle them here for ∆-flow, the same questions arise in any policy that couples world modeling with action
generation, and we expect the comparisons below to be informative beyond this work.

Having examined how future visual prediction supports action generation, we next investigate whether

the visual-to-action connection, whereas stop-gradient blocks them while preserving visual conditioning.

rollouts per configuration. One-step predictions are compared with ground-truth images 16 control steps
ahead.
As shown in Figure 8, stop-gradient achieves higher future-image PSNR and higher overall LIBERO-Plus
success. With stop-gradient, the WAM visual branch learns future visual changes more accurately under
visual supervision. Action generation conditioned on its intermediate representations also achieves higher
overall success. These results suggest that allowing the visual branch to learn independently of action-loss
gradients can benefit both future visual prediction and action generation in WAMs.

7.3
Is Action Flow Matching Still Necessary?
We next examine whether endpoint consistency alone can supervise the action branch in ImageWAM with
the 80% image-latent prior. We remove action flow matching and increase the action endpoint-consistency
weight from 0.5 to 1.0, while keeping the visual objectives unchanged. Detailed training settings are provided
in Appendix D. As shown in the left and middle panels of Figure 9, endpoint-only training closely matches
the combined objective. It achieves 97.40% success on LIBERO and 87.30% on LIBERO-Plus, compared
with 97.70% and 87.37%, respectively. Endpoint consistency alone thus supports accurate one-step action
prediction without supervising velocities along the interpolation path.
Flow matching nevertheless improves early learning, as shown in the right panel of Figure 9. After one epoch,
the combined objective achieves 89.90% LIBERO success, compared with 78.10% for endpoint consistency
alone. By the second epoch, the two configurations achieve nearly identical success rates. Flow matching
therefore helps the action branch learn faster early on, while endpoint consistency alone nearly matches its
final one-step performance. We retain both objectives to combine faster early learning with accurate one-step
prediction.
7.4
How to Choose Action Representation?
The action representation defines the space in which ∆-flow learns history-to-future changes. Figure 10
compares three representations: 7D local delta with axis-angle rotations, 7D absolute with axis-angle rotations,
and 10D absolute with rotation-6D. The three control spaces are defined in Appendix E.
With axis-angle rotations, the local-delta representation achieves 98.40% success on LIBERO and 85.82% on
LIBERO-Plus, compared with 94.40% and 61.07% for the absolute representation. The difference is particularly
pronounced under camera-viewpoint, background-texture, and sensor-noise perturbations. Absolute actions,
however, perform better under robot-initial-state perturbations, indicating that the relative performance also
depends on the type of distribution shift.
Within the absolute-action configurations, rotation-6D increases success from 94.40% to 98.00% on LIBERO
and from 61.07% to 71.67% on LIBERO-Plus. It performs better in six of the seven perturbation categories,
with robot initial states as the exception. Although the 10D absolute representation approaches local-delta
performance on standard LIBERO, its LIBERO-Plus success remains lower, at 71.67% versus 85.82%. Overall,
local-delta actions achieve the highest aggregate success on both benchmarks, while rotation-6D performs
better than axis-angle within the absolute-action configurations. These results show that action representation
deserves consideration alongside source construction when learning history-to-future changes.

Conclusion
We revisited a design choice that flow-based robot policies take for granted: where generation begins. Visual
states and actions both change continuously, so the recent past is already close to the future a policy must
produce. Taking it as the flow source leaves only a small residual ∆to learn. ∆-flow builds such a source by
perturbing the history with mild noise. The change touches mainly the source, so it integrates into existing
backbones without new modules or extra data. ImageWAM reaches 90% LIBERO success in one epoch rather
than six, while Fast-WAM reaches 95% in two epochs rather than eight. Complete one-step inference gives
2.49× and 4.45× speedups over their ten-step Gaussian references. LIBERO-Plus success improves by 6.06
points on ImageWAM and 3.07 points over the Gaussian sparse-joint Fast-WAM reference. On π0.5, the
construction applies to actions alone. With LoRA fine-tuning, its first-epoch result exceeds the baseline’s
five-epoch result, and it reaches 98.00% on LIBERO after five epochs. The approach also preserves action
continuity at one step, and action-source replacement can improve a frozen Gaussian-trained WAM policy
without retraining. These results support history-based sources as a practical design choice across robot
foundation backbones.

Future Work
The noise scale σm is a hyperparameter, although in practice it requires no tuning: all three backbones use
the same action-source noise scale. Still, the history is not equally reliable throughout a trajectory (Jia et al.,
2026a). Adapting σm per sample or per timestep, from the observation, from how fast the scene is changing,
or learned together with the vector field, is a promising extension. Validating ∆-flow on more VLA backbones
and on physical robots also remains future work.

7D local delta actions.
The action contains three position increments, three axis–angle rotation increments,
and one gripper command. The position and rotation increments describe how the end effector should move
from its current pose.
7D absolute actions.
The action contains three target-position values, three axis–angle values for the target
orientation, and one gripper command. Instead of predicting a pose change, the policy directly specifies the
end-effector pose to reach.
10D absolute actions with rotation-6D.
This action specifies the same type of target pose, but uses six values
to represent orientation. It contains three target-position values, six rotation values, and one gripper command.
The six rotation values encode the first two columns of the target rotation matrix. Before execution, they are
converted into a valid rotation matrix by orthonormalization (Zhou et al., 2019), then into the absolute pose
command accepted by the controller.
