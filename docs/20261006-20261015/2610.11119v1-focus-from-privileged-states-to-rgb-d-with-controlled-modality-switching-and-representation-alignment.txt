Abstract:
Vision-based reinforcement learning for robotic manipulation is
sample-inefficient because RGB-D observations are high-dimensional and noisy.
Privileged state information available in simulation can accelerate training, but
its absence at test time creates a train–test modality gap. We propose FOCUS, a
single-stage PPO framework that trains the critic on privileged state while auto-
matically regulating whether the actor collects rollouts from RGB-D or privileged-
state latents. Regulation is driven by the KL divergence between the action distri-
butions induced by the two modalities, while representation alignment encourages
consistent action selection across them. Together, these mechanisms limit RGB-D
rollouts when the actor’s action distributions from RGB-D and privileged-state la-
tents disagree. As they align, RGB-D exposure increases, shifting on-policy train-
ing toward the RGB-D inputs used at test time. Across five manipulation tasks,
FOCUS raises average test success from 0.71 to 0.93 relative to the strongest
RGB-D-at-test baseline on each task. When accounting for each method’s com-
plete training pipeline, budget-normalized training-success AUC increases from
0.47 to 0.65. On Pick-and-Place, test success rises from 0.47 to 0.86, while AUC
increases from 0.12 to 0.61, a 5.0× improvement in learning efficiency over the
fixed interaction budget.
Keywords: Vision-Based Reinforcement Learning, Learning Representations for
Robotic Perception and Control, Robotic Manipulation

Introduction
Reinforcement learning enables robots to learn closed-loop manipulation policies through trial-and-
error interaction and optimize them directly for task success. However, reinforcement learning for
robotics remains limited by poor sample efficiency, sensitivity to reward design, and the cost and
safety risks of real-world trial-and-error data collection [1]. Simulation-based RL mitigates some of
these issues through resettable environments, parallel rollouts, controlled task variation, and access
to training-only privileged information.
Learning manipulation policies directly from RGB-D observations is difficult because visual inputs
are high-dimensional and noisy [2, 3, 4]. In some settings, visual observations may also provide
an incomplete view of task-relevant state [5, 2]. In simulation, however, training can access com-
pact privileged state information, such as object and gripper poses, contact signals, and task-goal
variables, that helps determine which action should be taken [6]. This creates an opportunity to use

privileged state during RL training to improve sample efficiency while still producing an RGB-D
policy that does not require privileged state at test time.
The difficulty is addressing the modality gap between privileged-state training and RGB-D-based
action selection at test time. While privileged states can accelerate policy learning, reliance on
them during training can produce representations and action decisions that are poorly matched to
the RGB-D observations available at test time. Conversely, training only from RGB-D avoids this
mismatch but can be sample-inefficient. The problem we address is therefore how to adapt the actor
from privileged-state rollouts toward RGB-D control during RL training while avoiding premature
RGB-D exposure.
Prior work improves sample efficiency with privileged information during training in several ways.
Asymmetric actor–critic methods give privileged state to the critic while training the actor from
visual inputs [7]. This can improve value estimation and the resulting policy updates, but the ac-
tor still generates rollouts from high-dimensional visual observations throughout training. More
broadly, asymmetric learning methods use full-state information during training to improve policies
that operate from partial observations [8]. However, these methods do not explicitly regulate visual
rollout exposure according to whether privileged-state and visual inputs induce compatible action
distributions. Teacher–student and distillation methods transfer knowledge from a trained teacher
to a student model or policy [9, 10, 11]. In robotics, privileged simulator state is often used to train
a teacher policy or world model that is then transferred to a vision-based student [12, 13, 14, 15].
Such pipelines typically separate privileged teacher training from visual student transfer. In con-
trast, FOCUS performs this transition online within a single PPO run by using cross-modal policy
disagreement to regulate whether rollouts are generated from privileged-state or RGB-D latents.
Other privileged-learning methods use training-only information to guide visual attention, provide
auxiliary learning signals, or regularize reliance on privileged inputs [16, 17, 18]. Separately, vi-
sual RL methods improve learning from pixels through representation learning and data augmen-
tation [19, 20, 3]. These directions are complementary, but they do not use cross-modal action
agreement to regulate which modality generates on-policy rollouts. We ask whether privileged in-
formation can be used online for this purpose.
In this work, our goal is to improve the sample efficiency and training reliability of RGB-D policies
in simulated PPO-based manipulation without requiring RGB-D-based rollout collection from the
start or separating privileged training and RGB-D transfer into distinct stages. We propose FOCUS,
a single-stage RL framework applied to PPO [21] for privileged-to-vision transfer in robotic manip-
ulation. FOCUS is based on two complementary ideas. First, we use cross-modal representation
alignment to reduce mismatch between training-time and test-time inputs. Second, we introduce
an online KL-guided switching mechanism that regulates when the actor uses RGB-D rather than
privileged-state latents. An upper-quantile KL budget reduces RGB-D exposure when rare high-
disagreement states remain. This progressively shifts on-policy training toward the RGB-D inputs
available at test time while retaining privileged-state value estimation.
Contributions.
(1) We formulate FOCUS as a single-stage PPO-based method that makes privileged-state and RGB-

Related Work
We review prior work related to privileged-to-vision transfer, focusing on teacher–student distilla-
tion, asymmetric actor–critic methods, and privileged-information-guided learning.
Teacher–Student and Distillation.
Knowledge distillation transfers behavior or representations
from a teacher to a student [9, 10, 11]. In robotic learning with privileged information, a policy or
world model is often first trained using privileged state, and its knowledge is then transferred to an
RGB- or depth-based student [12, 13, 14, 15]. Such pipelines typically separate privileged train-
ing from visual transfer. Related asymmetric imitation methods use a privileged expert to guide a
policy that operates from limited observations [8]. Recent methods reduce this separation through
joint teacher-student training or adaptive data sharing [22, 23, 24], but still organize learning around
distinct teacher and student policies and do not directly use their current behavioral agreement to de-
termine which observation pathway collects on-policy experience. FOCUS instead uses privileged-
state and RGB-D paths within a single actor and regulates their rollout use according to policy
disagreement, limiting premature RGB-D rollout collection while allowing the RGB-D path to learn
directly from task rewards. By reducing low-quality RGB-D rollouts early in training, this design
aims to improve sample efficiency while preserving or improving RGB-D test-time success.
Asymmetric Actor–Critic and Privileged Critics.
Asymmetric actor–critic methods provide
privileged information to the critic while training the actor from observations available at test time
[7, 25, 26]. The privileged critic can improve sample efficiency by providing more informative
value estimates without making the actor depend on privileged information at test time. Related
dual-critic methods combine privileged-state and observation-based critics to improve learning un-
der partial observability [27]. However, the actor must still learn perception and control jointly
from RGB-D from the start of training, so an immature visual pathway can generate low-quality
on-policy experience and slow learning. FOCUS retains a privileged-state critic, but additionally
provides privileged-state and RGB-D actor paths and regulates which path generates rollouts ac-
cording to their policy disagreement. This aims to reduce inefficient early RGB-D experience while
improving RGB-D-only success at test time.
Privileged-Information-Guided Learning and Visual RL.
Privileged information has been used
during training to guide visual attention, strengthen auxiliary learning components, or regularize
reliance on privileged inputs [16, 17, 18]. These methods can improve sample efficiency and final
visual-policy performance, but they do not explicitly align privileged-state and RGB-D actor paths
at the level of action selection or use their agreement to control rollout generation.
Separately, visual RL methods improve learning from pixels through reconstruction, latent dynam-
ics models, contrastive representation learning, and data augmentation [2, 28, 19, 20, 3]. These
methods directly optimize the test-time visual policy and can improve both learning speed and final
success, but still rely on RGB-D-generated experience from the start of training. FOCUS combines
privileged-state and RGB-D actor paths, using action-level alignment and policy disagreement to
limit inefficient early RGB-D rollouts while continuing to optimize RGB-D-only test-time perfor-
mance.
Fusion and Modality Routing.
Gated fusion, Transformer-based attention, multimodal trans-
formers, FiLM conditioning, and representation switching provide useful mechanisms for com-
bining or selecting representations [29, 30, 31, 32, 33]. However, they do not address replacing
training-only privileged state with RGB-D-based action selection at test time.

3.2
KL-guided modality switching
FOCUS controls which observation pathway generates each actor rollout. For each rollout, it uses
cross-modal policy disagreement to determine the probability of selecting the RGB-D pathway, and
keeps the selected modality fixed throughout the rollout. It measures how much the actor’s action
distribution changes when the privileged-state input is replaced by the RGB-D input, and converts
this disagreement into an RGB-D rollout probability λr. A single Bernoulli variable is sampled per
rollout and held fixed throughout that rollout, so the actor does not switch observation pathways
between timesteps. We use this KL only as a measure of cross-modal behavioral compatibility, not
as a certificate of task competence.

3.3
Representation alignment objective
The representation objective trains the RGB-D pathway to induce control behavior compatible with
the privileged-state pathway. Our primary term is a policy-output alignment loss. For corresponding
privileged-state and RGB-D inputs, we evaluate the same actor using zt,s and zt,o while keeping
proprioception and the previous action fixed, and denote the resulting action distributions by πs and
πo. During this loss, the actor and fusion network are frozen, so the RGB-D encoder is updated to
reduce their policy disagreement:

3.5
Training procedure
Training uses a single on-policy PPO loop with separate RL and representation optimizers. The over-
all rollout flow is illustrated in Figure 1, with the complete training procedure given in Appendix A.
The RL optimizer steps the actor-critic and privileged-state pathway, while the representation op-
timizer steps the RGB-D encoder and auxiliary representation components. Implementation-level

optimizer routing is provided in Appendix K. For each rollout, the KL-guided switch selects either
the privileged-state or RGB-D pathway for the actor and keeps that selection fixed throughout the
rollout, while the critic remains anchored to the privileged-state pathway. Cross-modal policy dis-
agreement measured during training updates the running statistics used to regulate RGB-D exposure
in subsequent rollouts.

Experimental Setup
Tasks and baselines.
We evaluate on Reach-Cube, Lift-Cube, Push-Cube, Open-Drawer, and two-
block Pick-and-Place (PnP) in IsaacLab (See Appendix I). We compare FOCUS against states-only
as a privileged-observability reference, RGB-D-only, RGB-D-asymmetric, and a 3-stage teacher-
student baseline that trains a privileged-state PPO teacher, distills an RGB-D student from simulator
rollouts, and then fine-tunes the student with PPO. These baselines are matched IsaacLab instan-
tiations of the corresponding paradigms rather than direct reproductions of every specialized prior
system.
Protocol and metrics.
All methods are trained with two training seeds and evaluated with five
evaluation seeds per checkpoint. Entries report mean ± standard deviation over training seeds af-
ter averaging evaluation seeds. Test performance is measured by test episode success rate over a
fixed 9000-step evaluation rollout. End-to-end training efficiency is measured primarily by budget-
normalized Training Success AUC, computed as 1

6
Limitations
This study is limited to simulation, relatively short-horizon manipulation, and two training seeds,
reflecting the engineering cost and long runtimes of full RGB-D PPO training. The method is also
sensitive to task-specific reward engineering, which may affect the learned behavior and compa-
rability across tasks. Some runs converge to locally successful but suboptimal behaviors. Given
10


the limited evaluation suite, ablations should be interpreted as component-level trends rather than
universal claims about which auxiliary losses are necessary. FOCUS addresses the privileged-state-
to-RGB-D observation gap and is orthogonal to the sim-to-real gap. Appearance shift, sensing
noise, calibration error, latency, and dynamics mismatch would still require dedicated sim-to-real
methods or real-world fine-tuning. Future work should evaluate FOCUS on physical robots, broader
manipulation suites, and less engineered training signals. More extensive task and seed coverage
is also needed to isolate when the auxiliary alignment components are necessary versus when the
lightweight FOCUS-simple core is sufficient.

I
Protocol details
We evaluate on five IsaacLab manipulation tasks: Reach-Cube, Lift-Cube, Push-Cube, Open-
Drawer, and two-block Pick-and-Place. Simulation runs at 100Hz, control at 50Hz, and episodes
last 5s unless stated otherwise. All methods use matched task definitions, observation preprocessing,
action spaces, and evaluation protocols.
The tasks were selected to cover different levels of visual and control difficulty. Reach-Cube is a
saturation and sanity-check task. Lift-Cube tests visually grounded grasping and lifting. Push-Cube
tests contact-rich object-goal control. Open-Drawer evaluates articulated-object interaction under
dense reward shaping. Pick-and-Place is the most demanding task, requiring grasping, transport,
and placement.
J
Implementation details
RGB-D observations are rendered from tiled cameras, which are used in IsaacLab to accelerate
parallel simulation, and are flattened before being passed to the policy implementation. Because
tiled-camera rendering can introduce cross-environment visual artifacts, some task configurations
apply a camera decontamination step before constructing the RGB-D observation. This step is used
only to remove artifacts of vectorized rendering and is not a source of task information.
The deployable observation interface remains RGB-D, proprioception, and the previous action. The
actor is not given object poses, privileged simulator state, contact state, semantic labels, segmenta-
tion IDs, or task variables at test time.
In the affected simulator configurations, the cleanup mask removes pixels that do not belong to the
agent’s rendered environment before RGB-D construction. This is equivalent in purpose to standard
deployable camera preprocessing such as invalid-depth removal, depth-range truncation, workspace
cropping, or background clipping using camera calibration. The cleanup is applied identically to all
vision-based methods on a given task, including RGB-D-only, RGB-D-asymmetric, teacher-student,
and FOCUS.
When such simulator-specific cleanup is unavailable, the same observation contract can be imple-
mented with depth truncation and workspace cropping alone. Thus, this preprocessing is a shared
rendering-artifact correction rather than a method-specific component or a source of privileged task
information.
