WoVR+: World Model as Reliable Simulator for
Versatile VLA Policy Optimization with RL
Shangqing Zhou1,2,†, Zhennan Jiang1,2,3,†, Ning Ma4, Xin Fu2, Yupeng Zheng1,2, Shuai Tian1,2, Huangrui Li1,2,
Jiahao Liu1, Chao Yu5, Zhongpu Xia1,6, Haoran Li1,*, Dongbin Zhao1
Abstract—Action-conditioned world models can reduce costly
real-robot interaction in reinforcement learning for Vision-
Language-Action models (VLA-RL), but hallucinations can mis-
lead policy optimization. Extending them to richer VLA inter-
faces and long-horizon tasks raises challenges in spatiotemporal
and cross-modal consistency, exploration–reliability balance in
RL, and compounding rollout errors. We present WoVR+,
extending WoVR’s hallucination-aware WM-RL framework by
redesigning the learned environment and imagined interaction
protocol: A VLA-interface-aligned world model provides multi-
view prediction and proprioceptive updates while mitigating
hallucinations. Chunk-wise noise preserves flow-based policy
exploration while limiting prediction-degrading action jitter. For
long-horizon tasks, stage-local imagined training combines stage-
entry resets, stage-specific rewards, local horizons, and control-
lable sampling to shorten generated prefixes and balance training
across stages. WoVR+ improves success over SFT by 21.09% and
16.7% on RoboTwin and 6 real-robot tasks. Long-horizon phone-
packaging success rises from 30% to 75%. Videos are available
at https://youmo445.github.io/WoVR Plus.github.io/.
Index Terms—Multi-View World Model, Vision-Language-
Action Model, Reinforcement Learning, Robot Learning
I. INTRODUCTION
V
ISION-Language-Action (VLA) models [1], [2] are play-
ing an increasingly important role in robotic manipu-
lation [3], where actions are generated end-to-end by con-
ditioning on language instructions and visual observations.
Most existing VLA systems are trained via supervised fine-
tuning (SFT). Although effective in many downstream tasks,
this paradigm fundamentally limits the performance ceiling of
VLA policies, as it is tightly constrained by the quality and
coverage of demonstration data.
Reinforcement Learning (RL) [4] can further improve VLA
policies [5], [6] obtained through SFT. However, off-policy
methods are generally more sample-efficient but often suffer
from distribution shift and training instability [7], whereas
on-policy updates [8], [9] require massive parallel environ-
ment interaction for stable and efficient training [10], making
them impractical for real robots due to costly data collec-
tion [11]. Although simulation-based alternatives have been

interaction with limited robot data. Several works directly

policies through RL [23], [24], using imagined interactions
to reduce additional online robot interaction.
However, learned ACWMs are not faithful simulators [25],
[26]. Here, hallucination refers to a systematic mismatch
between imagined and real outcomes in closed-loop interac-
tion. ACWMs may generate visually plausible yet physically
incorrect rollouts, resulting in spurious success signals under
the policy’s actions. If hallucinated trajectories are directly
used for policy optimization, RL is incentivized to exploit
systematic model errors rather than true task progress. This
leads to a critical question:
If world models inevitably hallucinate, how can RL remain
reliable under imperfect imagined dynamics?
WoVR [25] argues that using world models for RL is
not primarily a modeling problem, but a reliability prob-
lem, and controls hallucination at three interconnected levels:
controllable simulator design, reliable imagined interaction,
and policy–model alignment. Related efforts have also sought
to mitigate the impact of unreliable imagination by down-
weighting rewards from hallucinated rollouts [27], ground-
ing synthetic experience in real trajectories and regulating
its use [28], and shortening imagined trajectories through
branched rollouts initialized from recorded states [26].
Despite these efforts, extending reliable WM-RL to mod-
ern VLA policies and long-horizon tasks introduces addi-
tional challenges. First, unlike the predominantly single-view
ACWMs [20], [25], modern VLA policies [1], [2] rely on
multi-view observations and robot proprioception for fine-
grained manipulation, requiring world models to maintain
temporal, spatial, and cross-modal consistency. Second, during
WM-RL training of flow-based action-chunk policies, overly
stochastic action exploration can disrupt action smoothness
and exacerbate world-model hallucinations. Maintaining ef-
fective exploration while suppressing exploration-induced ac-
tion jitter is therefore essential to preserving the reliability
of imagined interaction. Finally, world-model generalization
errors can accumulate over autoregressive rollouts, limiting
the performance of existing WM-RL frameworks on long-
horizon, multi-stage tasks, making reliable policy optimization
increasingly challenging.

• Substantial redesigns for the extended WM-RL set-

WM-RL framework of WoVR [25] to align with modern
multi-view VLA interfaces, including proprioceptive feedback,
and support long-horizon multi-stage manipulation. Section III
summarizes the shared framework and clarifies the compo-
nents retained, redesigned, and introduced in the present work.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Multi-View World Models
Recent ACWMs increasingly support closed-loop inter-
action with multi-view VLAs. Ctrl-World [29] alternates
chunk-wise multi-view prediction with VLA inference, while
WEAVER [30] combines multi-view latent dynamics, mem-
ory, and reward prediction for policy evaluation, synthetic-data


3
augmentation, and test-time planning. GE-Sim2.0 [31] further
predicts multi-view observations together with robot proprio-
ception and success signals, and accelerates generation through
DMD2 [32]. Beyond individual architectures, some studies
have systematically investigated design choices for ACWMs:
GigaWorld-1 [33] analyzes pretraining, action representation,
memory, and evaluator-oriented post-training; GeniWorld [34]
studies URDF-rendered visual action representations. Despite
these advances, existing multi-view ACWMs mainly empha-
size prediction and evaluation, while their use as closed-
loop environments for VLA-RL places stricter requirements
on interface consistency across observations, actions, camera
motion, and proprioceptive transitions [35].
B. World-Model-Based VLA Reinforcement Learning
Recent works directly optimize VLA policies through in-
teractions with ACWMs. World-Env [21] and WMPO [20]
construct learned environments for VLA policy optimization
through pixel-space rollouts, with World-Env building on
EVAC’s [36] world model design. ProphRL [24] extends imag-
ined policy optimization to flow-based action policies [37].
WoVR [25] mitigates hallucination through shortened roll-
outs and policy-model adaptation and World-VLA-Loop [23]
alternates policy improvement with world-model refinement.
VLA-MBPO [26] further combines a world model with short
branched rollouts to reduce compounding prediction errors.
Despite these advances, existing WM-RL methods either do
not explicitly address the reliability of world-model-based
policy optimization or focus on individual reliability issues in
isolation. How to jointly construct a VLA-aligned learned en-
vironment, support effective policy exploration, and organize
reliable imagined policy optimization remains insufficiently
studied.
III. WOVR FRAMEWORK AND EXTENSIONS
Building on the same core premise that controlling hallu-
cination enables reliable policy optimization in imagination,
we retain WoVR’s overall WM-RL framework and reliability-
driven design principles. Within this framework, we system-
atically redesign the world model, exploration noise, and
imagined interaction protocol to support RL post-training
of multi-view VLA policies for long-horizon manipulation.
This section first reviews WoVR and the shared WM-RL
framework, then clarifies the components retained and the
extensions introduced in this work.

AdaLN and Cross-Attention, while using first-frame-anchored

WoVR addresses this mismatch through Policy-Aligned Co-

B. Extensions and Scope of the Present Work
Building on the WM-RL framework described above,
WoVR+ extend WoVR’s hallucination-aware design to sup-
port multi-view VLA policies and long-horizon, multi-stage
manipulation (Fig. 3).
The following summarizes the components retained, re-
designed, or newly introduced, and clarifies the scope of the
present study:
• Extended and Redesigned. We retain WoVR’s first-
frame anchoring principle and extend its temporal context
to an anchored sparse history that combines the persistent
scene anchor with fixed-stride recent observations. We
redesign the interaction interface and action-conditioning
mechanism. The single-view, delta-EEF formulation with
vector action conditioning in WoVR is replaced by a
VLA-aligned multi-view interface with absolute joint-
and-gripper commands, geometry-grounded action and
camera conditioning, and command-based proprioceptive
updates.
• New. We introduce chunk-wise Flow-SDE exploration for
absolute-action chunks, preserving the marginal explo-
ration scale while enforcing temporal coherence across
neighboring action targets.
• Broadened in scope. KIR in WoVR uses intermediate-
state initialization around a task-critical region to reduce
effective prediction depth. In the present work, we carry
this reset-based principle into long-horizon multi-stage
optimization, combining data-supported stage-entry resets
with stage-specific rewards, local rollout horizons, and
controllable stage sampling to organize training opportu-
nities across task stages.

3) Closed Loop Context and Proprioception Update:
a) Anchored sparse history: During autoregressive in-
teraction, generated observations become the context for
subsequent ACWM calls. Recent observations describe task
progress but may gradually lose initial scene information;
conversely, the first frame alone does not describe the current

V. EXPERIMENTS
Our experiments evaluate the proposed world model and its
use as an interactive environment for VLA-RL. We organize
the evaluation around five questions:
1) World Model Capability (Sec.V-A) Does the pro-
posed ACWM provide the basic predictive capability re-
quired of a VLA interaction environment, including high
fidelity, cross-view consistent, and action-conditioned
video generation?
2) Policy-Level Utility of the ACWM (Sec.V-B) Does
the WM, beyond high-fidelity video prediction, support
reliable policy evaluation and provide useful training
data for policy improvement?
3) Exploration in WM-RL (Sec.V-C) How should a flow-
based VLA explore when interacting with the learned
environment?
4) Policy Optimization with WM-RL (Sec.V-D) Can
RL entirely through imagined VLA–WM interaction
improve the policy?
5) Long-Horizon Policy Learning (Sec.V-E) How can
WM-RL be organized to effectively optimize long-
horizon tasks composed of multiple sequential manip-
ulation skills?
Our experiments span both simulation and real robots:
RoboTwin is used for controlled world-model and WM-RL
studies, while policy-level consistency, multi-task WM-RL,
and long-horizon task are evaluated on real robots.

E. Long-Horizon Policy Learning
Long-horizon WM-RL with full-start rollouts is limited by
three factors:
• stage-access imbalance: later stages receive fewer train-
ing opportunities because they depend on successful
preceding execution;
• reward sparsity: trajectory-level success rewards do not
distinguish intermediate task progress;
• prefix-induced model error: later-stage optimization re-
lies on increasingly long autoregressive ACWM prefixes.
We examine these limitations separately to evaluate whether
stage-wise rewards and stage-local imagined training can im-
prove long-horizon policy learning.
1) Task, Variants, and Evaluation Protocol: We consider
Phone Packaging, a 4-stage task spanning approximately 700
control steps at 10 Hz, including base positioning, phone
placement, lid reorientation and handover, and bimanual lid
closure (As shown in Fig. 16).
Stage boundaries are defined based on transitions between
manipulation skills and observable subgoal completion and
finalized through manual annotation, with one binary reward
model for each stage.
Under matched real-data and computing budgets, we eval-
uate the following WM-RL variants:
• Full-start + trajectory reward. All rollouts start from
the task initial state and receive only a binary full-task
success reward.
• Full-start + stage rewards. Rollouts remain full-start,
while stage-specific reward models provide intermediate
completion signals along the trajectory.
• Stage-reset + arrival-matched sampling. Rollouts start
from data-supported stage-entry histories, while the stage-
sampling probabilities match the empirical stage-arrival
distribution of full-start rollouts.
• Stage-reset + uniform sampling. Stage-entry histories
are sampled uniformly across the 4 stages, providing
balanced local training opportunities.
• KIR. Following WoVR [25] and using a simplified ver-
sion, imagined rollouts are only initialized from task-level
keyframes to shorten the generated prefix.
All WM-RL variants optimize the same shared π0.5 policy
under the instruction ‘Pack the phone.’. Stage information is
used only during training for reset selection, rollout horizon,
and reward routing. All policies are evaluated end-to-end from
the task initial state without intermediate resets.
2) Overall Policy Performance: Fig 17 reports completion
rates for all 4 stages. SFT succeeds in 30% of trials, while
full-start WM-RL improves this to 50% with trajectory-level
rewards and 55% with stage-wise rewards. KIR reaches 50%,
and stage-reset training shows a strong dependence on the
stage-sampling strategy: arrival-matched sampling drops to
20%, whereas uniform stage sampling achieves the best end-
to-end success of 75%.
The intermediate stage-completion rates further reveal dis-
tinct failure patterns. Full-start stage-wise rewards substan-
tially improve progress through Stages 2 and 3, but still leave
a large gap at Stage 4. Arrival-matched stage-reset training
preserves strong intermediate-stage completion yet fails to
convert this into final task success, whereas uniform stage
sampling yields the most balanced completion profile across
all stages. We analyze reward granularity, generated-prefix
length, stage-training allocation, and the difference from KIR
separately below.

of reward granularity by keeping full-start imagination un-

further compare the optimization efficiency of stage-local
and full-start training with stage-wise rewards. As shown in

7) Comparison with KIR used in WoVR: Finally, we com-
pare stage-local uniform training with the Keyframe-Initialized
Rollout (KIR) protocol introduced in WoVR [25]. KIR short-
ens the generated prefix by initializing rollouts near a task-
critical region, but does not explicitly organize optimization
across the multiple skill stages of the complete task.
KIR achieves cumulative completion rates of 18/20, 13/20,
10/20, and 10/20 across Stages 1–4, corresponding to 50%
end-to-end success. The fact that earlier-stage performance
also changes under KIR is expected because all stages share
the same policy parameters, so suffix-level optimization can
transfer to behaviors outside the directly optimized segment.
Nevertheless, these earlier stages receive no explicit stage-
balanced supervision.
In comparison, uniform stage-local training achieves 20/20,
17/20, 16/20, and 15/20, yielding 75% end-to-end success. The
improvement is therefore not explained by prefix shortening
alone. By combining stage-specific rewards, direct stage-entry
initialization, and explicit balancing of training opportunities
across stages, stage-local training provides a more systematic
optimization protocol for long-horizon manipulation while
retaining a single shared policy for end-to-end deployment.
VI. CONCLUSION
We present WoVR+, a world-model-based RL framework
for improving VLA policies with limited real-robot interaction.
WoVR+ combines a geometry-grounded multi-view world
model, temporally coherent chunk-wise exploration, and stage-
local imagined training to support reliable closed-loop policy
optimization. Experiments in simulation and on real robots
demonstrate improved world-model fidelity and downstream
policy performance, while long-horizon analyses further show
the benefits of localized rewards, shorter imagined prefixes,
and balanced stage-wise training.
Despite these advantages, the current framework relies on
predefined task stages and data-supported stage-entry states.
In addition, chunk-wise Flow-SDE introduces cross-token
correlations, while the current GRPO implementation employs
token-wise surrogate policy ratios. Future work may explore
automatic stage discovery, adaptive reset-state construction,
and joint chunk-level optimization that explicitly accounts for
temporally correlated exploration.

APPENDIX B
REWARD MODEL USED IN WM-RL
We use lightweight task-specific ResNet classifiers to con-
vert the current multi-view observation into a binary success
reward. Synchronized head, left-wrist, and right-wrist images

APPENDIX C
REAL-ROBOT PLATFORM AND DATA INTERFACE
All real-robot experiments are conducted on a dual-arm
ARX-5 manipulation platform. Each arm contains six actuated
joints and a parallel-jaw gripper, resulting in a 14-D joint-and-
gripper proprioceptive state. The platform is equipped with
three Intel RealSense D405 cameras as shown in Fig. 22.
Fig. 22. Dual-arm ARX-5 manipulation platform.
During demonstration collection, all streams retain their
original timestamps and are synchronized and resampled onto
the 10-Hz control timeline used for policy training and de-
ployment. Consequently, each policy observation consists of
synchronized head, left-wrist, and right-wrist RGB images
together with the 14-D proprioceptive state.
Our demonstrations are collected through direct kinesthetic
teaching. Since this procedure records the realized robot
proprioception rather than an independently logged command
sequence, we construct the absolute joint-and-gripper action
