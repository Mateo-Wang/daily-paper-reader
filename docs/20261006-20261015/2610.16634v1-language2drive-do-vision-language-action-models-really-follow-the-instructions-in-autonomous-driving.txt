Abstract
Recent Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models increasingly use natural-language instructions to condition
autonomous driving behavior. Yet existing closed-loop benchmarks primarily measure route completion,
safety, and rule compliance, leaving a fundamental question unresolved: does language actually determine
which feasible behavior the vehicle executes? A trajectory may be safe and successful while still violating
or even ignoring the passenger’s instruction. To address this, we introduce Language2Drive, a benchmark
designed to directly test this capability. Its key evaluation unit, the paired counterfactual unit (PCU), holds
the initial driving scene fixed while changing only the instruction. This design enables us to distinguish
isolated instruction success from counterfactual paired success across four instruction-following scopes.
To test whether this distinction persists across evaluation regimes, we instantiate Language2Drive in
complementary closed-loop CARLA simulation, reconstructed real-world scenes, and open-loop driving
benchmarks, including our released CARLA-Language2Drive simulator with purpose-built maps. Evaluating
representative driving VLAs across these settings reveals a striking mismatch between strong driving
competence and weak language control: 93.2% of successful rollouts do not have a successful counterpart
under the alternative feasible instruction. This failure is dominated by one-sided completion, where a
model repeatedly executes one preferred behavior but fails when the same scene requires the counterfactual
alternative. These results expose two fundamental limitations of current evaluation: isolated success and
driving scores, which are widely used by conventional driving benchmarks, can substantially
overstate language-following capability, while current driving VLAs themselves lack reliable
instruction following under matched counterfactual tests. Effective evaluation must therefore
measure not only whether a VLA can drive successfully, but whether changing the instruction reliably
alters which feasible behavior it selects and successfully executes.

1 Introduction
Recent advances in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have opened a new paradigm for autonomous
driving: natural-language instructions is able to condition driving behaviors (Zhou et al., 2026b,c; Shao et al.,
2024; Tian et al., 2024; Hwang et al., 2024; Renz et al., 2025). By building upon internet-scale pretraining in
large vision-language models, VLAs inherit rich world knowledge, open-world perception, scene understanding,
and multimodal reasoning capabilities from foundation models. It promise an interface to express passenger
intent without direct manual control, in which passengers can specify how to drive and what task to accomplish.
Realizing this promise requires more than generating plausible motion: when several safe, legal, and physically
feasible behaviors are available, the system must select and sustain the one requested by the passenger.
Existing evaluation does not fully establish this capability. Driving benchmarks emphasize route completion,
safety, and traffic rule compliance (Dauner et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025; Jia et al., 2024), while
language-guided evaluations assess instructed route execution or instruction-specific predicted actions (Shao
et al., 2024; Renz et al., 2025). These evaluations provide important evidence of driving competence and task
completion. Yet a safe trajectory can still reach the wrong named parking space or violate a requested passing
restriction. More fundamentally, even successful execution of an individual instruction does not establish that
language reliably determines the selected behavior. The central question is therefore: Do VLAs really follow
instructions, or can their apparent successes be explained by behaviors they would execute regardless of the
request?
To address this, we introduce Language2Drive, a closed-loop benchmark for evaluating reliable instruction
following in autonomous driving. The key challenge is that success on a single request does not establish
language-conditioned control. At an intersection where turning left and going straight are both safe and feasible,
for example, a policy that consistently turns left may succeed on the left-turn request without demonstrating
that it can execute the alternative when asked. Conversely, comparing instructions across different scenes
4


confounds language variation with environmental variation. Language2Drive therefore evaluates matched
alternatives through paired counterfactual units (PCUs). Each PCU holds the decision context, non-language
inputs, and simulation seed fixed while evaluating two mutually exclusive but feasible instructions in separate
runs. This design distinguishes isolated success from the ability to execute either requested alternative under
matched conditions. Because an instruction may admit many valid trajectories, each request is evaluated by
machine-checkable spatial, temporal, and safety criteria rather than reference-trajectory matching. Strict
Instruction Success Rate (SISR) measures individual completion, whereas Counterfactual Paired Success
(CFPS) requires both alternatives in a PCU to succeed. Three meaning-preserving expressions further test
whether paired success remains stable across linguistic variation.
Language2Drive organizes tasks by what language controls: atomic actions, persistent behavior, scene-grounded
navigation, and long-horizon programs. To support these tasks, we release CARLA-Language2Drive, a
CARLA extension that adds six purpose-designed maps covering underground parking, a port, Chinese urban
roads, a U.S.-style city with detailed signage, a campus, and a highway. A complementary AlpaSim track is
built from reconstructed real-world road scenes selected through targeted mining and manual review (NVIDIA
et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025). These environments provide alternative maneuvers, identifiable referents, and
execution intervals needed to test language-conditioned behavior. We also extend paired evaluation based on
the test sets developed from nuScenes, NAVSIM, and WOD-E2E, complementing closed-loop execution with
open-loop tests on recorded driving contexts.
Our evaluation reveals that driving quality and individual instruction success do not establish
reliable language control. Across principal CARLA systems, 93.2% of successful rollouts lack a successful
opposite-instruction counterpart. Most observed successes are therefore one-sided: one request is completed,
but its feasible alternative is not.
In reconstructed scenes, Alpamayo 1.5 achieves 100% Safety and a
reference-path DrivingScore of 97.17, yet zero CFPS. Moreover, no evaluated CARLA system completes both
alternatives under all three tested language expressions. The individual–paired discrepancy also appears in
open-loop evaluations across all three datasets. Together, these findings show why driving scores and isolated
successes must be supplemented by tests of alternative selection, complete execution, and consistency across
equivalent instructions.
Our contributions are threefold. First, we introduce a paired evaluation framework that distinguishes individual
instruction completion from successful execution of alternative requests under matched conditions. Second,
we instantiate four language-control scopes in Language2Drive, contributing an extended CARLA simulator
with six purpose-designed maps, a curated AlpaSim track, and complementary open-loop evaluations. Third,
we reveal a substantial separation between driving quality and reliable instruction following, characterizing
one-sided success, incomplete execution, and fragile paired success across paraphrases. These findings establish
matched, instruction-specific execution as an essential complement to conventional driving evaluation.
2 Language2Drive
2.1 Overview
We introduce Language2Drive, a closed-loop benchmark designed to evaluate whether vision-language-action
models for autonomous driving reliably follow passenger instructions.
Task Definition
Autonomous-driving instruction following is the ability to select and execute the passenger-
requested behavior among safe, legal, and physically feasible alternatives when navigation is absent or
does not determine the choice. Without a passenger specifying each reaction, the system must still
respond to traffic; when a request conflicts with safety or feasibility, it should perform an appropriate
safe response.
2.2 Paired counterfactual units
A single successful request can coincide with a system’s default behavior. We therefore test the same decision
context twice: for example, one run asks the vehicle to turn left, while the other to continue straight. Both
requests are feasible, but a successful execution of one excludes the other. We call this matched test a Paired
Counterfactual Unit (PCU). It tests whether the system can execute either alternative when asked.
5


Both experimental conditions are initialized with the same map, ego/actor states, history, non-language
inputs, and simulation seed. The human language instruction changes; later observations evolve with each
run’s actions. Condition-specific destinations, future reference paths, and turn commands remain private to
the evaluator. Each PCU has two instructions, each with an associated success criterion; meaning-preserving
expressions test the same pair without changing their targets. Appendix A states the conditions under which
an instruction-invariant system cannot succeed on both test conditions.
2.3 Machine-checkable success criteria
Each instruction is paired with a machine-checkable success criterion over the executed trajectory. It specifies
the requested outcome, where the condition applies, and when execution is complete. Three examples illustrate
the distinction between plausible motion and successful execution: “Turn left at this intersection.” Enter
the requested left exit, without satisfying the paired straight-exit criterion. “Park in space B204.” Place the
vehicle footprint inside B204 with a valid heading, sufficiently low speed, and the required dwell time. “First
visit B208, then go to F206.” Complete the two stages in that order; visiting both in reverse order does not
count.
Persistent conditions apply to a reached, nonempty interval that the vehicle must complete. Stopping before
the interval cannot satisfy a request to maintain a speed throughout it. The verifier checks actual ego states
and composes geometric, behavioral, and temporal predicates; exact operators and thresholds appear in
Appendix C.4.

equivalent instructions; driving quality is reported separately.

2.5 Four language-control scopes
We organize instructions by what language controls and how long its requirements remain active. Figure 1
summarizes their task families and vocabulary.
A: Atomic action. Language selects a local maneuver or setpoint, such as a junction turn, lane choice,
speed/start action, or wait-then-park maneuver. Success requires completing the selected maneuver under its
applicable dwell and progress conditions while rejecting the paired alternative. This scope concerns one local
decision, even when execution takes several seconds.
B: Persistent behavior. Language imposes a speed, lane, or actor-related requirement over an annotated
interval, including passing versus following. Paired requests share the interval or actor but prescribe different
behaviors. Verification requires complete traversal and compliance from scope entry to exit; briefly satisfying
the condition or stopping before the interval is insufficient.
C: Scene-grounded navigation. Language selects a named road, parking region, port target, or exact
parking space. The requested entity must be grounded to its physical target: directed exit gates for roads
and polygons for regions or spaces. Exact parking additionally checks footprint, heading, speed, and dwell.
Reaching a nearby target does not satisfy the requested identity.
D: Long-horizon program. Language specifies obligations that depend on task state: ordered visits,
conditional fallbacks, invariants until an event, or reuse of a remembered reference. The verifier tracks stages,
triggers, and bound entities, rejecting incorrect ordering or incomplete execution. Program structure, rather
than duration alone, distinguishes this scope from persistent behavior.
Two CARLA diagnostics complement the feasible-request core. Foundation Driving Qualification (FDQ) tests
traffic reactions without passenger-specified instructions. Safety Qualification uses requests requiring a safe
fallback, refusal, or constrained alternative when literal compliance conflicts with safety or feasibility. An
open-loop extension developed from nuScenes, NAVSIM, and WOD-E2E evaluates predicted trajectories;
Section 3.5 describes its construction.
2.6 Benchmark construction process
Our construction process turns a physical decision opportunity into a matched, executable test (Figure 2).
Each stage addresses a separate validity requirement: meaningful alternatives, unambiguous grounding,
controlled inputs, or verifiable execution.
7


Figure 2 Benchmark construction process.The process to build the benchmark is as follows: (1) Source curation
identifies decision opportunities; (2) Geometric grounding binds language to physical entities; (3) Paired specification
fixes shared conditions and opposed outcomes; (4) Compilation separates public inputs from private criteria; (5)
Validation checks the resulting tasks. The benchmark contains 64 CARLA and 30 RealSim PCUs.
Source curation. CARLA-Language2Drive extends CARLA (Dosovitskiy et al., 2017) with six purpose-
designed environment types: underground parking, a port, Chinese urban roads, a US city with detailed
signage, a campus, and a highway. We select decision contexts that permit opposed feasible behaviors.
RealSim mines reconstructed PhysicalAI-NuRec scenes executed through AlpaSim (NVIDIA et al., 2025;
Wu et al., 2025); temporal screening requires both pre-instruction context and enough recording time for the
requested maneuver.
Geometric grounding. Directed lane graphs, exit gates, named regions, parking polygons, and physical
actors bind linguistic references to measurable entities. Parking uses the ego footprint and heading, while
persistent tasks specify explicit entry/exit geometry and relevant actors. These bindings distinguish executing
a request from merely approaching its target and prevent an empty or unreached interval from counting as
compliance.
Paired specification. Each PCU fixes the initial scene and actor setup, then assigns mutually exclusive
requests with corresponding success predicates, private reference paths, and deadlines. CARLA has three
meaning-preserving expressions per arm; RealSim uses one.
Public/private compilation. Compilation separates public language, observations, ego state, and neutral
context from private targets and scoring information. Consequently, the evaluator can check the requested
alternative without revealing its future path or destination through auxiliary inputs.
Task validation. Static checks verify entity bindings, directed reachability, footprint constraints, and
matched non-language initialization. All PCUs are each validated via a full-scale evaluator that accounts for
dynamic execution—incorporating deadlines, occupancy, dynamic participants, and temporal requirements.
Human reviewers further verify designated parking spaces, road names, and the operational ranges of "next
road segments," making sure the model under test is able to determine the target and the scope of constraints
based on actual camera feeds, historical data, and shared context. Every PCU has successful privileged
executions of both alternatives under three seeds, giving witnesses that satisfy the requested criterion and
reject its counterpart.

3.2 Can success on one instruction establish language control?
Most successful requests have no successful counterpart. Across the principal CARLA systems, 93.2%
of successes are one-sided: one request succeeds while its feasible alternative fails. LMDrive achieves the
highest SISR, 12.24%, yet completes no pair. Alpamayo 1.5 and SimLingo each complete only 1.04% CFPS,
leaving 27 of 31 and 26 of 30 individual successes unpaired. These are genuine individual completions, but
they are rarely accompanied by successful execution of the alternative request. The discrepancy also appears
in reconstructed scenes: only one of 240 evaluated system–PCU pairs succeeds strictly, achieved by SimLingo,
while Alpamayo2-Super reaches 13.33% SISR with zero CFPS.
Successful pairs demonstrate the capability that isolated successes leave untested. Alpamayo 1.5
completes both passing and following on CARLA B-027/p1, and SimLingo satisfies both requested speed
bands on RealSim P22. These positive examples show that the evaluated systems can complete matched
alternatives in some contexts; the aggregate results show how rarely they do so across the benchmark. Paired
evaluation therefore distinguishes individual completion from demonstrated access to both requested behaviors.
Appendix F gives the count-level decomposition.

3.4 How reliable is control across wording and task structure?
Successful pairs do not persist across all tested language expressions. No CARLA system completes
both arms of a PCU under all three meaning-preserving expressions. A low overall outcome-change rate
can conceal fragile successes: SimLingo changes on only 9 of 128 arms, but those nine account for 60% of
the 15 arms it ever solves. Alpamayo 1.5’s corresponding conditional rate is 75%. On A-010, SimLingo’s
arm-outcome patterns are 110 and 101, so paired success occurs only for expression 0. LMDrive, by contrast,
completes 14 arms under every expression but never completes a pair. Consistency on one request therefore
does not establish consistent access to its alternative under the tested wordings.
Observed long-horizon program success is confined to maintaining an invariant until an event.
Across CARLA tracks, all seven strict successes in scope Long-horizon program occur in this family: six from
LMDrive and one from SimLingo. Ordered visits, conditional fallbacks, and reference-return programs yield
no strict success in 504 rollouts. The scope-level breakdown thus distinguishes limited success on one temporal
structure from the broader ability to execute instruction-defined programs. Detailed results and cases appear
in Appendices F and I.

paired predicted trajectories on our test set developed from nuScenes, NAVSIM, and WOD-E2E (Caesar et al.,

4 Related Work
VLA Models for Autonomous Driving.
LMDrive and SimLingo connect visual observations and
language to closed-loop driving, with SimLingo explicitly aligning language and actions (Shao et al., 2024;
Renz et al., 2025). Recent developments include Qwen-Drive’s unified perception–understanding–planning
framework (Zhou et al., 2026a), Alpamayo 1.5’s user-command-conditioned trajectory generation (Wang et al.,
2025b), and Drive My Way’s adaptation to persistent preferences and immediate style instructions (Wang
et al., 2026b). These methods motivate a separate evaluation question: can language reliably select and
sustain the requested behavior among feasible alternatives?
Language-Conditioned Driving Evaluation. nuPlan, Bench2Drive, and NAVSIM/v2 assess planning
quality and safety (Caesar et al., 2021; Jia et al., 2024; Dauner et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2025). LangAuto,
and Drive4C extend evaluation to language-guided navigation, capability-specific tasks, and open-ended
instruction realization, respectively (Shao et al., 2024; Sohn et al., 2025). Action Dreaming evaluates alternative
instruction-conditioned predictions within shared scenes (Renz et al., 2025), whereas ICR-Drive measures
driving-score changes under matched instruction perturbations (Hamid et al., 2026). Complementing these
protocols, Language2Drive requires both mutually exclusive, feasible requests to satisfy their own spatial–
temporal contracts in matched closed-loop runs, separating isolated completion from successful execution of
both alternatives (Table 3).
5 Conclusion
We introduce Language2Drive, a benchmark that makes reliable passenger control through language an explicit
evaluation objective for autonomous-driving vision-language-action models. Its paired counterfactual framework
combines matched initial conditions with machine-checkable spatial and temporal criteria, distinguishing
individual instruction success from the ability to execute both requested alternatives. We instantiate this
framework through an extended CARLA simulator with purpose-designed maps and curated reconstructed
scenes in AlpaSim, complemented by open-loop evaluations developed from nuScenes, NAVSIM, and WOD-
E2E. Our results reveal a substantial gap between driving performance and reliable instruction following.
Across the six principal CARLA systems, 93.2% of successful rollouts lack a successful paired counterpart,
and strict paired success reaches at most 1.04%. Success also remains fragile across paraphrases, while
ordered, conditional, and reference-return programs expose persistent execution difficulties. These findings
establish that producing a plausible driving trajectory is insufficient evidence of instruction following. By
evaluating which alternative is selected, whether its requirements are fully executed, and whether success
persists across equivalent expressions, Language2Drive provides a concrete basis for developing driving systems
that passengers can direct consistently, precisely, and safely through language.

A.1 What a paired test rules out
For PCU i, define the strict acceptance set for request j by
Ai,j = {τ : Oi,j(τ)(1 −Oi,1−j(τ))Si,j(τ)Pi,j(τ)Ti,j(τ)Hi,j(τ) = 1}.
The two sets are disjoint because each requires the paired-alternative criterion to fail. This remains true even
if a permissive trajectory can satisfy both raw target predicates.
Proposition 1 (Instruction-invariant execution). With identical non-language initialization and fully coupled
execution randomness, an instruction-invariant system has zero strict CFPS and at most 50 SISR on balanced,
mutually exclusive arm acceptances.
Proof. An instruction-invariant system receives the same effective inputs at initialization. The same control
law and coupled randomness give identical actions and simulator transitions. Induction over time therefore
gives the same executed trace τ under both requests. If both arms succeeded, τ would belong to the disjoint
sets Ai,0 and Ai,1, a contradiction. At most one arm can succeed, so every pair contributes zero to CFPS and
at most one half to SISR. A default behavior satisfying exactly one request in every pair attains the SISR
bound.
Coupling is a substantive assumption. An instruction-independent policy that independently chooses two
valid behaviors with probabilities q and 1 −q in the two runs has expected paired success q(1 −q), up to
25%, despite ignoring language. Coupling that behavior-selection randomness instead produces the same
choice in both runs and zero CFPS. A shared seed label does not guarantee identical latent randomness across
asynchronous workers or decoding implementations. The empirical scores establish execution of matched
alternatives under the reported system; instruction-ablated repetitions would additionally test causal language
dependence.

B.9 What paired success establishes
Instruction-invariant execution under coupled randomness. Let Ai,j be the strict acceptance set induced by
Equation 7. Alternative rejection gives Ai,0 ∩Ai,1 = ∅. With identical non-language initialization and fully
coupled execution randomness, an instruction-invariant system produces the same executed trajectory under
both requests. That trajectory cannot lie in both acceptance sets. Such a system therefore has zero CFPS
and at most 50 SISR on balanced pairs. The bound is attained when the same behavior satisfies exactly one
request in every pair.
22


Stochastic execution. Coupling is substantive, not merely a shared seed label. An instruction-independent
policy that independently selects the two valid behaviors with probabilities p and 1 −p in the two runs
has paired-success probability p(1 −p) and expected CFPS 100p(1 −p) ≤25.
Coupling its behavior-
selection randomness instead makes it choose the same behavior in both runs and yields zero paired success.
Asynchronous execution and decoding can prevent a shared seed label from coupling all relevant randomness.
Empirical CFPS therefore establishes successful matched execution under the reported system and protocol;
instruction-ablated repetitions would additionally test causal language dependence.
Statistical interpretation. The current model comparisons use one evaluation seed per track.
CARLA
expressions are clustered within PCUs, and several RealSim PCUs share a reconstructed scene. Uncertainty
estimates must respect these units rather than treat all arms and expressions as independent samples.
Aggregate scores alone do not provide such estimates, and small score differences are descriptive rather
than evidence of statistical separation. The reported metrics do not substitute for unperformed instruction
ablations, repeated-seed tests, or input/controller interventions.

C.4 Finite-trajectory criteria and clocks
The verifier checks normalized, time-ordered actual ego states. Geometric and behavioral predicates represent
gate entry, lane relation, speed, headway, actor order, region occupancy, and parking state. eventually
requires a target event; always requires a condition throughout a reached, nonempty interval; within imposes
a deadline; until maintains an invariant until a terminating event. sequence checks ordered stages and can
25


reject premature, simultaneous, repeated, or oscillatory stage transitions. if_else selects a branch from the
instantiated scene condition, and bind_reference preserves an entity for later reuse.
For example, A-001 binds its left and straight requests to distinct exit gates. D-001 reverses the ordered
parking stages B208 and F206. D-026 stores a named place and requires returning to the same entity after an
intermediate visit. The full strict verdict additionally applies alternative rejection and the safety, progress,
timing, and stability terms.
The CARLA monitor evaluates growing prefixes of the executed trajectory. A strict-success candidate normally
persists for 2 s, and persistent tasks must reach their required horizon. Relaxed semantic success is an auxiliary
scoring verdict: it does not terminate a rollout or shorten the opportunity to meet the full strict criterion.
Execution ends upon strict completion, the task deadline, or an invalid out-of-world ego state. The deadline
component uses satisfied-subcondition timestamps, or the trace endpoint when unavailable; it is not the
negation of a deadline termination label.
RealSim distinguishes recording time, rollout-relative time, and instruction time. Language is issued at
1.7 s. Deadlines are 7.0 s for 23 PCUs and up to 13.8 s for the remainder, leaving 5.3–12.1 s after instruction.
Privileged reference traces extend 6 s beyond the deadline at 0.1 s sampling; that tail is not extra model
execution time. The component validator uses a default maximum sample gap of 0.25 s and numerical speed
tolerance of 10−6 m/s.
C.5 Relaxed proxies, graded completion, and task progress
CARLA SemISR accepts the full requested contract or its predefined relaxed proxy. Depending on the task, the
proxy may use heading, displacement, expanded gate proximity, a lane relation with progress, an observation
in an expanded speed/headway band, or relaxed region geometry. Parking proxies may accept an expanded
footprint-center region and 45◦heading tolerance without stopped dwell. Sequence and reference proxies
retain ordered event checks. These are deterministic partial-completion rules, not a free-form language-model
judgment.
RealSim branch tasks use the first correct irreversible commitment, with timeliness relaxed in the semantic
component. Lane tasks can relax dwell. Scoped-lane and speed tasks retain full entry-to-exit traversal, relevant
exclusivity and trigger conditions, and, for speed, all active samples in the requested band. Target-lane dwell
is 1 s; scoped-lane tasks require a 2 s minimum dwell. Referent triggers must occur after instruction and by
scope entry, then remain latched until exit.
The task-specific maps Ci,j define graded completion in [0, 1] and are aggregated by Equation 3. The binary
strict criterion, relaxed proxy, and continuous map are distinct evaluator outputs. The open-loop rules appear
in Appendix E.2. Within each protocol, all language metrics use the same scheduled pair set, and unsuccessful
executions remain in the denominator.
Task progress is a gate in the strict-success criterion, distinct from continuous reference advancement. CARLA’s
default progress term requires evaluator progress of at least 0.05. RealSim checks a 10 m threshold over the
available rollout, including its pre-instruction segment. CARLA safety excludes normalized collision, off-road,
and red-light events. RealSim scoring v0.2 checks collision and off-road events; requested speed remains a
language criterion. These components are distinct from continuous reference advancement.

E.6 Language interventions and statistical uncertainty
nuScenes intervals. The original binary SemISR and Sem-CFPS intervals use 1,000 scene-cluster bootstrap
replicates with Python random.Random(2026). After sorting the replicate values, the endpoints have indices
⌊0.025(B −1)⌋and ⌊0.975(B −1)⌋, with B = 1000. Supplementary continuous metrics use 2,000 replicates
with NumPy default_rng(2026) and linearly interpolated quantile([.025,.975]). Family-macro intervals
resample scenes independently inside each family before averaging the family means; replicates with an
undefined valid-output denominator are excluded. A scene currently equals one PCU, with its paired arms
retained together. The paired arms of each scene are retained together throughout resampling.
NAVSIM intervals. Stored canonical binary intervals use 1,000 bootstrap replicates over 19 original recordings
(13 for the 22-PCU common-3 s subset), retaining the PCUs within each selected recording. The canonical
NAVSIM intervals are computed over recording clusters; WOD-E2E proxy results are reported deterministically
for the frozen prediction set.
Completed RealSim intervention subset. The September 26 supplemental report adds 42 successful runtime
conditions: two SimLingo versions × three PCUs × seven conditions. These conditions are normal, two
same-instruction repeats, opposite, empty, neutral, and shuffled. The selected PCUs are the first speed, lane,
and route PCUs in frozen source order. Each condition independently resets the closed-loop session, while the
four-camera, pose, route, and other non-language prefixes match before intervention. Normal and its two
repeats share seed 1701.
Same-instruction NBD averages all three pairwise comparisons among the normal runs. Opposite-instruction
NBD and SRG average comparisons against those three runs. Opposite-minus-repeat differences retain
negative values. Shuffled cyclically reassigns the complete normal instruction across the three scenes, rather
than shuffling words. Empty removes only the task-instruction string, retaining the model’s state and task
template. Empty/neutral/shuffled completion is scored against the original target solely as an ablation. Zero
common semantic samples follow the scorer’s fixed zero convention and are accompanied by active-sample
counts and an informative flag. These controls are reported as supplemental diagnostics alongside the full
30-PCU RealSim comparison.

outputs, and scoring artifacts. These assets preserve the mapping from each reported aggregate back to its

F.2 Development/test split and wording robustness
For one PCU/arm, its strict outcomes form a three-bit expression pattern. A changed outcome has one
or two successes; an all-expression success has three. Conditioning the changed-outcome count on arms
with at least one success separates fragile successes from consistent failures. SimLingo changes on 9 of 15
sometimes-successful arms (60%), Alpamayo 1.5 on 12 of 16 (75%), and LMDrive on three of 17 (17.65%).
SimLingo’s A-006 left and straight patterns are 100 and 111; A-010’s two patterns are 110 and 101. Each
PCU completes both alternatives only at expression 0. Alpamayo 1.5’s successful pairs occur on B-011/p0 and
B-027/p1. No system achieves all-expression paired success W > 0. These observations describe the evaluated
systems under the tested wordings; single-seed evaluation does not isolate the contribution of execution
variability.

G.2 FDQ, Bench2Drive driving score, and route completion
The FDQ set contains fixed, loadable Bench2Drive scenario routes, one per ability group and seed 1. Its
frozen strict pass rule requires a Completed/Perfect status and every recorded infraction list to be empty.
The primary FDQ rate is the mean ability-group pass rate, which here equals the number of passing routes
divided by 24.
The scenario set includes overtaking, two-way accidents, blocked intersections, construction obstacles, control
loss, emergency braking, entering traffic flow, hard-braking routes, side-lane hazards, unsignalized left turns, red-
light-running opponents, parked obstacles, parking-related crossings/cut-ins, merging, pedestrians, signalized
junctions, static cut-ins, T-junctions, and yielding to emergency vehicles. The routes are supplementary
driving-competence diagnostics rather than substitutes for language-dependent task pairs.

J Discussion
Language2Drive reveals substantial limitations in the ability of current driving VLAs to translate passenger
instructions into reliable behavior. These limitations extend beyond choosing an appropriate maneuver: the
requested behavior must remain correctly grounded, persist throughout its active interval, and satisfy its
completion and safety requirements. Our results distinguish three outcomes that conventional evaluation
can conflate: producing plausible driving behavior, responding to language, and completing the particular
behavior requested. Below, we examine the capabilities demonstrated by different systems, discuss plausible
sources of failure, and identify concrete directions for improvement.
J.1 Which models demonstrate instruction-following capability?
Paired execution is possible, but remains sparse. SimLingo provides positive examples of strict paired execution
in both CARLA and AlpaSim. Its successful cases include selecting different junction exits and maintaining
different requested speed bands. Alpamayo 1.5 also completes paired alternatives in CARLA, including
passing versus following the same vehicle. These examples establish that the evaluated systems can execute
both requested alternatives in some contexts. However, neither system achieves reliable performance across
60


the benchmark, and no evaluated CARLA system completes both alternatives under all three instruction
expressions. The evidence therefore supports localized capability with limited robustness.
Individual success and driving quality identify different strengths. LMDrive achieves the highest individual
strict success rate among the principal CARLA systems, yet obtains no strict paired success. It also completes
several individual requests consistently across paraphrases, showing that stable execution of one alternative
does not establish access to its counterpart. Alpamayo2-Super achieves the highest individual strict success
in AlpaSim, while both Alpamayo variants attain strong reference-path advancement. Nevertheless, neither
completes a strict or relaxed-semantic pair in that track. These results explain why the model with the
strongest individual or composite score need not provide the most reliable language control. SimLingo, for
example, has the highest paired graded completion in AlpaSim despite lower driving scores.
Partial completion should be recognized without overstating reliability. Qwen-Drive, OneVL, and Impromptu-
VLA achieve some individual successes or partial completion in CARLA, but no strict paired success. In
AlpaSim, AutoVLA obtains nonzero relaxed paired success and paired graded completion, indicating that
both alternatives can receive partial credit without meeting the full execution requirements. Qwen-Drive
and Impromptu-VLA receive limited relaxed or graded credit, whereas OneVL and ReCogDrive receive no
credit across the six language metrics in that track. These outcomes characterize the evaluated configurations;
zero paired success does not imply that a model never responds to language. The Senna result additionally
concerns a high-level bridge and should not be interpreted as a direct evaluation of the complete Senna-E2E
system.
Open-loop results show a similarly uneven capability profile. DriveMA-2B on WOD-E2E and OpenDriveVLA-
0.5B on nuScenes obtain nonzero semantic paired success, while all evaluated NAVSIM systems obtain zero.
Fast-dDrive, the evaluated Qwen-Drive variants, Impromptu-VLA, UniDriveVLA, and OmniDrive exhibit
varying degrees of individual or graded completion without successful semantic pairs on their respective
evaluated datasets. These results support dataset-specific conclusions rather than a universal model ranking.
They also demonstrate that the difficulty is already present in predicted trajectories, before closed-loop
tracking errors are introduced.
J.2 Why does instruction following break down?
Training may allow language to remain unnecessary for action selection. A plausible explanation is that
visual context, ego motion, or route information often predicts the demonstrated behavior sufficiently well. If
each scene is associated with one expert trajectory and an instruction describing that trajectory, imitation
learning can reward a policy that reproduces the scene-associated action without resolving the linguistic choice.
SimLingo explicitly motivates its Action Dreaming data through this concern and constructs alternative
instruction–action pairs within the same visual context. Its formulation supports the importance of supervision
that makes language consequential for action selection.
Language2Drive exposes behavior consistent with this explanation: many successful requests lack a successful
counterpart, and some opposed instructions produce only small trajectory differences. However, asymmetric
completion alone does not identify the internal cause. A model may distinguish the requests but fail to execute
one of them. Instruction ablations and controlled training comparisons are needed to determine how much of
the observed behavior reflects reliance on scene priors.
Language understanding may not be preserved through planning and control. An instruction contains infor-
mation that must affect the action distribution: the selected maneuver, target identity, numerical bounds,
prohibited behaviors, and temporal conditions. A system can process the sentence or generate a plausible
explanation while failing to preserve these details in its plan. The Alpamayo 1.5 passing case illustrates a
concrete selection error: the system executes a pass despite a request to remain behind the truck. Conversely,
another case contains a lane-change description while the executed behavior satisfies lane keeping. Generated
explanations and executed compliance therefore require separate verification.
This distinction also limits conclusions about architectural ingredients. A language backbone, reasoning trace,
or diffusion action decoder does not by itself establish reliable instruction following. Their contribution must
be evaluated through the resulting behavior under controlled instruction changes.
61


J.3 How can instruction following be improved?
Make language necessary in the training data. Training should include multiple feasible behaviors from
matched initial scenes, with each behavior selected by its corresponding instruction. Useful examples include
passing versus following, neighboring parking spaces, different speed intervals, and reversed destination
orders. Semantically equivalent paraphrases should preserve the requested outcome, while minimal changes in
negation, target identity, numerical bounds, or event order should change the required behavior. Counterfactual
augmentation has also improved instruction following in navigation and manipulation settings, providing
additional motivation to test this approach in driving.
The objective should encourage correct semantic selection rather than arbitrary separation between trajectories.
Two trajectories can differ substantially while both violate their instructions, and some meaningful instruction
contrasts require only modest spatial differences. Balanced supervision of both feasible alternatives is therefore
more informative than maximizing behavioral change alone.
Preserve grounded goals and constraints throughout action generation. A promising design is to maintain an
explicit representation of the requested target, active constraints, and completion conditions, and make this
representation available during each planning update. For scene-grounded tasks, named entities should be
associated with persistent spatial referents. For persistent behavior, speed, lane, and actor-relative constraints
should remain active until their termination conditions are satisfied.
Train for complete execution and the weaker alternative. Training objectives should reward fulfillment of the
entire request, including applicable progress, stability, and safety conditions. Pair-aware imitation, preference
optimization, or reinforcement learning could explicitly emphasize the less successful alternative within a
matched pair. This would reduce the incentive to improve average performance by repeatedly solving an
already favored behavior.
Reward design must also respect the instruction. Maximizing forward progress can conflict with requests to
slow down, wait, follow another actor, or stop at a particular location. Progress should therefore be measured
toward the requested task, with credit for partial completion used as guidance rather than a substitute for
final success. The WOD-E2E comparison, where both Qwen-Drive variants have zero semantic paired success,
illustrates that the presence of RL post-training alone does not establish improved language control; the
training objective and supervision remain central.
Add persistent task state and feedback during execution. Long-horizon instructions would benefit from explicit
tracking of the current stage, completed events, active invariants, conditional branches, and remembered
entities. Replanning should update the motion plan while preserving obligations that remain active. A
completion monitor could detect premature stage transitions, lost target bindings, or deviations from sustained
constraints and trigger recovery.
For agents that retrieve task records, memory integrity is a further design consideration. A-MemGuard
validates retrieved memories through reasoning-path consensus and retains negative lessons to defend LLM
agents against memory poisoning (Wei et al., 2025), providing a reference for protecting stored information
before it influences planning.
Training should include interrupted and partially completed executions, so the policy learns to recover
from deviations and resume the outstanding task. For ordered and conditional programs, supervision of
intermediate state transitions may be more informative than a terminal success label alone. These mechanisms
are motivated by the observed failures, but their effectiveness on Language2Drive remains to be tested.
Align planned behavior with the controller that executes it. Models should be evaluated and, where appropriate,
trained with the execution interfaces used at deployment. Controller-aware rollouts can reveal whether failures
arise from incorrect plans or from tracking, timing, and stopping errors. Calibrating trajectory timestamps,
coordinate frames, vehicle dynamics, and replanning intervals is particularly relevant for narrow speed bands,
exact parking, and sustained lane constraints.
A useful diagnostic intervention is to execute the same predicted plan through a verified controller, or to
provide a correct plan through the existing controller. These comparisons can localize an execution bottleneck
before changing the language model. They would also clarify which improvements transfer across simulators
and which depend on a particular interface.
62


Teach useful safe alternatives alongside refusal. The exploratory safety-conflict results indicate that avoiding
a prohibited action and completing a useful replacement are distinct capabilities. Training should therefore
cover safe fallback, justified refusal, and constrained alternatives, with clear supervision of the behavior
appropriate to each conflict. Benign matched requests are also needed to detect unnecessary refusal.
A successful system should preserve as much of the passenger’s feasible intent as possible while respecting
safety constraints. This requires learning what to do after rejecting literal compliance, rather than treating
inactivity as a universally acceptable response. Evaluation should score the usefulness and completion of the
replacement behavior separately from avoidance of the unsafe action.
J.4 What would constitute convincing progress?
Improvement should be demonstrated through increased paired completion on held-out scenes, targets, and
instruction compositions, together with robustness across paraphrases and repeated seeds. Individual success,
paired success, graded completion, and driving quality should remain separately visible. Higher aggregate
scores or larger instruction-induced trajectory differences are insufficient if the requested alternatives remain
uncompleted.
Process-level diagnostics can complement these outcome metrics. Agentic-MME verifies intermediate visual-
tool and search steps in multimodal agents (Wei et al., 2026), while TELBench and DRIFT study span-level
error localization in deep-research agent trajectories (Wang et al., 2026a). For driving, analogous diagnostics
could inspect target bindings, program-state transitions, and constraint violations along a rollout to identify
where execution departs from the requested behavior.
Mechanistic claims require targeted interventions. Removing or shuffling language can test dependence on
the instruction; supplying a correct target binding can test grounding; supplying the correct program state
can test temporal tracking; and substituting a verified controller can test execution. These interventions
should preserve the other relevant conditions and be assessed with uncertainty estimates that respect the
dependence between paired arms and paraphrases. Paired success provides stronger behavioral evidence than
isolated success, but repeated controls remain necessary to distinguish instruction dependence from stochastic
