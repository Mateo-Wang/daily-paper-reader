ABSTRACT
Predictive world models provide compact visual representations for control. Con-
trol requires a latent geometry aligned with temporal reachability rather than pre-
dictive similarity alone.
We introduce Directed Temporal Representations for
Control (DTRC), which learns such a geometry from offline visual trajectories
on top of frozen LeWorldModel (LeWM) features. DTRC constructs a directed
temporal quasimetric over the learned control representation. Short-range tem-
poral offsets calibrate the distance scale. Bootstrapped targets extend temporal
reachability across longer horizons. Action-conditioned consistency aligns the
representation with local transition dynamics. The resulting distance estimates
temporal reaching cost, and its change across a transition defines goal-relative
temporal progress. We use this progress signal as a temporal critic for direct
goal-conditioned policy learning. Model-assisted targets provide an additional
training-time refinement under behavior-support and dynamics-agreement con-
straints. Across ten visual control tasks, DTRC achieves strong goal-conditioned
control performance relative to planning and direct-policy baselines. Held-out di-
agnostics on the four LeWM tasks show consistent short-range temporal calibra-
tion, task-dependent long-range and directional structure, and positive transition-
level progress. Temporal supervision improves the same flow-policy parameteri-
zation across all four LeWM tasks, while the resulting policy acts directly without
iterative trajectory search at test time. Our project website provides code and visu-
alizations: https://chenyang-yuan.github.io/dtrc-project/.

INTRODUCTION
Latent world models compress visual observations into predictive representations and learn action-
conditioned dynamics in latent space, enabling control through imagined rollouts (Hafner et al.,
2019; 2020; Hansen et al., 2022; 2024). Visual goal-reaching planners evaluate candidate actions
using distances between predicted latent states and goal representations (Zhou et al., 2025; Maes
et al., 2026). Latent geometry therefore directly influences the control objective. Recent work
reshapes latent spaces to align Euclidean distance with feasible transition structure and improve
planning (Wang et al., 2026). Temporal reachability provides a complementary control structure
based on ordered state–goal relations. These relations are associated with interaction cost and may
be asymmetric across opposite transition directions (Wang et al., 2023). These properties motivate
a control representation aligned with temporal reachability rather than predictive similarity alone.
Offline trajectories provide direct supervision for this temporal structure. Future states serve as
achieved goals for learning from offline experience (Andrychowicz et al., 2017; Ghosh et al., 2021).
Temporal relations between states have also been used to construct goal-conditioned representa-
tions and value structure (Eysenbach et al., 2022; Park et al., 2023; 2024; Wang et al., 2023). For
control, temporal reachability requires local scale, directional structure, and long-horizon consis-

tency. Short-range temporal offsets establish the local distance scale. Ordered state–goal relations
preserve directional information. Multistep relations extend temporal reachability across longer
horizons (Zheng et al., 2026). Changes in temporal cost across individual transitions provide a lo-
cal measure of progress. A control representation should preserve these properties in a common
temporal geometry.
We introduce Directed Temporal Representations for Control (DTRC) to learn this geometry from
offline visual trajectories. Starting from frozen LeWorldModel (LeWM) features (Maes et al., 2026),
DTRC learns a control representation equipped with a directed temporal quasimetric. A symmet-
ric metric component defines the base geometry. An order-sensitive residual introduces directional
variation in state–goal reaching cost (Liu et al., 2023). Short-range temporal offsets calibrate the
distance scale. Bootstrapped targets extend temporal reachability across longer horizons. Action-
conditioned consistency aligns the representation with local transition dynamics. The resulting dis-
tance estimates temporal reaching cost. Its reduction across a transition defines goal-relative tempo-
ral progress.
The directed temporal distance provides a critic for policy learning. Advantage-weighted methods
use value estimates to weight recorded actions during policy learning (Peng et al., 2019; Kostrikov
et al., 2022). DTRC uses goal-relative temporal progress for the same purpose. Recorded actions re-
ceive larger weights for transitions with greater temporal progress. Policy-generated actions receive
the same temporal evaluation through their predicted successor states. Additional model-assisted
targets are restricted by behavior support and dynamics agreement (Yu et al., 2020; 2021; Sikchi
et al., 2022). Only supported candidates with lower predicted temporal cost than the recorded con-
tinuation contribute to the additional policy objective. Model-assisted targets therefore provide an
additional training-time refinement. At test time, the goal-conditioned policy acts directly without
iterative trajectory search.
We evaluate DTRC on ten visual goal-conditioned control tasks spanning navigation, reaching, and
manipulation. We first evaluate end-to-end goal-conditioned control performance. We then study
temporal structure beyond the directly supervised local range, including task-dependent directional-
ity and transition-level progress. Controlled comparisons isolate the effect of temporal supervision
on the same flow-policy parameterization. Finally, qualitative visualizations examine the organiza-
tion of the learned control representation across tasks. Our main contributions are threefold:
• We introduce DTRC, a framework for learning a directed temporal control representation
on frozen predictive visual features. The representation uses a directed quasimetric to rep-
resent ordered state–goal relations through temporal reaching cost.
• We develop an offline learning objective for temporal representation learning.
Short-
range trajectory offsets establish the distance scale, bootstrapped targets propagate tem-
poral reachability across longer horizons, and action-conditioned consistency aligns the
representation with local transition dynamics. Distance reduction then defines goal-relative
temporal progress for policy supervision.
• Across ten visual goal-conditioned control tasks, DTRC achieves strong performance rel-
ative to predictive-model planning and goal-conditioned policy baselines. Held-out diag-
nostics on the four LeWM tasks show consistent local temporal calibration, task-dependent
long-range and directional structure, and positive transition-level progress. Temporal su-
pervision further improves the same flow-policy parameterization across four LeWM tasks.

We introduce Directed Temporal Representations for Control (DTRC), an offline framework for
visual goal-conditioned control. DTRC learns a directed temporal geometry on top of frozen pre-
dictive visual features. The method contains three stages. First, we learn a control representation
equipped with a directed temporal quasimetric (Section 3.1). Second, offline trajectory supervi-
sion calibrates the local temporal scale and propagates temporal reachability across longer hori-
zons. Action-conditioned consistency further aligns the representation with local transition dynam-
ics (Section 3.2). Third, temporal distance reduction provides a goal-relative signal for direct policy
learning (Section 3.3). Model-assisted targets provide an additional training-time refinement.

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Environments and datasets.
We evaluate DTRC on ten visual goal-conditioned control tasks
spanning navigation, reaching, and manipulation.
We use the four offline datasets adopted by
LeWM (Maes et al., 2026): PushT, TwoRoom, Reacher, and OGBench-Cube.
We addition-
ally evaluate on six visual OGBench tasks: PointMaze, Cube-Double, Scene, AntMaze-Medium,
HumanoidMaze-Medium, and Puzzle-3x3. All methods receive current and goal images encoded
by pretrained LeWM encoders.
Baselines.
We compare against LeWM (Maes et al., 2026), goal-conditioned inverse dynamics
model (GC-IDM) (Nguyen et al., 2026), goal-conditioned behavioral cloning (GCBC) (Ghosh et al.,
2021), goal-conditioned IQL (GC-IQL) (Kostrikov et al., 2022; Park et al., 2025a), and a goal-
conditioned adaptation of Hilbert foundation policies (HILP) (Park et al., 2024). Baseline and im-
plementation details are provided in Appendices A–D.
4.2
GOAL-CONDITIONED CONTROL PERFORMANCE
We first evaluate end-to-end goal-conditioned control performance. The four LeWM tasks provide
a direct comparison with predictive-model planning and goal-conditioned policy baselines. We then
evaluate on six additional visual OGBench tasks to assess performance across a broader set of nav-
igation and manipulation environments. Figure 2 compares DTRC with predictive-model planning
and direct-policy baselines on PushT, TwoRoom, Reacher, and OGBench-Cube. DTRC achieves
the highest or tied-highest success on PushT, TwoRoom, and Reacher, and the second-highest suc-
cess on Cube. Relative to LeWM, DTRC matches its performance on PushT and improves success
by 14.7, 24.0, and 30.0 percentage points on TwoRoom, Reacher, and Cube, respectively. The re-
sults show that temporal representation learning can support direct goal-conditioned control without
test-time trajectory search.

4.3
TEMPORAL STRUCTURE OF THE LEARNED REPRESENTATION
We first evaluate the learned temporal representation independently of policy performance. We
use the EMA target network at 200,000 updates and sample state–future-goal pairs from held-out
episodes. For each task, we draw 200 pairs at every temporal offset from 1 to 100 transitions,
yielding 20,000 pairs. All distances are computed in the full learned representation. Recorded
temporal offset is used as a reference for trajectory duration, rather than as ground-truth optimal
reaching cost. Figure 3 illustrates three properties on PushT: temporal structure across state–goal
offsets, directional state–goal cost, and transition-level temporal progress.
Figure 3a shows that the learned forward distance retains substantial temporal structure beyond the
directly supervised local range, with Spearman correlation ρ = 0.845 over offsets from 1 to 100
transitions. The median distance increases with recorded offset while deviating from the equality
reference at longer horizons. This behavior is consistent with the learning objective: short-range
offsets calibrate the local distance scale, while bootstrapped targets represent longer-horizon state–
goal reaching cost rather than recorded trajectory duration. Figure 3b further shows pronounced
directional structure on PushT. The reverse cost exceeds the forward cost for 92.76% of the sam-
pled pairs, while the symmetric contribution is unchanged under reversal. The asymmetry there-
fore arises from the order-sensitive component (see Appendices F.3 and F.4). Figure 3c evaluates
the one-step instance of temporal progress from Eq. 12. The learned distance decreases after the
recorded transition for 81.41% of the sampled state–goal pairs. This result shows that the learned
geometry resolves local goal-relative progress over the balanced offset range. Temporal progress is
subsequently converted into the temporal advantage used for policy supervision.
We next summarize the same diagnostics across the four LeWM tasks. Table 2 separates short-range
temporal calibration from longer-range, directional, and transition-level properties of the learned
geometry. Short-range MAE remains below two transitions on all four tasks, while long-range
association and directional structure vary by task (Table 2). Positive one-step progress remains
above 50% on these tasks and above 60% on all six additional OGBench tasks (Appendix F.2). Thus,
DTRC resolves goal-relative transition progress across all ten tasks without requiring a common
long-range or directional geometry.

4.4
EFFECT OF TEMPORAL SUPERVISION ON POLICY LEARNING
The representation diagnostics above establish temporal structure in the learned geometry. We next
evaluate whether this structure provides useful supervision for policy learning. We compare un-
weighted flow behavioral cloning with the same flow-policy parameterization augmented by the
learned DTRC control representation and temporal-advantage weighting. Model-assisted targets are
disabled in this comparison.
Temporal supervision improves the same flow-policy parameterization on all four tasks (Table 3).
Success increases by 9.3 percentage points on PushT, 5.3 on TwoRoom, 32.0 on Reacher, and 6.0
on Cube. The largest change occurs on Reacher, where success increases from 68.0% to 100.0%.
Since model-assisted targets are disabled, this comparison isolates the effect of the learned temporal
representation and temporal-advantage supervision from model-assisted policy refinement. With the
representation diagnostics, these results connect the learned temporal geometry to policy learning.
Full component ablations, including behavior-support and directional-residual variants, are reported
in Appendix E.1.

CONCLUSION
We introduced Directed Temporal Representations for Control (DTRC), which learns a directed tem-
poral geometry on top of frozen predictive visual features. The learned quasimetric represents goal-
relative temporal reaching cost, while distance reduction provides transition-level progress for direct
policy learning. Across ten visual goal-conditioned control tasks, DTRC achieves strong control per-
formance and exhibits task-dependent long-range and directional structure together with consistent
local progress. Controlled comparisons further show that temporal supervision improves the same
flow-policy parameterization across the four LeWM tasks. These results support directed temporal
geometry as an effective interface between predictive visual representations and goal-conditioned
control.

BASELINES
All baselines operate on LeWM features extracted from current and goal images. The visual en-
coders are kept fixed during downstream training and evaluation. Below, we describe the control
models and the adaptations used in our comparison.
A.1
LEWM
We use the pretrained LeWM encoder and action-conditioned predictor with CEM-based model-
predictive control (Maes et al., 2026). Candidate action sequences are evaluated by rolling out the
predictor in latent space and comparing the predicted outcomes with the encoded goal. Control
proceeds in a receding-horizon manner using updated visual observations. We retain the original
latent-planning formulation without adding the temporal distance or policy-learning components of
DTRC.
A.2
GC-IDM
We use the goal-conditioned inverse-dynamics policy of GC-IDM (Nguyen et al., 2026). The pol-
icy takes the current LeWM feature, goal feature, and remaining horizon as inputs and predicts
the recorded action through a supervised regression objective. We retain its MLP architecture and
horizon-conditioning mechanism. For the additional OGBench tasks, the action output dimension is
adjusted to the corresponding environment. At evaluation time, the policy predicts actions directly
from the updated visual input without latent rollout or trajectory search.
A.3
GCBC
GCBC learns a direct goal-conditioned policy by supervised imitation (Ghosh et al., 2021). Future
observations from the same trajectory are sampled as goals, and an MLP predicts the recorded action
from the current feature, goal feature, and remaining horizon. Training minimizes the mean squared
action-prediction error. This baseline uses neither a value function nor advantage weighting. It is
distinct from the flow-matching behavioral-cloning variant used in our component comparisons.
A.4
GC-IQL
GC-IQL learns a goal-conditioned action-value function Q(zt, at, zg) and value function V (zt, zg)
(Kostrikov et al., 2022; Park et al., 2025a). The action-value function is trained by Bellman re-
gression using a target value network. The value function is trained by upper-expectile regression
toward the target action-value estimates for recorded actions. Goal relabeling supplies a reward of
zero at the relabeled goal and −1 otherwise, with bootstrapping terminated at the goal. We first train
the value networks and then extract a direct goal-conditioned policy using the weighted regression
procedure described below.
A.5
HILP
We adapt the Hilbert value parameterization of HILP (Park et al., 2024) to frozen LeWM features:

The main text visualizes the transformation from frozen LeWM features to the learned DTRC rep-

an approximately cyclic organization in both the control representation and directed potentials. Cube

the representation differently. Together with the PushT visualization in the main text, these exam-
ples show that DTRC does not impose a single low-dimensional geometric form across tasks. The
relevant common structure is instead defined by the induced state–goal temporal distance and its
control semantics.
Additional OGBench tasks.
We further visualize the same four representation spaces on the six
additional OGBench tasks. These tasks exhibit substantially different projected geometries, provid-
ing a broader qualitative view of the task-dependent representations learned by DTRC.
Across the ten tasks, the learned representation does not converge to a single common projected
geometry. Navigation, reaching, manipulation, and puzzle environments produce different organi-
zations in e, S(e), and A(e). In several tasks, the leading principal components of the frozen LeWM
features explain only a small fraction of the variance, while the learned control and distance-head
representations exhibit substantially more structured low-dimensional organization. The symmet-
ric and directed heads also differ markedly across tasks, consistent with their distinct roles in the
quasimetric and with the task-dependent directional statistics reported in the main text. These visu-
alizations therefore support interpreting DTRC through the induced state–goal temporal geometry
rather than through any single low-dimensional representation shape.

F.3
QUASIMETRIC DECOMPOSITION
We further examine the two components of the directed temporal quasimetric along recorded tra-
jectories. For each task, we evaluate pairwise distances between states from a recorded episode and
index source and goal states by normalized episode time. We separately visualize the symmetric
metric contribution, the order-sensitive directed contribution, and their sum. These matrices provide
a qualitative view of the structure induced by the two components of Eq. 4.
The decomposition varies substantially across tasks. On PushT, the pairwise structure of the to-
tal temporal distance is dominated by the order-sensitive component, consistent with the strong
forward–reverse asymmetry observed in the main-text diagnostics. TwoRoom, Reacher, and Cube
exhibit different relative contributions and pairwise structures. The directed component therefore
provides task-dependent order-sensitive structure rather than imposing a common asymmetric pat-
tern across environments. The symmetric component remains invariant to source–goal reversal,
while asymmetry in the total distance arises from the directed component by construction.
Additional OGBench tasks.
We provide the same quasimetric decomposition for the six addi-
tional visual OGBench tasks. As in the four LeWM tasks, each matrix evaluates pairwise temporal
distance along a recorded trajectory and separates the symmetric metric contribution from the order-
sensitive directed contribution.
Across the additional tasks, the relative contributions of the symmetric and directed components vary
substantially. PointMaze, Cube-Double, and Scene exhibit visible structure in both components,
while AntMaze-Medium, HumanoidMaze-Medium, and Puzzle-3x3 place comparatively greater
structure in the order-sensitive component. The total distance inherits these task-specific decom-
positions rather than following a common pairwise pattern across environments. Together with the
four LeWM tasks, these visualizations show that the metric–residual construction provides a com-
mon structural form while allowing the learned balance between symmetric and directed geometry
to adapt to the control problem.

TRAINING DYNAMICS
We visualize the evolution of the learned DTRC representation during training on the four LeWM
tasks. For each task, we show the control representation e, symmetric coordinates S(e), and directed
potentials A(e) at 0, 1,000, 10,000, 20,000, 60,000, and 200,000 training updates. Points are colored
by normalized offline episode time. These visualizations are intended to illustrate the development
of representation structure during optimization rather than to provide quantitative convergence cri-
teria.
The learned structures emerge progressively during optimization and differ substantially across
tasks. On TwoRoom, the control representation develops a curved geometry while the directed
potentials expand into a distinct order-sensitive structure. Reacher develops an approximately cyclic
control representation and directed-potential geometry. PushT and Cube exhibit different non-cyclic
organizations, consistent with the task-dependent final representations reported in Section F.1. These
visualizations indicate that DTRC does not converge toward a single common projected geometry
across environments.
Additional OGBench tasks.
We further visualize representation evolution on the six additional
OGBench tasks using the same checkpoints and PCA protocol. These results show that task-specific
geometric structure also emerges progressively beyond the four LeWM tasks.
Across the additional tasks, representation structure develops progressively during optimization
while remaining strongly task-dependent. PointMaze and AntMaze-Medium develop distinct non-
linear organizations in the control representation and directed potentials. Cube-Double and Puzzle-
3x3 form different structured manifolds, while Scene and HumanoidMaze-Medium exhibit more dif-
fuse projected organizations. The symmetric and directed heads also evolve differently across tasks.
These visualizations are therefore consistent with the final task-dependent geometries reported in
the representation analyses, rather than with convergence toward a single common projected form.
