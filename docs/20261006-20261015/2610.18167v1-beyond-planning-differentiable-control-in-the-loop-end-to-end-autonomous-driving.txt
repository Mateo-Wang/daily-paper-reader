Beyond Planning: Differentiable Control-in-the-loop
End-to-End Autonomous Driving
Zhidong Wang, Zirui Li, Zitong Shan, Zhiqi Mao, Qihang Huang,
Zhan Chen, Jingsong Liang, Han Yu, and Chen Lv
Abstract—End-to-end autonomous driving has achieved re-
markable progress by planning trajectories directly from raw
sensor data. Yet the step from planning to control still relies
on hand-tuned tracking controllers that block the gradient path
from vehicle motion back to the network. This gap stems from
non-differentiable vehicle dynamics, aliased supervision from
coarsely sampled expert labels, rate mismatch between high-rate
control and low-rate re-planning, and vehicle model discrep-
ancies between training and deployment. This paper proposes
DIVA (Differentiable Identified Vehicle dynamics for end-to-end
Autonomous driving) to close this gap. An identified actuator-
level vehicle model is embedded in the training loop of the
end-to-end driving network, forming a differentiable chain from
perception to vehicle states. A learning-based control decoder
replaces the external controller and is supervised on the rolled-
out vehicle states, bypassing the aliased control-input labels. A
rate-consistent control-input parameterization reconciles the het-
erogeneous sampling rates and enables asynchronous execution
with a 3.8 ms control-only cycle. On-policy re-identification and
an online disturbance observer address training-time distribution
shift and deployment-time model mismatch respectively. On the
closed-loop Bench2Drive benchmark, DIVA achieves a driving
score of 86.7, surpassing the widely adopted state-of-the-art
approach by 3.3 points while nearly halving mean jerk and
reducing brake switching by 64%, and maintains superior path
tracking accuracy under significant vehicle model mismatch at
deployment.
Index Terms—End-to-end autonomous driving, vehicle dy-
namics, system identification, differentiable simulation, motion
control, imitation learning.

II. RELATED WORK
A. The Planning-to-Control Interface in Autonomous Driving
In modular stacks, planning interfaces with control through
a reference trajectory tracked by geometric, PID, linear-
quadratic or model predictive controllers [5, 6], with modern
MPC designs incorporating actuator delays and tire limits [7].
In end-to-end stacks, the output representation determines
where this interface resides [8]: end-to-end networks output
control commands [9], trajectory–control fusions [10], tempo-
ral trajectories [11, 12], or geometric paths to be tracked [13],
where retuning controller gains alone can improve driving
performance significantly [14]. Yet the training always stops
at the planning output, and the learned reference trajectory
is state-conditioned, temporally inconsistent and reliable only
within the preview distance.
Several works extend the differentiable chain beyond the
planner through kinematic or nominal vehicle models: Diff-
Stack [15] employs a unicycle-based LQR, analytic pol-
icy gradients [16, 17] supervise through bicycle dynamics,
Rodr´ıguez-Vidal et al. [18] enable waypoint-space supervision
via a kinematic model, and DIPC [19] couples a differentiable
planner with a neural MPC through a six-state vehicle model
with Magic Formula tires. In all cases, the vehicle model
is kinematic or nominal with assumed parameters, without
actuator dynamics such as slew-rate limits or lags, and the
label, perception and control rates are not decoupled.
B. Learning-based Controllers Considering Model Mismatch
Reinforcement learning [20] and imitation learning [21] can
train end-to-end vehicle controllers, but the learned dynamics
are implicitly encoded in the network weights and cannot be
re-calibrated when the vehicle dynamics changes. The classical
remedy pairs a nominal model with an observer: unknown-
input observers generate steering commands [22], and adaptive
Kalman filters supply tire and friction estimates to hand-
designed controllers [23]. Hybrid neural–physical structures
increasingly identify the nominal model itself [24, 25]. On
the learning side, domain randomization tolerates a range
of vehicle dynamics characters [26], and continual meta-
learning adapts dynamics models online [27]. Explicit dis-
turbance estimates have been supplied to learned quadrotor
controllers [28], while for ground vehicles online adaptation
refines the dynamics model within an optimizer [29]; DIPC
examined certain vehicle parameter variation without retrain-
ing or adaptation [19].
These approaches remain disconnected: observer-based
methods serve rule-based controllers, learned controllers ab-
sorb mismatch through domain randomization, and online
adaptation refines the dynamics model rather than the con-
troller. No existing work feeds explicit mismatch estimates
to a learned controller within an end-to-end driving stack;
DIVA does so by unifying model identification, differentiable
planning and mismatch-aware control in a single trainable
framework.
III. METHODOLOGY
A. Overall Framework
The end-to-end autonomous driving networks are supervised
at the planning level; DIVA extends the differentiable compu-
tation graph to the vehicle-state level so that the training loss
directly reflects closed-loop execution performance.
1) Architecture Overview: Fig. 1 illustrates the overall
architecture. A perception backbone fuses camera and LiDAR
observations into perception tokens, from which a planning
decoder produces a reference route and a target speed dis-
tribution. A subsequent control decoder maps these planning
outputs together with state feedback and disturbance estimate
into control-input knots that are upsampled to the control rate
via first-order hold (FOH) interpolation. The identified vehicle

through the differentiable vehicle model: the state-loss gradient
back-propagates through the vehicle dynamics into the control
decoder and, modulated by a learnable gradient gate, further
into the planning decoder. At deployment, the control decoder
executes at every control step with cached perception features
and receives an online disturbance estimate, enabling real-time
mismatch-aware control.

B. Control Decoder
The learned planner regenerates its reference at every
perception update, conditioned on the current vehicle state
and reliable only within the preview horizon. A controller
that relies solely on this reference inherits its uncertainty.
To mitigate this limitation, the control decoder receives not
only the explicit reference but also the raw scene tokens and
the planner’s intermediate features, enabling compensation for
planning inaccuracies through richer contextual information.

produces planning tokens P, from which a GRU decodes the
route R and a softmax head outputs the speed distribution pv.

C. Identified Differentiable Vehicle Dynamics
The vehicle dynamics model F constitutes the sole differ-
entiable path from the control decoder’s output knots to the
predicted vehicle states; consequently, the controller implicitly
adapts its commands to the characteristics of this model. This
coupling imposes three requirements on F. First, the model
must faithfully capture the actuator dynamics that govern the
vehicle’s response to a given command. Second, it must pro-
vide well-defined gradients throughout the controller’s operat-
ing envelope, including at standstill. Third, it must generalize
beyond the identification excitations and remain accurate under
the command distribution induced by the trained controller. In
addition, F serves a fourth role by parameterizing the vehicle-
side model mismatch for online estimation.
1) Model Structure: The state of the model is
z = [X, Y, ψ, vx, vy, r, ˜cs, ˜ct, ˜cb, ad, ab, χ]⊤,
(5)
which comprises the vehicle pose (X, Y, ψ), the longitudinal
and lateral velocities vx and vy, the yaw rate r, and the actuator
states: the rate-limited steering, throttle and brake inputs ˜cs,
˜ct and ˜cb, the lagged powertrain and brake accelerations ad
and ab, and a wheel-lock indicator χ ∈{0, 1}.
The model explicitly represents the actuator chain from
commanded input to vehicle response. Each command first
passes through the vehicle’s slew-rate limiter, which enforces
the physical rate constraints on steering, throttle and brake.
The powertrain acceleration ad is governed by a static map
A(vx, ˜ct), filtered through a first-order lag; this map encodes
the steady-state acceleration on a flat road, accounting for road
load, engine braking and the gear selected by the transmission
shift schedule at the current speed. The brake acceleration ab
is similarly modeled as a constant deceleration abrk < 0:
ax,k = ad,k+1 + ab,k+1 −g sin γk,
(6)
where g is the gravitational acceleration and γk is the road
grade angle, positive uphill. Two discrete regimes, a wheel
lock in the first gear and a standstill hold under braking,
complete the longitudinal channel. In the lateral channel, the
steering input sets the road-wheel angle through a speed-
dependent steering limit and the Ackermann geometry. A
single-track model with saturating lateral tire forces yields vy
and r, and blends into the kinematic single-track model at low
speed.
The model advances by one control period Ts per step and
is formulated directly in discrete time rather than as a numer-
ical discretization of continuous-time dynamics; empirically,
reducing the integration step below Ts yields no improvement
in open-loop accuracy on held-out drives (Fig. 2(f)). This
discrete-time formulation also motivates the design choice:
the control-input knots are interpolated to the control rate and
integrated at that rate, rather than integrated at the coarser
label rate. The model parameters are identified through a three-
tier procedure in which each tier is fitted with all preceding
tiers held fixed: specification-level parameters are drawn from
the vehicle datasheet, calibration-level parameters are obtained
from constant-input, step and coast-down experiments, and the
remaining parameters are identified from step-steer and swept-
sine maneuvers. Appendix A provides the complete model
equations, the identification protocol, the resulting parameter
values, and a validation against measured vehicle data.
2) Gradients Through Non-Smooth Actuator Characteris-
tics: Several elements of F are non-smooth: the split of ux
into throttle and brake, the floor [·]+ on the speed, the dead
band of A, the discrete regimes, the slew-rate limiter and the
clipping of the commands. A zero gradient there would stop
learning exactly where the vehicle launches and stops. The
split and the floor therefore use a leaky straight-through esti-
mator [32]: the forward pass computes [x]+, and the backward
pass uses the slope 1 for x > 0 and ϵst = 0.1 otherwise.
Gradients thus still flow at standstill and in the dead band. The
indicators of the discrete regimes are detached in the backward
pass, and the limiter and the clipping keep the subgradient.
The Ackermann relation is implemented in a form that is
continuously differentiable at zero steering (Appendix A). A
finite-difference check confirms the analytical gradients with
respect to the knots.

D. Dynamics-in-the-Loop Imitation Learning
With the differentiable chain from control decoder to ve-
hicle states now defined, this section addresses the training
procedure: the supervision signal and loss formulation, the
strategies that ensure closed-loop validity and robustness to
model mismatch, and the joint training of the controller with
the planner.
1) State-Space
Supervision
With
Identifiability-Aware
Control-Input Anchor: The design of the supervision signal
is governed by two properties of the expert demonstrations.
First, the expert commands are recorded at a rate lower than
the control rate, so the raw control-input labels suffer from
temporal aliasing and cannot faithfully reproduce the expert

expert states via gradient-based inversion yield substantially
more accurate trajectories. Second, the longitudinal command

any command within the pedal dead band produces zero
acceleration and leaves the vehicle state unchanged.

expert. The indicator ι ∈{0, 1} activates the asymmetry
(κ = 3) in braking situations; the minimizer is then the
1/(2 + κ) quantile, so the roll-out commits to stopping
once the stop-class probability exceeds 1/(2+κ) = 0.2. A
braking situation is flagged when the vehicle is moving
and either (i) a hazard has been flagged or braked for
within the horizon while the speed reference signals a
stop, or (ii) an emergency brake has occurred within 0.5 s.
An auxiliary head predicts the emergency-braking flags
with a binary cross-entropy loss Lhaz.
• Causal state feedback. Including acceleration states in
the feedback token introduces a causal shortcut [34], since
these states effectively encode the previous command and
allow the network to copy rather than predict it. This
shortcut manifests after each upshift torque interruption,
where the closed loop settles into a self-confirming low-
acceleration equilibrium. The state-feedback token is
therefore restricted to vx and ravg, where the yaw rate
is computed via a backward finite difference to preserve
strict causality; a central difference would implicitly leak
the next expert state into the current observation.
• Recovery samples. With probability 0.3, the initial speed
of both the roll-out and o is perturbed while the targets
remain the expert states, so that the controller learns speed
feedback from off-trajectory states without additional
sensor data.
• Beyond-preview reference distrust. With probability
0.5, the reference route beyond the preview distance is
shifted along its normal by an offset that grows quadrat-
ically from zero at the preview boundary to a random
value at the final point, so that the controller learns to
rely on its own scene understanding.

reproduce the expert behavior under mismatch. The controller

E. Asynchronous Execution and Online Disturbance Estima-
tion
During training, the controller is evaluated on open-loop
roll-outs initialized from recorded states, with perception avail-
able at every step. At deployment, however, two additional
challenges arise: the control decoder must execute at every
control step while perception updates arrive at a lower rate,
and the deployed vehicle inevitably deviates from the nominal
dynamics model.
1) Asynchronous Execution: Because perception cannot run

relative route are recomputed at every control step. Between
perception updates, the cached route is transformed to the

2) Online Disturbance Observer:
The lumped devia-
tions defined in Eq. (8) are estimated online by an aug-
mented unscented Kalman filter (UKF). The filter state is
[vx, vy, r, bs, dx]⊤, with speed and yaw rate serving as mea-
surements. The process model consists of one control step
of F evaluated at the nominal parameters θ0, in which
¯bs and ¯dx are augmented as random-walk states; the filter
estimates of these augmented states thus converge to ¯bs and
¯dx. The road grade γ is set to zero in the process model
so that its effect is absorbed into the longitudinal deviation
dx. To prevent uninformative measurements from degrading
the estimates, each deviation is frozen in operating regimes
where the corresponding measurement channel carries negli-
gible observability, such as low-speed steering for ¯bs or near-
standstill braking for ¯dx. Additionally, a speed innovation
exceeding Ts times the maximum tire-limited acceleration
triggers a temporary freeze of both estimates, safeguarding the
filter against corruption from collision events. The resulting
estimates ˆbs and ˆdx are assembled into the disturbance token
d = [ˆbs, ˆdx, 1]⊤that the control decoder receives as input.
Two quantities are deliberately excluded from the estimation:
tire-road friction, which is observable from speed and yaw rate
only during the brief transients in which the tires saturate and
is therefore not reliably estimable in steady driving, and the
brake gain deviation, which is not separately identifiable and
enters the filter through ¯dx.
IV. EXPERIMENTS
The experiments address four questions: (1) does extending
the differentiable chain to vehicle control improve closed-
loop driving and execution quality? (2) which components of
DIVA account for the effect? (3) how does the model behave
under sustained vehicle dynamics mismatch, compared with
classical controllers that use the same identified model and
observer? (4) what does the control loop cost, and does it
remain effective when perception runs slower than the control
rate? Section IV-F presents case studies.
A. Experimental Setup and Baselines
1) Benchmark and Training Data: All closed-loop experi-
ments use the Bench2Drive-220 benchmark [35] with its offi-
cial metrics: driving score (DS), success rate (SR), efficiency,
comfortness and five ability scores. Training follows [30] on
the PDM-Lite dataset [36], recorded at 4 Hz, with the same
route filter and data split; the perception backbone is pretrained
as in [30] and frozen.
2) Evaluation
Subsets:
The
ablation,
mismatch
and
perception-rate studies use Hard-52, a subset of 52 routes
from Bench2Drive-220 that emphasize lateral and longitudinal
control complexity: 23 lateral maneuvers, 13 junction turns,
12 merging or highway routes and 4 emergency-braking
routes, fixed before any ablation run. On the remaining routes,
DIVA and DIVA+PID are nearly tied (92.14 vs. 91.20 DS).
The model mismatch study additionally uses a traffic-free
maneuver suite of 16 routes grouped into six families (junction
turns, highway, lane changes, stop-and-go, emergency braking,
curves). Background traffic and scenario actors are removed
except in the emergency-braking family; the evaluation is then
nearly deterministic, so tracking metrics resolve the controllers
and a single trial per condition suffices.
3) Shared Stack and Controllers: All configurations share
the same backbone, rules and safety logic of [30]; only the
planner and the controller change. Two planners are used: the
baseline planner (trained alone) and the joint planner (trained
together with the control decoder of DIVA). The compared
configurations are:
• TF++ [30]: the baseline planner with its PID, the
most widely used controller of closed-loop driving in
Bench2Drive: a lateral PID towards a look-ahead point
and a longitudinal PID with full-brake and saturating-
throttle branches.
• MPC+DOB: the baseline planner with an MPC that
shares the identified model, the disturbance observer
(DOB) of Section III-E2, the parameterization and the
horizon of the control decoder, solved at every control
step by MPPI [37] with 1024 roll-outs; it optimizes a
hand-designed tracking cost online instead of applying a
learned mapping.
• DIVA+PID: the joint planner with the PID of TF++,
which isolates the effect of joint training.
• DOB-PID: the PID of TF++ extended with the DOB,
which subtracts the estimated steering bias and cancels
the longitudinal disturbance via the identified map.
• DIVA w/o observer: the full DIVA network with the
estimate withheld (ν = 0).
In the mismatch study, all controllers use the joint planner.
Control-input behavior cloning is treated as an ablation.
4) Metrics and Statistics: Beyond the official metrics, ex-
ecution quality is quantified by dynamics metrics (mean jerk,
mean |ax|, peak deceleration and lateral acceleration, sideslip
angle, comfort-limit exceedances) and actuator-activity and
tracking metrics (wide-open-throttle (WOT) fraction, throttle
variation, brake switches, steering rate, speed error, lateral
offset), all macro-averaged over routes. Variants are compared
by paired per-route differences with a 95% CI.

Four variants incur a statistically significant DS loss (boot-

significant DS loss, confirming that minimizing the control-

tripled timeouts rather than collisions. Fifth, the observer adds
no measurable benefit at nominal conditions; its value emerges
under strong mismatch.

V. CONCLUSION
DIVA bridges the planning-to-control gap in end-to-end
autonomous driving by jointly training the controller through
identified differentiable vehicle dynamics. Supervising on
states rather than commands decouples the demonstrations
from the recording vehicle’s actuators: a dedicated controller
can be trained for any identified vehicle on the same demon-
stration set in a single run, without per-vehicle controller-
tuning. The identified model and observer are independently
valuable, already conferring mismatch robustness on classical
controllers, while the learned controller adds smooth, actuator-
aware execution at the control rate with a sub-millisecond
control-only cycle decoupled from perception latency. On
Bench2Drive, DIVA achieves a higher driving score than the
most widely adopted baseline with substantially smoother ex-
ecution. The ablations confirm that task performance requires
a vehicle dynamics model, execution quality depends on its
fidelity, and closed-loop viability demands rate consistency
across control-input parameterization, supervision, and execu-
tion. Future work will transfer the framework to real vehicles,
add observer channels for friction and brake effectiveness, and
extend this method into wider end-to-end paradigms.
