ABSTRACT
Driving world action models (WAMs) learn from paired trajectories and future
observations. Post-training with reinforcement learning allows the policy to ex-
plore alternative trajectories and improve planning using driving rewards. How-
ever, driving logs contain future videos only for the recorded trajectories, leaving
these alternatives without corresponding visual supervision. Thus, we introduce
SIMVTA, a simulation-grounded post-training framework that combines trajectory
optimization with video supervision. First, we sample candidate trajectories for
each observation and use a real-world simulator (e.g., World Engine) to render
future videos for high-reward candidates in real-world scenes reconstructed with
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS). This provides trajectory-matched supervision in-
dependent of the model’s own predictions. Under a limited rendering budget,
we prioritize video supervision for higher-error futures among these rendered
candidates. Finally, we optimize trajectory planning with Group Relative Policy
Optimization (GRPO) and use a flow-matching loss on the matched videos to
update shared representations. At inference time, the model predicts trajectories
directly, without simulator calls. On NAVSIM v1, applying SIMVTA to a WAM
initialization improves PDMS from 86.2 to 91.3 (+5.1). It achieves 88.2 EPDMS
on NAVSIM v2 and an average HD-Score of 38.8 in closed-loop evaluation on
HUGSIM. Ablations show that updating shared representations with video supervi-
sion improves planning, while prioritizing higher-error futures among high-reward
trajectories further improves performance. The results and demo are available at
https://github.com/OpenDriveLab/SimVTA.

INTRODUCTION
Driving WAMs combine future-scene prediction with trajectory planning for the ego vehicle. Pre-
dicting future frames provides dense visual supervision that complements sparse trajectory targets
and helps models learn representations for planning (Li et al., 2025b). Recent driving models re-
tain video prediction during training but predict trajectories directly at inference time, avoiding the
computational cost of future-video generation (Zhao et al., 2026).
Reinforcement learning can refine a planner trained on demonstrations to explore alternative trajecto-
ries and learn from driving rewards (Zhou et al., 2025). However, driving logs contain future videos
only for recorded trajectories. For example, when a candidate turns while the recorded trajectory
continues straight, the logged video does not show the visual consequences. Reusing such videos
creates incorrect trajectory-video pairs, while omitting video training leaves these candidates without
visual supervision. This creates a supervision gap during post-training: current-policy trajectories
lack matched future-video targets (Figure 1).

To address this gap, we introduce SIMVTA, a simulation-grounded framework for video-trajectory
alignment. A WAM’s own video predictions provide no external reference for checking whether they
follow a candidate trajectory. We use the 3DGS-based simulator in World Engine (Li et al., 2026c) to
render reconstructed real-world scenes from camera poses along executed candidate paths (Figure 1).
These renderings provide prediction-independent targets that match the scene geometry and motion
of each selected trajectory, establishing video-trajectory alignment.
We jointly train video prediction with flow matching and trajectory planning with GRPO, allowing
the video loss to update shared representations. The former teaches the model to predict the visual
consequences of candidate trajectories; the latter compares driving rewards among candidates and
favors higher-reward trajectories.
Rendering videos for every sampled trajectory is costly. We first select high-reward candidates for
rendering, then prioritize video supervision for higher-error futures among the rendered candidates.
This separates trajectory exploration from video-supervision target selection. Our ablations examine
shared representation updates, target selection, trajectory-video correspondence, and online versus
offline rendering. At inference, the planner predicts trajectories directly, without simulator calls or
future-video generation.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We introduce SIMVTA, a post-training framework that combines trajectory GRPO with simulator-
based video supervision. It connects policy sampling, trajectory execution, rendering, and joint
updates to learn the visual consequences of actions beyond those recorded in driving logs.
• We introduce a video-target selection strategy based on trajectory reward and video prediction
error. It prioritizes higher-error futures of high-reward trajectories for video supervision without
restricting trajectory exploration.
• Applying SIMVTA to a WAM initialization improves NAVSIM v1 PDMS by 5.1 points, from 86.2
to 91.3, and achieves 88.2 EPDMS on NAVSIM v2 and an average HD-Score of 38.8 on HUGSIM.

Video prediction can also be retained during training and omitted at inference, as explored in robotics
by Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026) and in driving by SimWAM (Zhao et al., 2026). SIMVTA focuses
on trajectory-matched video supervision during driving WAM post-training.
Simulation for Driving.
Driving simulators support policy training and evaluation through con-
trolled rollouts (Dosovitskiy et al., 2017; Li et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2024; Cusumano-Towner et al.,
2025; NVIDIA et al., 2025a; Tian et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026c; He et al., 2026). 3DGS (Kerbl et al.,
2023) renders reconstructed scenes from specified camera poses, providing visual observations for
closed-loop simulation (Zhou et al., 2024; NVIDIA et al., 2025a). World Engine (Li et al., 2026c)
renders these observations along simulated ego motion for policy post-training. CausalDrive (Yan
et al., 2026) instead uses a learned renderer. SIMVTA uses World Engine to render video targets
along selected candidate trajectories.
Post-Training for Driving.
Driving policies can be refined after initial training through reinforce-
ment learning or model adaptation (Wang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a; Yang et al., 2025b; Tang
et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026d; Zhou et al., 2025; NVIDIA et al., 2025b; Li et al., 2026a). GRPO (Shao
et al., 2024) has been applied to both diffusion and autoregressive planners (Li et al., 2026d; Tang
et al., 2026). PlannerRFT (Li et al., 2026a) uses closed-loop reinforcement fine-tuning with adaptive
exploration to improve diffusion planners. Our trajectory optimization uses the stochastic differential
equation (SDE) sampling formulation from Flow-GRPO (Liu et al., 2025). ReSim (Yang et al.,
2025a) trains a video simulator on simulated non-expert trajectories to improve action controllability.
SIMVTA jointly updates shared representations through video prediction and trajectory GRPO.

METHOD
SIMVTA’s post-training framework is illustrated in Figure 2 and comprises trajectory sampling,
video rendering, and video supervision. We sample candidate trajectories from the current policy
and execute them to obtain paths and driving rewards. World Engine (Li et al., 2026c) renders
videos along selected paths during training. We select video targets based on trajectory reward and
video prediction error, then update the model with trajectory GRPO and video supervision. The next
sampling round uses the updated policy.

VIDEO RENDERING
We first check candidate execution validity, motion-conditioning support, and safety, then rank
eligible candidates by Ri. World Engine renders the highest-reward candidates within the rendering
budget K. These checks determine eligibility for video supervision and do not remove candidates
from trajectory learning. For each selected candidate, the simulator S renders a target video along its
executed path, and the video model Wϕ predicts the future along the same path:

the rendered target and the prediction correspond to the same ego motion.
Rendering during training allows visual supervision to follow changes in the policy. A fixed offline
cache contains videos only for trajectories sampled before optimization, whereas policy updates
continuously change the trajectory distribution explored during post-training. The stored videos
remain valid for their original paths, but do not provide matching targets for new paths with different
motions or viewpoints. In each sampling round, SIMVTA renders videos for selected candidates
from the current policy, maintaining trajectory-video correspondence as training proceeds.

Target Selection.
We first select candidates for rendering by trajectory reward, then select rendered
videos for supervision by prediction error (Figure 2(c)). For each valid rendered candidate, we
compute the teacher-forced latent prediction error ETF
i

mean over valid rendered candidates. This subset, denoted Q2, contains high-reward, high-error
candidates. Their matched videos serve as targets for the flow-matching loss in Eq. 4, focusing
supervision on visual consequences that the model predicts less accurately.

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Implementation Details.
We initialize SIMVTA from an Epona checkpoint adapted to NAVSIM
as described in Appendix A, and perform post-training on NavTrain samples with available World
Engine scene reconstructions. We use the 3DGS-based simulator in World Engine (Li et al., 2026c) to
render matched future videos. In SIMVTA (Exp2), the video flow-matching loss uses Q2 targets and
updates both the video network (VideoDiT) and the shared encoder. All ablations retain trajectory
GRPO and the BC regularizer, with trajectory GRPO updating the shared encoder throughout. The
mixed high/low-reward variants, Exp4 and Exp6, additionally apply the safety-specific advantage
treatment described in Appendix D.
Benchmarks and Metrics.
We evaluate SIMVTA on the NavTest splits of NAVSIM v1 and v2
(Dauner et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2025), using PDMS and EPDMS, respectively, to assess safety,
progress, and comfort. On HUGSIM (Zhou et al., 2024), we report route completion (RC) and HD-
Score, which combines RC with safety and comfort. Training uses the reward in Eq. 6; Benchmark
scores follow their respective evaluation protocols. We report results averaged over three independent
runs with different random seeds. Metric details are provided in Appendix F.

4.2
MAIN RESULTS
NAVSIM.
Starting from the NAVSIM-adapted Epona initialization at 86.2 PDMS, SIMVTA reaches
91.3 (+5.1) on NAVSIM v1 (Figure 3a; Table 1). Ego progress rises from 80.3 to 86.7, time-to-
collision compliance from 93.9 to 97.0, and drivable-area compliance from 95.0 to 97.2. On NAVSIM
v2, SIMVTA improves EPDMS from 85.0 to 88.2 (+3.2), and achieves 90.8 ego progress and 98.5
for both drivable-area and time-to-collision compliance (Table 2).
SIMVTA also improves planning in scenes where the Epona initialization fails (Figure 4). In these
examples, Epona’s trajectories lead to a collision or leave the drivable area, whereas SIMVTA avoids
the collision and stays within the drivable area.
HUGSIM.
We further evaluate the final planner in closed-loop driving on the same 345 pre-
challenge HUGSIM scenarios, using the same corrected evaluator and controller as Kirby et al.
(2026). SIMVTA achieves the highest average RC (51.5) and HD-Score (38.8) among the compared
methods, exceeding the reported DrivoR results by 1.7 and 3.1 points, respectively (Table 3). It also
leads on both metrics in hard scenarios, reaching 38.3 RC and 23.3 HD-Score.
Video-Trajectory Alignment.
Beyond the planning gains, we compare SIMVTA with Epona to
examine whether post-training improves the consistency of predicted videos with the scene along a
trajectory (Figure 6). Epona generates an additional vehicle on the right (highlighted by red boxes)
that is absent from both the bird’s-eye view (BEV) and the corresponding World Engine renderings.
SIMVTA avoids this inconsistency in the illustrated scene.

4.3
ABLATION STUDIES
Updating the Shared Representations.
Video supervision improves planning when its gradients
reach the shared encoder. Restricting video gradients to VideoDiT (Exp1) yields 89.1 PDMS,
matching trajectory GRPO alone (Exp0; Figure 3a). Allowing video gradients to also update the
shared encoder (Exp2) raises PDMS to 91.3 (+2.2) (Table 5).
Selecting Informative Futures.
We compare video supervision across four candidate groups: Q1
(high reward, low error), Q2 (high reward, high error), Q3 (low reward, low error), and Q4 (low
reward, high error). High- and low-reward candidates come from the top and bottom of the trajectory
reward ranking, respectively. Within each scene, errors below the mean over valid rendered candidates
are low; those at or above it are high.
Q2 supervision yields the highest PDMS among the four settings. Exp2 uses Q2 supervision and
achieves 91.3 PDMS. Exp5 uses the same high-reward rendering pool but expands video supervision
to Q1+Q2, yielding 91.0 PDMS. Under the same high-reward rendering pool, focusing supervision
on the higher-error Q2 subset performs better than supervising Q1+Q2. Exp4 and Exp6 use mixed
high/low-reward pools with Q2+Q3 and Q1–Q4 supervision, respectively, both achieving 90.9
PDMS (Table 6). These two variants also apply the safety-specific advantage treatment described in
Appendix D.

CONCLUSION
We presented SIMVTA, a simulation-grounded post-training framework that combines trajectory
GRPO with video supervision. Rendering futures along current-policy trajectories provides visual
targets beyond logged action-future pairs and jointly trains shared representations through video
prediction and trajectory optimization. The full framework improves NAVSIM v1 PDMS from 86.2
to 91.3 (+5.1) and achieves 88.2 EPDMS on NAVSIM v2 and 38.8 average HD-Score on HUGSIM.
Ablations show that updating shared representations with video supervision improves planning, while
prioritizing higher-error futures among high-reward trajectories further improves performance.
Limitations and directions for future work are discussed in Appendix G.

A
TRAINING PROCEDURE
Before applying SIMVTA, we fine-tune the nuPlan-pretrained Epona checkpoint on NAVSIM
following the official adaptation recipe to adapt it from 10 Hz to 2 Hz. The adapted checkpoint
initializes both Exp0 and Exp2. We evaluate it using the same NAVSIM v1 protocol as the post-
trained models and obtain 86.2 PDMS, matching the overall score reported for Epona. Unless stated
otherwise, the implementation details below describe Exp2.
Training Loop.
1. Encode observation histories and sample G trajectories, retaining their normalized denoising
states.
2. Execute each candidate with PDM, compute its training reward, and normalize rewards
within each observation group.
3. Check execution validity, pose support, and safety. Rank eligible candidates by reward and
select the rendering pool.
4. Render the selected paths and encode their future frames, retaining the candidate-to-video
correspondence.
5. Compute detached teacher-forced errors and classify the valid rendered candidates into
quadrants.
6. Select the video-training subset and future frame for this update. Generate the detached BC
teacher chain from the current shared representation.
7. Combine the trajectory loss, selected video loss, and BC regularizer. Update the trainable
parameters and repeat with the updated policy.
Architecture and Temporal Settings.
Epona’s spatiotemporal Transformer is the shared encoder,
paired with TrajDiT and VideoDiT. Four observed frames condition eight trajectory waypoints
spanning four seconds. PDM evaluates the path at 10 Hz; video pose conditions are sampled at 2 Hz.
Exp2 computes teacher-forced prediction errors over future frames within the planning window and
trains one sampled future frame per update.
Trajectory Reward.
Each candidate is scored together with the cached PDM reference trajectory.

Gradient Paths and Candidate Pools.
Exp0 uses trajectory GRPO and BC without video supervi-
sion. In Exp1, video gradients update only VideoDiT; trajectory and BC gradients still update the
shared encoder. Exp2 also allows video gradients to update shared parameters. Both use Q2 targets.
Exp3 shuffles rendered targets across trajectory conditions within a scene after selection. Scenes
without a valid target permutation contribute no shuffled video supervision.
Exp4 selects the two highest- and two lowest-reward candidates from the execution- and pose-valid
pool. If this pool contains n < 4 candidates, the high and low subsets contain ⌈n/2⌉and ⌊n/2⌋
candidates, respectively. The subsets are disjoint, with reward ties broken by sampling index. Exp4
applies the four safety checks before training on Q2 and Q3. Exp5 renders four safe high-reward
candidates and trains on all valid Q1 and Q2 candidates. Exp6 uses the same high/low pool as
Exp4 but trains on all valid rendered candidates, including individually unsafe candidates. In Exp4
and Exp6, an unsafe candidate’s positive trajectory advantage is additionally truncated to zero:
Ai ←min(Ai, 0). Exp2 does not apply this projection. Exp7 increases the rendering budget from
four to eight while retaining Exp2’s Q2 selection and video-training rule. Both settings train on all
valid Q2 candidates. Table 12 distinguishes the rendered pool from the subset used for video training.
Fixed Offline Cache (Exp9).
Exp9 follows the same experimental settings as Exp2, except that
trajectories are sampled offline from the initial model and their rendered videos are stored in a
fixed cache. The cached trajectory-video pairs are reused throughout post-training. All other
settings, including candidate selection, rendering budget, training objectives, and optimization
hyperparameters, are identical to Exp2. Exp9 achieves 90.4 PDMS, compared with 89.1 for Exp0
and 91.3 for Exp2 (Table 8).

Video GRPO Control.
Exp8 evaluates video GRPO under the same four-candidate rendering
budget used by Exp2. Exp8 applies video GRPO to all valid rendered high-reward candidates,
whereas Exp2 first selects the Q2 subset and optimizes the selected targets with flow matching. For
each candidate, it generates future latents with five stochastic Euler steps and noise scale 0.3, using
preceding target frames as context. The reward is the negative framewise latent MSE, aggregated
with normalized temporal weights proportional to 0.85r−1. It does not include the motion-weighted
temporal-change term in Eq. 9. Different trajectory conditions within the same scene form the video
comparison group. The video policy objective uses group-normalized advantages and a clipped ratio
computed from dimension-normalized Gaussian log densities, with clipping parameter 0.2. One
future frame is replayed for optimization per update, cycling through the video horizon. Groups with
fewer than two valid candidates or reward standard deviation below 10−6 contribute no video policy
update. Video GRPO replaces video flow matching and updates VideoDiT and shared parameters; its
reward does not enter trajectory advantages.
Rendering and Optimization Budgets.
K specifies the number of candidates budgeted for render-
ing; Table 12 separately lists those used for video training. Error-based selection follows rendering
and scoring, so these costs apply to every rendered candidate. Total training cost also includes
trajectory sampling, PDM evaluation, video optimization, and optimizer updates. Comparisons at
equal K match rendering caps, while video optimization costs depend on the training subset and
objective. Exp9 renders its cache once; Exp2 renders candidates at each sampling round.
Interpreting the Ablations.
Table 12 summarizes the supervision configurations underlying the
main-text ablations. For Exp3, scenes without a valid within-scene target permutation contribute no
shuffled video supervision. Exp2 and Exp5 compare Q2 versus Q1+Q2 supervision from the same
rendering pool, although the number of video-training candidates differs. Exp4 and Exp6 additionally
modify the safety-specific treatment of trajectory advantages and are therefore interpreted as broader
configuration comparisons.

CROSS-BENCHMARK RESULTS
Evaluation Metrics and Scope.
PDMS combines collision and drivable-area terms with progress,
TTC, and comfort. EPDMS additionally evaluates driving direction, traffic lights, lane keeping, and
further comfort measures. On HUGSIM, HD-Score combines safety and comfort over the rollout
and scales the result by RC. Higher scores are better. We evaluate SIMVTA and the NAVSIM-
adapted Epona initialization on the full NAVSIM v2 NavTest set using the NAVSIM v2.2 devkit with
single-stage EPDMS evaluation, LQR tracking, and non-reactive traffic agents.
Result Sources and Comparison Scope.
Figure 7 summarizes the NAVSIM and HUGSIM results.
On NAVSIM v1, the full framework improves PDMS by 5.1 points over the initialization (86.2 to
91.3). The comparison with Exp0 (89.1 to 91.3) measures the additional gain when video supervision
is added within the framework. NAVSIM v2 and HUGSIM extend the evaluation to additional
planning criteria and closed-loop driving. On NAVSIM v2, the NAVSIM-adapted Epona initialization
obtains 85.0 EPDMS under the same evaluation protocol as SIMVTA, which reaches 88.2 (+3.2).
This aggregate improvement coexists with lower traffic-light compliance (99.7 to 99.0) and extended
comfort (67.6 to 60.0) (Table 2). Baseline HUGSIM results in Table 3 are taken from Kirby et al.
(2026). SIMVTA achieves the highest reported averages among these methods and leads on hard
scenarios, while DrivoR has higher extreme-scenario RC and HD-Score (47.1/32.5 versus 40.1/27.2).
These closed-loop results assess the final planner; isolating the post-training gain on HUGSIM
additionally requires the initialization and Exp0 under the same routes, controller, and rollout settings.

LIMITATIONS
Reconstruction Quality and Coverage.
Occlusions and limited pose coverage can degrade render-
ings away from logged paths, while renderer metrics assess only logged viewpoints. Even with safety
and pose-support checks, rendering artifacts can still appear. A high prediction error can stem from
either model limitations or poor-quality simulation targets. Quality-aware filtering and evaluation at
alternative poses remain necessary.

Distribution Shifts.
Current-policy trajectories broaden action coverage, but observation histories
remain log-based and rendered targets can differ from real images. Reconstruction coverage and
candidate filtering further limit supervision. Teacher-forced selection cannot capture autoregressive
error accumulation. We leave robustness evaluation under shifted observation histories and reactive
traffic (Jia et al., 2024; 2026b) for future work.
Architecture and Training Cost.
We validate our framework based on the Epona backbone. Gen-
eralization to alternative WAM architectures is left for future work. Online rendering, error scoring,
and video optimization increase training cost, while inference uses direct trajectory prediction.
