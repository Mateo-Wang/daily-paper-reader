Abstract
Reinforcement Learning (RL) substantially improves planning
performance in autonomous-driving Vision-Language-Action
(VLA) models. However, existing RL methods rely on scalar
trajectory rewards that measure overall quality but fail to iden-
tify specific planning errors or provide actionable correction
signals. In long-tail scenarios, errors in scene understanding,
behavior selection, and trajectory execution are compressed
into a single score. We observe that Reflective Guidance turns
trajectory evaluation into explicit diagnoses and corrective
instructions, substantially improving planning accuracy. Yet
such guidance requires an initial prediction and its evalua-
tion results, making it unavailable for direct single-pass infer-
ence. We propose Reflect-VLA, which treats Reflective Guid-
ance as training-time privileged information and internal-
izes its planning benefits through Dual-Path On-Policy Self-
Distillation (Dual-OPSD). Unlike scalar-reward optimiza-
tion, Dual-OPSD uses the driving score only to route student-
generated trajectories. It distills dense token-level corrective
distributions from the teacher for risky trajectories while ap-
plying self-imitation to preserve reliable ones. To provide re-
liable corrective distributions on student rollouts, the teacher
is progressively constructed through Driving-Knowledge Pre-
training, Planning-and-Refinement SFT, and Self-Refinement
Rejection Sampling. At inference time, Reflect-VLA gener-
ates trajectories in a single pass without explicit guidance
or iterative refinement. Experiments on NAVSIM v1/v2 and
Bench2Drive demonstrate competitive planning performance
while reducing training time by over 90% compared with RL.

Introduction
Autonomous driving is shifting from conventional modular
pipelines toward end-to-end Vision-Language-Action (VLA)
systems (Jiang et al. 2025b). Unlike modular systems (Hu
et al. 2023; Jiang et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2024) that coordinate
perception, prediction, and planning through handcrafted
interfaces, VLA models integrate visual observations, lan-
guage instructions and action generation within a unified
architecture. Recent driving VLA methods commonly adopt
a multi-stage training paradigm (Li et al. 2025c; Zhou et al.
2025b; Luo et al. 2025a, 2026b; Lu et al. 2026). They first ac-
quire domain knowledge from large-scale driving question-
answering data, then learn trajectory generation through su-
pervised fine-tuning, and finally improve the policy using

Despite progress, VLA models struggle to learn from plan-
ning failures, especially in complex and long-tail scenarios.
A scalar planning score can identify a poor trajectory but can-
not reveal whether the error arises from scene understanding,
behavior selection, or trajectory execution, nor indicate how

to correct it. Trajectory-level rewards collapse these hetero-
geneous failure modes into a single value, leaving the model
without a direction for improvement. ELF-VLA (Luo et al.
2026a) alleviates this problem by introducing structured fail-
ure information, showing that detailed feedback can guide
the correction of failed trajectories. However, how to con-
vert feedback-assisted correction into an intrinsic single-pass
planning capability remains underexplored.
We begin with a key observation: Reflective Guidance
provides substantial planning gains. As illustrated in Fig. 1,
a VLA model may produce an incorrect trajectory during di-
rect inference, yet generate a markedly better trajectory when
provided with evaluation-derived diagnoses and corrective
suggestions (Yang et al. 2026; He et al. 2026; Chen et al.
2026b). Constructing the guidance requires both an initial
trajectory and its evaluation results, whereas an autonomous
driving system must produce an executable plan before act-
ing. The central challenge is therefore not to perform itera-
tive refinement at inference time, but to transfer the planning
preferences induced by Reflective Guidance into the model’s
direct trajectory-generation policy.
To this end, we propose Reflect-VLA, a training frame-
work that treats Reflective Guidance as privileged informa-
tion and internalizes its planning benefits through Dual-Path
On-Policy Self-Distillation (Dual-OPSD). Starting from
the same VLA checkpoint, the model supplied with Reflec-
tive Guidance acts as the frozen teacher, while the model
receiving standard inputs acts as the student. The student
first generates trajectories from the input available at deploy-
ment. A planning score then routes each trajectory to one of
two learning paths. For risky or suboptimal trajectories, the
teacher provides dense token-level distributions that encode
corrective planning preferences. For reliable trajectories, a
self-imitation objective preserves the student’s existing deci-
sions. The scalar score is therefore used only for trajectory-
quality-aware routing, while the teacher distribution supplies
the fine-grained supervision required for correction.
Effective self-distillation requires a teacher that can re-
fine trajectories drawn from the student’s rollout distribu-
tion. We construct this teacher using a progressive strategy
comprising Driving-Knowledge Pretraining, Planning-and-
Refinement SFT, and Self-Refinement Rejection Sampling.
The first two stages establish driving knowledge, direct tra-
jectory generation, and guidance-assisted refinement. The
final stage exposes the teacher to student-generated failures
and retains verified refinements, reducing the distribution
gap between offline refinement data and on-policy trajecto-
ries. After Dual-OPSD, Reflect-VLA performs single-pass
trajectory generation without Reflective Guidance, an online
planning evaluator, or an iterative correction procedure.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We construct Reflective Guidance that converts trajectory
evaluation results into structured, trajectory-specific diag-
noses and corrective suggestions, and use it as privileged
information available only during training.
• We propose Dual-OPSD, a trajectory-quality-aware self-
distillation method that transfers corrective distributions
for risky trajectories while preserving reliable planning.
• We develop a progressive teacher-construction strategy
that combines driving-knowledge pretraining, planning-
and-refinement supervision, and self-refinement rejection
sampling to support stable guidance internalization and
efficient single-pass planning.

Related Work
2.1
VLA models for autonomous driving
VLMs and VLA models have been increasingly studied for
autonomous driving, with applications ranging from scene
understanding (Jiang et al. 2024, 2025a; Marcu et al. 2024)
to direct trajectory prediction (Xing et al. 2025; Zhao et al.
2025; Liu et al. 2025; Fu et al. 2025). To improve inter-
pretability and planning accuracy, several methods introduce
explicit intermediate reasoning, such as Chain-of-Thought
reasoning (Liu et al. 2025; Zhao et al. 2025; Luo et al.
2025b). Their training pipelines typically begin with su-
pervised fine-tuning on driving data and subsequently ap-
ply reinforcement learning, including Group Relative Pol-
icy Optimization, to improve planning performance (Luo
et al. 2025a; Zhou et al. 2025b). Although scalar planning
rewards provide a direct measure of trajectory quality, they
offer limited supervision when diverse failure causes receive
similarly low scores (Chen et al. 2026a). This issue is es-
pecially pronounced in long-tail scenarios, where repeated
low-reward rollouts can make policy optimization inefficient.
ELF-VLA (Luo et al. 2026a) addresses this limitation by us-
ing teacher-generated feedback to diagnose planning failures
and construct improved training samples. Our work instead
focuses on internalizing the benefit of such structured guid-
ance so that it is no longer required during inference.
2.2
On-Policy Distillation and On-Policy
Self-Distillation
On-policy distillation (OPD) and on-policy self-distillation
(OPSD) are post-training approaches that supervise a stu-
dent on trajectories sampled from its own policy (Agarwal
et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2026). Unlike off-policy distillation,
this formulation queries a teacher with student-generated
prefixes, thereby providing token-level supervision over the
distribution encountered during training. OPD commonly
relies on a separately trained teacher, whereas OPSD can
derive the teacher from the same model by conditioning
it on privileged information (Ye et al. 2026; Sang et al.
2026), such as ground-truth answers, additional instructions,
or preference information. Recent work has combined dense
token-level signals from OPD with reinforcement learning
for VLA post-training (Zhong et al. 2026), but its applica-
tion to autonomous-driving planning remains limited. We
formulate Reflective Guidance as privileged information and
use it to obtain corrective teacher distributions on student-
generated trajectories. Dual-OPSD further accounts for tra-
jectory quality by distilling corrections only when needed
and preserving decisions that are already reliable.

3.2
Data Construction
To develop both direct trajectory prediction and guidance-
conditioned trajectory refinement capabilities, we first pre-
pare the corresponding training data as follows.
Pretraining and Base Data.
We first collect a diverse
set of open-source driving QA datasets, including Lin-
goQA (Marcu et al. 2024), DriveGPT4 (Xu et al. 2024),
Senna (Jiang et al. 2024), DriveLM (Sima et al. 2024),
NuScenes-QA (Qian et al. 2024), NuInstruct (Ding et al.
2024), and DriveLMM (Ishaq et al. 2025). Pretraining on


these datasets equips the general VLM with domain-specific
knowledge for autonomous driving, including road seman-
tics, traffic regulations, and driving-intent understanding.
We then construct the base planning dataset, with each
sample containing a front-view camera image, a high-level
navigation command, the ego-vehicle state, a historical tra-
jectory, and a ground-truth future trajectory represented as
discretized special trajectory tokens.
Guidance-conditioned Refinement Data.
To enable the
model to refine low-quality initial trajectories, we further
construct a guidance-conditioned refinement dataset. Specif-
ically, for each driving scenario, we first build a candidate
trajectory set Ocan = Oplan ∪Orule, where Oplan denotes
the trajectories generated by a classical planner, while Orule
consists of trajectories obtained through rule-based pertur-
bation. Each candidate is assessed using the NAVSIM eval-
uator (Dauner et al. 2024), together with map constraints
and trajectory-level analysis. Based on the resulting scores,
low-quality candidates are selected as the initial trajecto-
ries oinit for refinement. In particular, the NAVSIM evalu-
ator provides the Predictive Driver Model Score (PDMS),
which comprehensively measures collision safety, drivable-
area compliance, time-to-collision, ego progress, and over-
all trajectory quality. The Reflective Guidance g is derived
from the evaluation results, transforming scalar scores into
structured, trajectory-specific corrective supervision. Conse-
quently, training samples in the guidance-conditioned refine-
ment dataset can be represented as:
Drefine = {(qgui, oref)} = {(qbase, oinit, g, oref)}.
(5)
where oref denotes the target refined trajectory, which can
either be the ground-truth trajectory or a high-quality refined
trajectory verified by the trajectory evaluator.
Self-Refinement Rejection Sampling.
Training on the
constructed planning and refinement data enables the model
to leverage guidance for trajectory correction. However, be-
cause the initial trajectories in this dataset are generated of-
fline, they may not fully capture the failure patterns encoun-
tered during model rollout. To mitigate this distribution mis-
match, we introduce Self-Refinement Rejection Sampling.
Specifically, the model first predicts an initial trajectory from
the base input. When the trajectory is identified as problem-
atic, the same model subsequently refines its prediction in
response to the corresponding guidance. We retain only re-
fined trajectories that satisfy the validity constraints and out-
perform their initial counterparts, and use these high-quality
samples to further optimize the model.
3.3
Three-stage SFT for Planning and Refinement
We adopt a progressive three-stage training strategy con-
sisting of driving-knowledge pretraining, planning-and-
refinement supervised fine-tuning, and self-refinement rejec-
tion sampling. In the first stage, the model acquires essential
domain knowledge for autonomous driving. In the second
stage, it is trained on a mixture of base planning data and
guidance-conditioned refinement data, enabling both direct
trajectory generation and trajectory refinement. In the final
stage, Self-refinement rejection sampling trains the model
to correct failures from its own unguided rollouts, thereby
yielding a more reliable teacher for subsequent on-policy
self-distillation.
3.4

the ability to refine trajectories with reflective guidance. In-
spired by on-policy self-distillation for LLMs (Zhao et al.
2026), where a single model serves as both teacher and stu-
dent under different input contexts, we propose Dual-Path
Online Policy Self-Distillation (Dual-OPSD), which dis-
tills the planning capability elicited by guidance-conditioned
inputs into the model’s direct trajectory generation behavior.

Detailed metric definitions are provided in Appendix E.
Training Details.
We use Qwen3.5-0.8B/2B/4B (Team
2026) as the base model. Training consists of three stages:
(1) driving-knowledge pretraining for 2 epochs with batch
size 1 and a learning rate of 1 × 10−5; (2) fine-tuning on
the planning, refinement, and rejection-sampling data for 2
epochs with batch size 4 and a learning rate of 4×10−5; and
(3) on-policy self-distillation for 2 epochs with batch size 2
and learning rates of 8 × 10−6 and 2 × 10−6, respectively.
Training is conducted on 8 NVIDIA H20 GPUs. Further
details are provided in Appendix E.

5
Conclusion
In this paper, we introduce Reflect-VLA, a training frame-
work that internalizes reflective guidance into VLA mod-
els for autonomous driving. Through dual-path on-policy
self-distillation, Reflect-VLA transfers token-level correc-
tive distributions from guidance-conditioned generation to
direct trajectory prediction, enabling strong planning with-
out explicit guidance or iterative refinement at inference
time. Experiments on NAVSIM and Bench2Drive demon-
strate competitive driving performance and up to a 10× train-
ing speedup over reinforcement-learning baselines, while re-
taining efficient, guidance-free single-pass planning across
different model scales.

B
Question
Q1: Why not directly use Reflective Guidance during in-
ference?
A: Constructing Reflective Guidance requires an initial tra-
jectory and its evaluation results, which introduces an addi-
tional prediction–evaluation– refinement loop. Reflect-VLA
instead treats the guidance as training-time privileged infor-
mation and internalizes its corrective preferences, enabling
single-pass inference without an online evaluator.
Q2: Why does Dual-OPSD use separate learning paths
for reliable and risky trajectories?
A: Teacher correction is useful for risky trajectories but
may unnecessarily modify predictions that are already re-
liable. Dual-OPSD therefore distills guidance-conditioned
teacher distributions for risky rollouts while applying self-
preservation to reliable ones, balancing correction and be-
havior retention.
Q3: Why is Self-Refinement Rejection Sampling needed
before Dual-OPSD?
A: Offline refinement data may not cover the failure pat-
terns produced by the model itself. Self-Refinement Rejec-
tion Sampling exposes the teacher to model-generated fail-
ures and retains only evaluator-verified improvements, mak-
ing its corrective behavior better aligned with the student
rollout distribution.
Q4: Does Reflective Guidance require an additional VLM
or manual feedback?
A: No. Reflective Guidance is constructed through determin-
istic templates using trajectory-evaluation results, geometric
differences from a reference trajectory, and the navigation
command. It therefore provides structured corrective super-
vision without introducing another VLM or requiring manual
annotation.

C.2
Ablation Studies
Effect of Top-k Distillation.
Table 9 evaluates the size of
the teacher-distribution support retained during token-level
distillation. Increasing k from 16 to 64 improves PDMS from
90.2 to 91.5, since the larger support preserves more infor-
mative teacher preferences. Further increasing k to 256 or
the full vocabulary yields only marginal gains, indicating di-
minishing returns once the dominant teacher distribution is
sufficiently covered. We therefore use k = 64 as the distilla-
tion support size.
Effect of KL-Divergence Direction.
Table 10 shows that
reverse KL improves PDMS from 91.0 to 91.5. Reverse KL
encourages the student to concentrate on the teacher’s high-
probability corrective tokens and suppress unlikely alterna-
tives. In contrast, forward KL favors broader distribution
coverage, which may dilute the teacher’s dominant correction
preferences. We therefore adopt reverse KL for token-level
distillation.
Effect of the Routing Threshold.
Table 11 shows that in-
creasing s from 0.65 to 0.85 gradually improves PDMS, as
more suboptimal trajectories are routed to distillation and
aligned with the teacher’s corrective preferences. However,
a larger threshold of 0.95 also distills many already reliable
trajectories, where unnecessary teacher intervention may dis-
rupt effective student behaviors. We therefore set s = 0.85
to balance correction and preservation.

D.2
Reflective Guidance Construction
We construct Reflective Guidance for low-quality trajecto-
ries using a deterministic template-based procedure. It relies
on training-time privileged information, including trajectory-
evaluation results, the navigation command, and a refer-
ence trajectory oref, without requiring an additional vision-
language model. Given an initial trajectory oinit, we decode
it into continuous waypoints when necessary and generate
four components: Trajectory Verdict, Planning Diagnosis,
Expert Alignment, and Refinement Directive.
Trajectory Verdict.
The Trajectory Verdict converts eval-
uator outputs into an explicit reliability judgment. It identifies
whether the initial prediction is problematic and summarizes
the available PDMS, NC, DAC, TTC, EP, and CF metrics,
providing quantitative context for subsequent correction.
Planning Diagnosis.
The Planning Diagnosis identifies
the dominant causes of failure, including collision risk,
drivable-area violation, insufficient TTC margin, inadequate
progress, and trajectory misalignment. Safety-critical fail-
ures are prioritized over efficiency-related errors, and only
the most relevant diagnoses are retained. When event-level
information is available, the first collision or boundary viola-
tion is localized to the corresponding waypoint and position.


Expert Alignment.
The Expert Alignment compares oinit
with oref and translates their geometric differences into qual-
itative adjustments. These adjustments indicate whether to
preserve or shift the lateral position, increase or reduce lon-
gitudinal progress, and maintain or correct the final heading.
The navigation command is included to keep the correction
consistent with the intended maneuver.
Refinement Directive.
The Refinement Directive converts
the diagnosis and alignment results into an ordered correc-
tion plan. Collision and TTC failures first require creating
a safer gap, while drivable-area violations require moving
the path back into the valid lane corridor. The directive then
specifies the required lateral, longitudinal, and heading ad-
justments, while preserving smooth intermediate waypoints
and navigation intent. A primary focus, such as scene inter-
pretation, lateral control, or longitudinal control, is assigned
based on the dominant failure.
Construction Flow.
We first normalize the trajectories and
evaluator outputs and determine whether the initial trajectory
requires correction. For a problematic trajectory, we extract
the dominant failures, derive reference-relative adjustments,
and instantiate the four components in the above order. The
resulting guidance g is combined with the base input and

D.3
Details of Pretraining Data
We collect several open-source driving QA datasets
for driving-knowledge pretraining, including LingoQA,
DriveGPT4, Senna, DriveLM, NuScenes-QA, NuInstruct,
and DriveLMM.
LingoQA. LingoQA contains approximately 419.9K QA
pairs from short driving video clips, covering scene descrip-
tion, localization, and reasoning.
DriveGPT4. DriveGPT4 provides visual instruction data
from multi-frame driving videos, covering action interpre-
tation, driving reasoning, question answering, and low-level
control prediction.
Senna. Senna contains planning-oriented QA data over
multi-view driving scenes, covering scene understanding,
motion prediction, and high-level planning decisions.
DriveLM. DriveLM contains approximately 0.3M image-
question pairs from 696 nuScenes scenes for graph-based
driving-scene reasoning.
NuScenes-QA. NuScenes-QA provides approximately 460K
QA pairs over multi-frame camera and LiDAR observations.
NuInstruct. NuInstruct contains approximately 91K multi-
view video-QA pairs across 17 spatial, temporal, and multi-
view reasoning tasks.
DriveLMM. DriveLMM provides over 18K training VQA
examples with step-by-step reasoning for perception, predic-
tion, and planning.
D.4
Details of SFT Data
Planning Data.
For each dataset, we construct a base plan-
ning set in which each sample contains a front-view cam-
era image, the ego-vehicle state including velocity and ac-
celeration, a high-level navigation command (Go Straight,
Turn Left, or Turn Right), a historical ego trajectory, and
the ground-truth future trajectory. Both historical and future
trajectories are sampled at 0.5-second intervals, and the fu-
ture trajectory is converted into discrete special tokens for
autoregressive prediction.
Guidance-Conditioned Refinement Data.
Each refine-
ment sample pairs the base driving input with a low-quality
initial trajectory, its corresponding Reflective Guidance, and
a reference trajectory. The initial trajectories are obtained
from two complementary sources:
• Planner-generated trajectories. We collect trajectories
produced by a classical planner and retain those exhibiting
correctable failures in safety, drivable-area compliance, or
progress.
• Rule-perturbed trajectories. We perturb valid trajec-
tories along the longitudinal, lateral, or heading dimen-
sions to construct representative planning failures while
preserving a valid trajectory format.
All candidates are evaluated using the trajectory evaluator
and map constraints, and low-quality but valid trajectories are
selected as oinit. The ground-truth expert trajectory is used
as the refinement target; alternatively, an evaluator-verified
refined trajectory is retained when it resolves the failure and
improves upon the initial trajectory.
Self-Refinement Rejection Sampling Data.
We construct
self-refinement data from the model’s own rollouts. For each
base input, the model first generates an unguided initial tra-
jectory, which is evaluated to identify problematic cases. We
then construct the corresponding Reflective Guidance and
prompt the same model to generate a refined trajectory. A
sample is retained only when the refined trajectory satisfies
the trajectory and map constraints and achieves a higher eval-
uation score than the initial trajectory. When multiple valid
refinements are available, we retain the highest-scoring one.

Experimental Details
E.1
Training Parameters and Hardware
Configuration.
We train Qwen3.5-0.8B/2B/4B on 8 NVIDIA H20 GPUs.
Driving-knowledge pretraining is conducted for 2 epochs
with a batch size of 1 and a learning rate of 1 × 10−5.
Planning-and-Refinement SFT is then performed on the
base planning and guidance-conditioned refinement data,
followed by Self-Refinement Rejection Sampling on the re-
tained self-refinement data. These two stages use the same
configuration of 2 epochs, a batch size of 4, and a learn-
ing rate of 4 × 10−5. Dual-OPSD is subsequently performed
for 2 epochs with a batch size of 2 and learning rates of
8 × 10−6 and 2 × 10−6 for the first and second epochs, re-
spectively. During Dual-OPSD, on-policy rollouts are gener-
ated through greedy decoding, with the temperature set to 0.


For token-level distillation, we retain the teacher’s top-k sup-
port with k = 64 and minimize the reverse KL divergence
over the truncated distribution. The routing threshold is set
to s = 0.85. Reliable trajectories are optimized through self-
preservation, whereas risky trajectories receive token-level
supervision from the frozen guidance-conditioned teacher.

Figure 4 presents three representative failure cases. In each
scenario, the unguided student produces a problematic tra-
jectory, while the guidance-conditioned teacher generates
a safer correction. After Dual-OPSD, the distilled student
largely reproduces this behavior without requiring guid-
ance at inference time. For the Drivable-Area Violation, the
teacher shifts the trajectory away from the road boundary
and back into the valid lane corridor. For the Insufficient
TTC Margin, it reduces forward progress to maintain a safer
distance from the conflict region. For the At-Fault Colli-
sion Risk, it increases clearance and postpones longitudinal
progress until the hard safety constraint is resolved. The dis-
tilled student exhibits similar lateral and longitudinal adjust-
ments in all three cases. The token-level distributions further
reveal the transfer process. Under the same student-generated
prefix, the teacher assigns greater probability to tokens corre-
sponding to safer lateral or longitudinal motions, whereas the
original student favors riskier alternatives. Dual-OPSD shifts
the student toward these corrective preferences, resulting in
safer token selection and improved trajectory generation
