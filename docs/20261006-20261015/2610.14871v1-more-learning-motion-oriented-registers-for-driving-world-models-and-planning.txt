ABSTRACT
By predicting future states, latent world models provide rich self-supervision for
end-to-end driving, but often inherit dense features from pretrained visual en-
coders as their world-state interface. This creates a mismatch: visual pretrain-
ing produces information-rich, spatially dense features, but their adaptation for
motion-conditioned world modeling and planning is deferred to downstream train-
ing. The dense grid also makes future prediction more computationally expensive
as visual resolution increases. We therefore argue that video pretraining should di-
rectly learn state representations suited to downstream world modeling and plan-
ning, rather than relying on downstream training to adapt inherited visual features.
We propose MORE, a V-JEPA framework that learns Motion-Oriented REgisters
during video pretraining as a shared state interface for driving world models and
planning. MORE injects temporally aligned ego motion into a small set of reg-
isters and uses them as the sole context for masked patch prediction, channeling
dense patch-level supervision through a compact state bottleneck. A complemen-
tary blockwise register-masking objective further shapes predictive structure in
the register space. During downstream training, the same register space defines
the future states predicted by the world model and provides shared scene memory
for trajectory generation and scoring. On NAVSIM-v2, MORE outperforms the
state of the art by 1.6 in EPDMS, while achieving superior efficiency with only
1/10 the inference latency of DriveLaW. These results support learning the state
interface for world modeling and planning directly during video pretraining.

INTRODUCTION
End-to-end autonomous driving (Wu et al., 2022; Hu et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2025)
predicts future trajectories from sensor observations, but low-dimensional expert trajectories provide
limited supervision for high-capacity visual encoders. Driving world models provide richer signals
by predicting future observations (Wang et al., 2024b; Li et al., 2026c; Wang et al., 2024a), occu-
pancy or BEV states (Zheng et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026), or latent features (Li
et al., 2025b; Zheng et al., 2025). Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA) (LeCun, 2022)
offer a complementary route by learning predictable representations rather than reconstructing pix-
els. V-JEPA (Bardes et al., 2024) extends this principle to spatiotemporal masked prediction in
videos, while V-JEPA 2-AC (Assran et al., 2025) and Drive-JEPA (Wang et al., 2026b) further
demonstrate its potential for action-conditioned prediction and trajectory planning.
However, a fundamental mismatch remains between pretrained visual representations and the
states required by downstream world models and planners.
Visual pretraining primarily learns
information-rich representations of scene content and spatiotemporal structure. In contrast, a world
model requires states that capture scene evolution conditioned on ego motion and support future
prediction (Min et al., 2024), while a planner further requires decision-relevant context. Existing
approaches typically learn visual representations first and adapt them during downstream train-
ing (Yang et al., 2026a; Zheng et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025b). For example, V-JEPA 2-AC (As-

We refer to this gap, illustrated in Fig. 1(a), as a state-interface mismatch.
Beyond this alignment gap, the organization of future states poses a related efficiency challenge.
V-JEPA 2-AC (Assran et al., 2025) predicts spatiotemporally indexed patch features, DriveX (Shi
et al., 2025) and WoTE (Li et al., 2025c) predict latent BEV grids, and World4Drive (Zheng et al.,
2025) predicts spatial visual latents. Because these future states retain spatial grids, finer grids
require more tokens to be predicted at each future step. DrivoR (Kirby et al., 2026) and NTR (Li
et al., 2026a) instead compress dense visual features into a few planning tokens. This reduces state
size for current-scene planning, but compactness alone does not ensure ego-motion sensitivity or the
predictive structure needed for future modeling.
Our central question is therefore not how to further compress visual features during downstream
training, but whether video pretraining can directly learn state representations suited to subsequent
world modeling and planning. The key challenge is to retain dense visual supervision while endow-
ing this state space with sufficient information, ego-motion sensitivity, and predictive structure.
To instantiate this principle, we propose MORE, a V-JEPA framework that directly learns motion-
oriented register states. During video pretraining, we introduce a small set of registers into the video
encoder and condition them on temporally aligned ego motion. To ensure that these registers retain
sufficient visual information, we require masked patch representations to be predicted solely through
the registers. Dense patch-level supervision is therefore routed through a compact register bottle-
neck and directly shapes the state interface used downstream. We further apply blockwise masking
and prediction in the register space to explicitly encourage predictive state structure. During joint
training, we retain the same register space. A non-autoregressive world model predicts future reg-
isters from observed registers and ego motion, while the planner uses both as shared scene memory
for trajectory generation and scoring. The world model can therefore model future evolution di-
rectly in the state space shaped during pretraining, rather than constructing predictive states from
generic visual features. By processing only a small set of registers, MORE also reduces downstream
computation, yielding the low inference latency shown in Fig. 1(b) and detailed in Appendix E.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We identify and address the state-interface mismatch between pretrained visual representations
and the states needed for downstream world modeling and planning by learning a shared state
interface during video pretraining.

• We introduce motion-oriented registers, register-mediated masked patch prediction, and blockwise
register masking to jointly promote information sufficiency, motion sensitivity, and predictive
structure in the learned state space.
• We use the same register state space for non-autoregressive world modeling and end-to-end tra-
jectory planning, and evaluate the resulting framework on NAVSIM and NAVSIM-v2, together
with closed-loop driving evaluation in HUGSIM.

RELATED WORKS
Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures and World Modeling.
JEPA (LeCun, 2022) shifts
prediction from observation space to learned representation space. I-JEPA (Assran et al., 2023)
predicts masked image-region representations from visible context, V-JEPA (Bardes et al., 2024)
extends this principle to video, and V-JEPA 2 (Assran et al., 2025) scales both model size and pre-
training data. V-JEPA 2.1 (Mur-Labadia et al., 2026) further improves fine-grained spatial and tem-
poral representations through dense predictive loss and deep self-supervision. Recent work explores
JEPA for action-conditioned latent world modeling. V-JEPA 2-AC (Assran et al., 2025) freezes
a pretrained video encoder and predicts future dense patch features conditioned on actions, while
systematic studies of JEPA-WMs examine multi-step latent prediction, action conditioning, and
planning strategies (Terver et al., 2025). For autonomous driving, Drive-JEPA (Wang et al., 2026b)
transfers V-JEPA representations to trajectory learning. These works demonstrate the potential of
JEPA for prediction and control, but the downstream state is generally inherited from visual pre-
training, with action-conditioned state evolution introduced mainly during post-training. We instead
shape the state space used for future modeling and planning directly during video pretraining.
World Models for Autonomous Driving. Autonomous-driving world models predict the future in
observation space, structured spatial representations, or latent feature space. DriveDreamer (Wang
et al., 2024a) and Drive-WM (Wang et al., 2024b) generate action- or trajectory-conditioned images
and videos; DriveVLA-W0 (Li et al., 2026c) uses future image prediction as dense self-supervision.
These approaches preserve rich visual information but also model appearance details not directly
relevant to planning. OccWorld (Zheng et al., 2024) and Drive-OccWorld (Yang et al., 2025) instead
predict occupancy states, DriveWorld (Min et al., 2024) and DriveX (Shi et al., 2025) model dy-
namics in structured scene representations, and WoTE (Li et al., 2025c) and ResWorld (Zhang et al.,
2026) use future BEV states to evaluate or refine trajectories. Such representations provide explicit
geometry but require a full spatial grid at every future step.
Latent world models avoid explicit observation reconstruction by predicting in feature space (Li
et al., 2025b; Lu et al., 2026b; Zheng et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2026a). LAW (Li et al., 2025b) uses
ego-trajectory-conditioned future latent prediction as an auxiliary objective, World4Drive (Zheng
et al., 2025) predicts future visual latents for trajectory selection, and WorldRFT (Yang et al., 2026a)
aligns latent representations with planning through geometric and decision supervision. These meth-
ods validate latent prediction for driving, but their states are typically derived from existing visual
representations and adapted to dynamics and planning through downstream objectives. Dense latent
grids also keep state size tied to visual resolution. We instead pretrain the downstream state space
itself, using compact registers as both future prediction targets and planning context.

METHOD
We propose MORE, which learns motion-oriented registers as a compact state interface for driving
world models and planning. As shown in Fig. 2, our pipeline consists of two stages. During pre-
training, ego-motion conditioning, register-mediated patch prediction, and blockwise register mask-
ing shape an informative and predictable register state space from dense supervision. During joint
training, we reuse the same register space to train the world model, trajectory generator, and scorer.

MOTION-ORIENTED REGISTER PRETRAINING
Given a video clip I ∈RT ×H×W ×3, we tokenize it into non-overlapping tubelets of size τ × p × p,
yielding a token grid P ∈RNt×Nh×Nw×D, where Nt = T/τ, Nh = H/p, and Nw = W/p. For
each temporal chunk k, we denote its Np = NhNw patch tokens by Pk ∈RNp×D and introduce

masked patch prediction, and blockwise register masking.

Two-Branch Pretraining with Shared Encoders.

pretrain two complementary branches, namely Register-Mediated Masked Patch Prediction and
Blockwise Register Masking. Both share the online encoder Eθ, predictor Pϕ, and EMA target

EXPERIMENTS
Datasets, Benchmarks & Metrics. We pretrain on OpenScene (Contributors, 2023), a compact
redistribution of nuPlan (Caesar et al., 2021) comprising approximately 120 hours of urban driving
at 2 Hz, and jointly train the world model and planner on NAVSIM navtrain. We evaluate on
the navtest splits of NAVSIM v1 and v2 (Dauner et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2025), reporting the
Predictive Driver Model Score (PDMS) and Extended Predictive Driver Model Score (EPDMS)
under their respective protocols. We further evaluate closed-loop driving in HUGSIM (Zhou et al.,
2026), where actions affect subsequent states and observations, reporting Route Completion (RC)
and HUGSIM Driving Score (HD-Score). Metric details are provided in Appendices C and D.
Implementation Details. We follow Transfuser (Chitta et al., 2023) in using an input resolution of
256 × 1024, formed by stacking the front-left, front, and front-right images. The first-stage encoder
is a ViT-L initialized from V-JEPA 2 (Assran et al., 2025) weights, with a temporal tubelet size of
2 frames and 8 registers per tubelet. We pretrain for 22,000 steps on OpenScene using AdamW
on 32 NVIDIA A100 GPUs with a global batch size of 256, an initial learning rate of 1.5 × 10−4
cosine-decayed to 10−6, and weight decay of 0.04. The target-encoder EMA momentum increases
from 0.996 to 0.999. For joint training, only the online and target encoders are initialized from
the first stage, while the world model and planner are trained from scratch. The model observes 4
frames and predicts the subsequent 8 frames at 2 Hz. We use K = 64 trajectory queries. We train
on navtrain for 10 epochs using 8 NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs with a global batch size of 128 and
a base learning rate of 2 × 10−4, applying a 0.1 multiplier to the online encoder.

5
CONCLUSION
We present MORE, which learns motion-oriented registers during video pretraining as a compact
state interface for driving world models and planning. During pretraining, ego-motion condition-
ing, register-mediated masked patch prediction, and blockwise register masking retain dense visual
supervision while shaping informative, motion-sensitive, and predictive states. During joint train-
ing, the same register space supports non-autoregressive world modeling, trajectory generation, and
scoring. Results on NAVSIM-v1, NAVSIM-v2, and HUGSIM demonstrate strong planning perfor-
mance, low inference latency, and zero-shot closed-loop generalization. Overall, MORE shows that
video pretraining can directly shape the compact predictive state required by world modeling and
planning rather than leaving this adaptation to downstream training.

DATASETS AND MORE IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
B.1
DATASETS
OpenScene. OpenScene is a redistribution of nuPlan driving logs sampled at 2 Hz, comprising
approximately 120 hours of driving. We use its trainval logs for motion-oriented register pre-
training. Consecutive windows start 1 frame (0.5 s) apart. Each window provides a 12-frame video
clip to the model. No OpenScene test logs are used for pretraining.
NAVSIM Training and Evaluation Splits. For joint training of the world model and planner, we
load NAVSIM’s navtrain. Frames are sampled at 2 Hz, and consecutive candidate windows
start 1 frame (0.5 s) apart. After scene filtering and data-validity checks, the training set contains
103,288 samples. For each sample, the model observes 4 frames and uses the following 8 frames
for future-state and trajectory supervision.
For evaluation, the NAVSIM’s navtest filter selects 12,146 scenarios. We report PDMS under the
NAVSIM-v1 protocol and EPDMS under the NAVSIM-v2 protocol on this same navtest set.
HUGSIM. HUGSIM is a photorealistic closed-loop simulator in which the ego vehicle’s actions
affect subsequent states and rendered observations. Its benchmark draws on reconstructed scenes
from KITTI-360 (Liao et al., 2023), Waymo Open Dataset (Sun et al., 2020), nuScenes (Caesar
et al., 2020), and PandaSet (Xiao et al., 2021). We evaluate the NAVSIM-trained model without
HUGSIM-specific training or fine-tuning on 436 scenarios: 80 Easy, 157 Medium, 96 Hard, and
15


103 Extreme. This evaluation tests the policy under interactive, long-horizon driving rather than the
non-reactive NAVSIM scoring protocol.

B.3
MORE IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
Input Processing. For each timestamp, we use synchronized front-left, front, and front-right im-
ages. Following TransFuser (Chitta et al., 2023), we remove 28 pixels from the top and bottom of
each image and crop 416 pixels from both horizontal sides of the two lateral views. The processed
views are concatenated in left–front–right order, resized once to 256×1024. We apply no additional
image augmentation to preserve the geometry of the stitched view.
Stage1: Motion-Oriented Register Pretraining. We use a ViT-L video encoder with 24 Trans-
former blocks, a token dimension of 1,024, and 16 attention heads. Using a temporal tubelet size
of 2 frames and a spatial patch size of 16 × 16, each 12-frame clip yields 6 temporal chunks and
48 registers. A three-dimensional ego-motion vector is linearly projected to the encoder dimension
and added to the registers of its corresponding chunk before self-attention. The register-mediated
masked patch prediction and blockwise register masking branches share a 12-layer Transformer
predictor with a hidden dimension of 384 and 12 attention heads.
Stage2: Joint World Modeling and Planning. Four observed frames yield two temporal chunks
and 16 observed registers, while the eight future frames correspond to four chunks and 32 learned
future queries. The trajectory generator and scorer each contain 4 Transformer layers with a hidden
dimension of 256. We configure AdamW for 10 epochs, with the base learning rate 2 × 10−4,
followed by cosine decay to 10−6. The encoder parameter groups use a learning-rate multiplier of
0.1, giving scheduled rates of 2 × 10−5 at the start and 10−7 at the end. Training uses bfloat16
precision.

NAVSIM EVALUATION
NAVSIM (Dauner et al., 2024) evaluates predicted ego trajectories through a lightweight simulation-
based protocol. The official simulator executes the trajectory using its vehicle model and evaluates
it against map, route, and traffic-agent information.
16


NAVSIM v1. NAVSIM v1 uses non-reactive simulation, in which the ego vehicle executes the pre-
dicted trajectory, while surrounding agents follow their recorded future trajectories. The Predictive
Driver Model Score (PDMS) comprises five normalized subscores. No At-Fault Collisions (NC)
penalizes collisions for which the ego vehicle is considered responsible, with a softer penalty for
collisions with static objects. Drivable Area Compliance (DAC) checks whether the ego footprint
remains within the drivable area. Ego Progress (EP) measures progress along the route center rela-
tive to a safe upper bound estimated by PDM-Closed. Time-to-Collision (TTC) checks whether the
projected TTC under constant velocity and heading remains above a safety threshold. Comfort (C)
verifies that trajectory acceleration and jerk remain within predefined bounds. The PDMS is
PDMS = NC × DAC × 5 EP + 5 TTC + 2 C

HUGSIM CLOSED-LOOP EVALUATION
HUGSIM (Zhou et al., 2026) is a photorealistic, interactive closed-loop simulator constructed us-
ing 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023). Unlike NAVSIM’s per-scene trajectory evaluation,
HUGSIM repeatedly queries the policy throughout an episode. The predicted trajectory is executed,
the ego vehicle and surrounding actors are updated, and new observations are rendered from the

EFFICIENCY ANALYSIS
As shown in Tab. 6, MORE achieves the highest EPDMS of 92.5 while maintaining an inference
latency close to Drive-JEPA (Wang et al., 2026b), which does not use an explicit future-state world
model (110.0 VS. 73.3 ms). With comparable peak memory (6.18 vs. 6.89 GB), MORE requires
only about 1/10 the inference latency of DriveLaW (Xia et al., 2026) (110.0 vs. 1,167.0 ms). These
results show that motion-oriented registers provide an effective and efficient shared state interface
for future world modeling and planning.

F.2
REGISTER SIMILARITY ANALYSIS
To examine how our pretraining design shapes the register space beyond merely introducing reg-
ister tokens, we compare the pretrained representations of configuration #4 in Tab. 4 and MORE.
As shown in Fig. 5, configuration #4 exhibits broadly high similarity within and across temporal
chunks, with a mean of 0.793, indicating substantial redundancy among registers. MORE reduces
the mean similarity to 0.653 and produces more differentiated patterns across register slots and tem-
poral chunks. These results show that our register pretraining design encourages complementary
rather than highly overlapping representations, yielding a more expressive compact state interface
for future world modeling and planning.
