Abstract
End-to-end driving policies can achieve strong task perfor-
mance while offering limited control over their driving style.
Driving style describes how a vehicle accelerates, brakes,
and maintains distance. Policies trained without explicit
preference conditioning lack a direct mechanism for adjust-
ing these behaviors at inference. We propose YOUDrive, a
vision–language–action planner for continuous and compos-
able driving-style control. Each teacher’s behavior is dis-
tilled into a task vector, a learned parameter update that in-
duces a style direction in the planner’s representation. These
directions can be scaled and combined at inference without
retraining the backbone. Conditioned on these directions, a
flow-matching decoder models a multimodal trajectory dis-
tribution and generates kinematically feasible motion alter-
natives. We introduce the Style Alignment Score (SAS) to de-
scribe human-referenced kinematic behavior after safety and
task gating. On NAVSIM navtest, a single coefficient mono-
tonically controls assertiveness across six teacher-derived
personas, while PDMS remains above 0.90 across the tested
individual and blended configurations, reaching 0.954. Fur-
ther analyses reveal systematic policy-specific kinematics
after controlling for scene context, whereas the evaluated
discrete style labels provide limited additional explanatory
power. Code, checkpoints, and the metric will be released.
1. Introduction
End-to-end driving models learn to predict trajectories from
sensor inputs using large-scale driving datasets that pool
demonstrations from multiple drivers [3, 9, 12]. These data
reflect not only the demands of each scene but also how
individual drivers execute their maneuvers. Their patterns
of acceleration, braking, and following distance characterize
driving style. A policy trained on these demonstrations
acquires its own driving behavior, but learning from different
†Corresponding author.
drivers does not by itself give users control over that behavior.
The challenge is to make driving style adjustable at inference
while preserving the policy’s ability to respond to traffic and
complete the driving task.
One approach is to condition a driving policy on discrete
style labels, such as aggressive, normal, and conservative [6].
However, the distribution of scenes and motion states can dif-
fer substantially across these categories. When conservative
labels are concentrated in low-speed or near-stationary clips,
they reflect scene conditions more than driver preferences
(Sec. 4.4). This limits their ability to distinguish driver pref-
erences from scene-dependent behavior. For style control,
we instead seek a behavioral reference that can be examined
under comparable driving conditions. We treat each trained
teacher policy as a driver and characterize its style through
the trajectories it produces across a common set of scenes.
Building on this policy-level view, we propose YOUDrive
to transfer teacher behaviors to a shared vision–language–
action (VLA) planner and control their expression at infer-
ence. For each teacher, we distill its driving behavior into
a task vector, a learned parameter update relative to a com-
mon base planner [11]. For a given scene, the difference
between the updated and base representations defines the
teacher’s style direction. A coefficient controls the strength
of this direction, allowing interpolation between the two rep-
resentations. Directions from multiple teachers can also be
combined through weighted addition in the same represen-
tation space, without retraining the shared planner. A flow-
matching decoder translates the resulting style condition into
motion by generating control sequences and integrating them
through a constrained motion model. This produces multiple
kinematically feasible trajectory candidates under the cho-
sen style condition. To characterize the resulting behavior,
we introduce the Style Alignment Score (SAS), which mea-
sures interpretable, human-referenced kinematic features
after safety and task gating. Reporting SAS alongside the
unchanged PDMS allows style changes to be examined sepa-
rately from planning performance. Our NAVSIM evaluation

2. Related Work
End-to-end and VLA planning.
End-to-end driving meth-
ods integrate scene understanding and trajectory planning
within a learned policy. TransFuser combines camera and Li-
DAR features for imitation-based driving [3]. UniAD coordi-
nates perception and prediction tasks around planning, while
VAD uses vectorized scene representations [9, 12]. VLA ap-
proaches use pretrained vision-language models to connect
visual observations and language instructions with action
generation. EMMA represents driving inputs and outputs in
a unified language format [10], whereas AutoVLA encodes
trajectories as discrete feasible actions and predicts them
autoregressively [27]. For multimodal trajectory generation,
DiffusionDrive uses truncated diffusion with multimodal an-
chors [19], while GoalFlow employs goal-conditioned flow
matching [25]. YOUDrive builds on a VLA planner and
replaces discrete action decoding with a continuous flow-
matching head in a kinematically constrained control space.
We focus on steering the resulting trajectory distribution with
teacher-derived behavior directions.
Personalized autonomous driving.
Personalized driving
has been studied through learning driving styles from demon-
strations and modeling social preferences in vehicle interac-
tions [13, 23]. In end-to-end driving, StyleDrive introduces
scenario-aware aggressive, normal, and conservative anno-
tations and corresponding style-conditioned baselines [6].
Its style-modulated PDMS adjusts comfort, progress, and
time-to-collision terms to assess style alignment. Although
these labels enable categorical control, unequal scene and
motion-state distributions across categories complicate the
interpretation of individual preferences, as examined in our
analysis (Sec. 4.4). StyleVLA conditions trajectory gener-
ation on driving-style instructions with physics-informed
training objectives [7]. TrajHF fine-tunes generative trajec-
tory models using human feedback to align their outputs
with driving preferences [14]. YOUDrive focuses on de-
riving reusable behavior directions through teacher-policy
distillation. Each teacher provides a behavioral reference
across scenes, and its induced direction can be scaled and
combined with other teacher directions within a shared VLA
planner. We also retain PDMS and report human-referenced
kinematic behavior through SAS after safety and task gat-
ing, so behavioral changes can be examined separately from
planning performance.
Task vectors and activation steering.
Task arithmetic
constructs task vectors by subtracting pretrained weights
from their fine-tuned counterparts, allowing learned adap-
tations to be scaled and combined [11]. Model Soups aver-
ages fine-tuned model weights [24], while TIES-Merging
addresses interference between parameter updates during
merging [26]. Activation steering instead intervenes in hid-
den representations during inference. ITI shifts selected
attention-head activations [15], while Persona Vectors identi-
fies activation directions associated with behavioral traits [2].
YOUDrive links parameter updates to representation-level
control through teacher-policy distillation. The learned task
vector induces a scene-dependent direction in the VLA plan-
ning representation. We scale and compose these directions
to condition trajectory generation, rather than directly merg-
ing weights at inference.

4. Experiments
4.1. Setup
Our planner uses Qwen2.5-VL-3B [1] as its scene encoder:
multi-view images, ego status, and the navigation com-
mand are tokenized into content tokens summarizing both
the scene and the requested maneuver.
On top we run
the flow-matching decoder of Sec. 3.4, conditioned on the
content tokens through cross-attention; it flows in a con-
trol space of longitudinal acceleration and yaw rate under
a unicycle model, integrated with K steps and sampled
N=16 times per frame. We warm-start from the released
AutoVLA PDMS 89 checkpoint [27] and fine-tune the lan-
guage backbone jointly with the decoder on NAVSIM nav-
train, vision encoder frozen and the human trajectory as
target; the result is our “base” (α=0). Each persona adapter
is distilled from a teacher planner—GTRS [18], Hydra-
MDP [17], WoTe [16], TransFuser [3], GoalFlow [25], Dif-
fusionDriveV2 [19]—and applied through Eq. (2). Each is a
LoRA [8] module (r=64, αLoRA=128, dropout 0.05) on the
attention and MLP projections; the base VLM (including the
vision tower) and the flow head are frozen, so only the LoRA
weights train per persona. We evaluate on NAVSIM [5]
navtest (about 12k scenarios, 8 shards) and report scenario-
weighted PDMS (v1), alongside our Style Alignment Score
(SAS), which places each policy on an assertiveness axis H
and a proximity axis P relative to the human (Sec. 4.2).
4.2. SAS: Style Alignment Score
Gate, then score. Safety and driving style answer different
questions and should not share one axis, so we evaluate a
policy in two stages. First a gate: a trajectory must pass
NAVSIM’s safety and task terms (no-collision, drivable-
area compliance, time-to-collision, ego progress); style is
scored only for trajectories that clear the gate, and style
never modulates the safety verdict. Second, we ask how the
passing motion aligns in style with a target driver. SAS and
PDMS are thus reported on separate axes: we leave PDMS
unchanged and report SAS beside it. This separation is one
of reporting—neither score modulates the other—not a claim
of statistical independence; indeed a persona that improves
ego-progress can also raise PDMS.
Atoms and human-referenced normalization. We summa-
rize a trajectory by physically interpretable kinematic atoms:
dynamics {a+, a−, jℓ, jt} (peak accel/decel, longitudinal
and lateral jerk) and proximity {thw, ttc, dmin, dvru} (min
time-headway, time-to-collision, object and VRU gap). For
a policy d and atom f we take the per-scene value and its
median mf(d) over the split. To compare heterogeneous
units against the human driver, we apply a signed z-score

4.4. Style is a per-model signal, not a label
A style axis is only meaningful if style is a real, systematic
signal rather than scene noise. Here a driver is a behaviorally
self-consistent policy—a model, in the sense of Sec. 1—so
its style is the kinematic signature it produces across scenes.
On a fixed set of tokens we collect trajectories for 16 such
drivers (the human reference, external planners, and our
base) and run a one-way variance decomposition of per-
token kinematics with driver identity as the factor (Tab. 2).

4.7. Limitations
SAS is validated along assertiveness (H); proximity (P) is

by open-road, low-interaction scenes. Within this narrow
range every native teacher loosens gaps (P<0) while every
persona tightens them (P>0, Fig. 2), so the persona direc-
tion does not reproduce the teacher’s sign on P; with the
signal this close to degenerate, whether P is independently

5. Conclusion
We presented YOUDrive, which treats driving style as a
measurable, composable control axis on a shared backbone—
each driver a reusable direction—rather than an average
in the data or a label pasted onto it. The framework rests
on three parts: a flow-matching decoder that commits to
a feasible trajectory rather than averaging, and a low-rank
task vector per driver that one coefficient scales and com-
poses without retraining(expression); the Style Alignment
Score (SAS), which reports style on its own terms after
gating hard safety (measurement); and, on NAVSIM, evi-
dence that this axis is a systematic per-model signal, unlike
a coarse label, with a single coefficient tracing a wide style
range while PDMS stays above 0.90 (validation). Its main
limit: SAS is validated along assertiveness alone; the prox-
imity axis is near-degenerate on navtest, so whether social
style is independently controllable remains open, calling for
interaction-dense, reactive closed-loop evaluation. Validat-
ing style against human preference is the next step.
