Abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are gaining attention in autonomous driving,
inheriting commonsense priors from large language models (LLMs) and widening
the navigation interface from a fixed command set to free-form natural-language
instructions. However, this open-ended interface exposes a new vulnerability surface:
an inattentive driver may issue instructions that are unsafe given the current scene, and
a malicious user may issue dangerous ones on purpose. We present the first benchmark
suite that evaluates navigation following and conflict awareness together, along with
adversarial robustness. Existing VLAs fall short on both: SimLingo completes no
language-instructed lane change in closed loop, and all models degrade sharply under
conflicting signals. We propose SafeDriveVLA, the first driving VLA that (1) explicitly
predicts a three-level navigation-compliance mode to decouple conflict reasoning from
action generation, and (2) uses navigation-conditioned world model dreaming to inform
this prediction. Unlike prior world models that roll out expert or planned actions,
ours dreams of strictly following the navigation signal, showing when following is safe.
SafeDriveVLA reaches 88.16 Driving Score (DS) and 68.64% Success Rate (SR) on
Bench2Drive, lifts closed-loop language following by 49.3% and reduces collisions under
unsafe instructions from 60 to 38 compared to the best SimLingo variant. Video
demonstrations are available on our project page.

Introduction
Autonomous driving has shifted from modular pipelines [1, 2, 3] to end-to-end (E2E) systems [4, 5],
where the latest generation of E2E models, Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs), further widens the
navigation interface from a fixed command set to free-form natural-language instructions [6, 7, 8, 9].
VLAs are also entering production vehicles: Li Auto has rolled out its VLA driver model to customer
cars [10], and NVIDIA is bringing its Alpamayo VLA [7] to production in the Mercedes-Benz CLA [11].
This progression reduces inter-module error accumulation and scales better with large driving datasets,
but also removes the explicit safety mechanism that once resolved conflicts between the navigation signal
and the local scenarios.
Shown in Fig. 1, classical modular stacks routed the navigation signal through a rule-based behavior
planner [2, 12, 13] that resolved it against local context, such as traffic lights and pedestrians, before
making driving decisions. E2E systems instead internalize this judgment into the neural network, leaving
the model to learn from data alone whether to follow or delay a given navigation signal. VLAs amplify
this difficulty, as the navigation interface becomes an open-ended language space.
In this work, we present the first benchmark suite that evaluates navigation following and conflict awareness
together, since a model that ignores every instruction looks safe on a conflict benchmark, while one
that follows every instruction looks good on a following benchmark. It covers (i) navigation following
(CARLA-F), a closed-loop CARLA benchmark with traffic disabled so every navigation signal should be
executed; (ii) navigation-scene conflict (B2D-C and NavSim-C), unsafe instructions or commands triggered
at safety-critical events; and (iii) adversarial instructions (B2D-Adv), unsafe under any circumstance.
This split mirrors the helpfulness–harmlessness trade-off in LLM alignment [14, 15]. Existing VLAs fall
short on both: SimLingo completes none of its language-instructed lane changes in closed loop despite
an 83% open-loop rate [6], and all models degrade drastically under conflicting signals, executing them
without grounding in the scene.
Next, we propose SafeDriveVLA, to our knowledge, the first driving VLA that (i) explicitly predicts a
three-level navigation-compliance mode to decouple conflict reasoning from action generation, and (ii)
uses navigation-conditioned world model dreaming to inform this prediction. First, we relabel every
expert frame with a mode (strict, cautious, or fallback) for whether the expert executes, cautiously
follows, or overrides the signal, and emit this mode token before the action tokens, so conflict reasoning is
directly supervised rather than entangled with action generation. Second, unlike prior world models that
roll out expert or planned actions, our world model dreams of strictly following the navigation signal,
showing the model when following is safe and making it less conservative than with the mode token alone.
SafeDriveVLA reaches 88.16 Driving Score (DS) and 68.64% Success Rate (SR) on Bench2Drive, lifts
closed-loop language following by 49.3% over the best SimLingo variant, and decreases B2D-C collisions
from 60 to 38. Our contributions are: (1) Benchmarks for navigation following and navigation-scene
conflict awareness; (2) Design of explicit navigation-compliance modes that decouple conflict reasoning
from action generation, and of navigation-conditioned world model dreaming that shows when following is
safe; and (3) Evaluation on Bench2Drive and all our benchmarks.

Related Work
2.1
Language–Action Alignment for Autonomous Driving
VLAs extend VLMs with an action head, carrying semantic reasoning into driving models [16, 17] and
enabling instruction following beyond a fixed command set. DriveBench [18] shows that plausible language
outputs can rely on textual cues or general knowledge without reliable visual grounding, motivating
evaluation beyond answer fluency. Recent VLAs pursue instruction following through action dreaming [6],
driver-preference alignment [19], scaled instruction-following data [8], and related designs [20, 21, 22, 23,
24, 25, 26, 27, 9]. NoRD [28] explores data-efficient driving without dense reasoning annotations. We
instead study both sides of this capability, training a VLA to reliably follow safe navigation signals and to
recognize when a navigation signal conflicts with the surrounding scene.
Early language-driven systems already connect semantic reasoning with driving decisions. DriveGPT4 [29]
interprets driving actions and predicts low-level control signals. Dolphins [30] studies grounded conversa-
tional reasoning and reflection, while DriveVLM [31] couples scene analysis with hierarchical planning.
DriveMLM [32] maps multimodal reasoning into explicit behavioral planning states, and RDA-Driver [33]
constrains the correspondence between reasoning traces and planning outputs. LinkVLA [34] further
2


aligns language and action through a shared codebook and bidirectional supervision. These mechanisms
address decision representation and consistency; our mode token explicitly indicates whether the requested
maneuver should be executed, delayed, or overridden. DriveTeach-VLA [35] strengthens action-grounded
visual representations, and complementary VLM/vision-only systems [36] exploit their different strengths
across driving scenarios.
2.2
World Models and Prospective Action Reasoning
Driving world models split broadly into pixel-space generation [37, 38, 39, 40, 41] and latent-state
prediction [42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47]. WorldLens [48] evaluates visual, geometric, physical, and behavioral
fidelity, emphasizing that realistic generation alone does not establish reliable control. The agentic world-
modeling taxonomy [49] further emphasizes action-conditioned simulation and decision-centric evaluation.
Latent designs typically roll out under the offline-collected expert action: LCDrive [42] and Uni-World
VLA [50] interleave world-state and action prediction inside a unified VLA, and OneVL [43] predicts
latent futures before acting. A second line rolls out under candidate intentions drawn from the policy or a
trajectory vocabulary [46, 47] and evaluates the predicted future. Drive-JEPA [51] combines predictive
video representations with multimodal trajectory distillation, while Vault [52] couples single-step latent
trajectory generation with evaluator-guided training and candidate scoring. We condition the rollout on
the action implied by the navigation signal to expose navigation–scene conflicts.
Recent methods reason about action consequences before execution. CF-VLA [53] revises proposed
meta-actions using counterfactual reasoning over the visual context, while VLA-World [54] reasons over
generated future images to refine trajectories. DriveWorld-VLA [55] integrates action-conditioned world
states with the planner in a shared latent space. AD-R1 [56] trains a world-model critic on synthesized
dangerous outcomes to reduce optimistic forecasts during policy refinement, and ExploreVLA [57] uses
dense future prediction and uncertainty-based, safety-gated exploration. CoWorld-VLA and DriveDreamer-
Policy [58, 59] integrate complementary world representations or explicit geometry with action planning.
CausalDrive [60] studies reactive, action- and text-conditioned video simulation for closed-loop evaluation
and policy training. Our dreamed latents inform an explicit instruction-conflict decision before action
generation. Action-conditioned forecasting does not by itself establish event-specific causal counterfactual
prediction [61]; we use dreaming as a prospective decision aid.
Generative distillation also studies supervision on model-generated states. DanceOPD [62] composes
capability-specific velocity fields through distillation at student-induced states. DiffusionOPSD [63] con-
structs reward-guided targets for clean-output predictions during diffusion post-training. In SafeDriveVLA,
imagined states serve a decision-making role: a frozen predictor exposes the consequences of a navigation-
conditioned action anchor to inform conflict reasoning.
2.3
Robust Perception and Safety-Aware Driving
Early safety work targeted perception-level robustness and adversarial attacks [64, 65, 66, 67, 68, 69].
LargeAD [70] and multimodal data-efficient scene understanding [71] improve cross-sensor representations
and reduce annotation dependence, while Pi3DET [72] and 3EED [73] study cross-platform 3D detection
and language grounding. Complementary corruption benchmarks study HD map construction [74] and
monocular depth estimation [75]. Physics-informed, geometry-grounded weather synthesis [76] provides a
complementary approach to improving perception robustness through adverse-weather augmentation.
World modeling also extends to structured spatial representations: DynamicCity and DOME [77, 78] study
dynamic semantic occupancy generation and trajectory-controllable occupancy forecasting, respectively,
LiDARCrafter [79] models temporal LiDAR sequences, and U4D [80] incorporates uncertainty into 4D
LiDAR generation. Such representations complement image and latent prediction by capturing scene
geometry and temporal structure. For instruction-conditioned driving, reliable perception supplies the
evidence needed to interpret a maneuver in context: road layout, surrounding agents, and evolving hazards
can determine whether the same request remains feasible. Improvements in scene representation therefore
support conflict reasoning, although representation quality alone does not determine how a policy should
respond to an unsafe request.
SafeAuto [81] verifies predicted actions against logic-encoded traffic rules. DriveSafe’s risk-detection frame-
work [82] identifies scene-grounded hazards and generates safety suggestions, while VLM-AutoDrive [83]
adapts VLMs to temporally localized collisions and near-collisions. A separate DriveSafe taxonomy [84]
evaluates unsafe requests to LLM-based driving assistants. SafeDrive [85] evaluates fine-grained risks

2.4
Navigation and Grounded Driving Evaluation
Related embodied-navigation studies connect visual imagination with control. Language-conditioned
world modeling [88] studies learning policies in imagined latent space and jointly predicting actions and
observations, while UniWM [89] combines visual foresight with memory-augmented planning. These
settings focus on reaching navigation goals; our benchmark explicitly tests whether a requested maneuver
is safe in the current scene.
For driving VLAs, the same instruction can be safe or unsafe depending on the scene where it executes.
LMDrive [90] injects misleading instructions uniformly along the route, and ICR-Drive [91] studies
instruction counterfactual robustness. Our benchmarks place conflicting instructions at safety-critical
events and measure the resulting driving behavior.
The RoboDrive and RoboSense challenges [92, 93] extend robustness evaluation across adverse sensing
conditions, language-grounded decisions, navigation, and platform shifts. Bench2Drive-Robust [94] tests
closed-loop deployment perturbations, while ReactSim-Bench [95] measures surrounding agents’ responses
to ego behaviors that differ from the recorded log. Existing benchmarks assess different links between visual
evidence, reasoning, and action. LingoQA [96] measures the truthfulness of driving-video answers, while
Reason2Drive [97] evaluates perception–prediction–reasoning chains. DriveBench [18] diagnoses reliability
under visual and textual perturbations, and DriveAction [98] connects real driving operations with high-
level action labels and action-rooted evaluation. MSSBench [99], SCD-Bench [100], and DSBench [101]
assess situational safety or driving risks through model responses. These evaluations establish important
tests of grounding and risk awareness, but a correct explanation or discrete action answer does not
establish safe execution. We evaluate navigation following, navigation–scene conflict, and adversarial
instructions through closed-loop CARLA driving and real-world NavSim data, measuring the consequences
of the policy’s actions.
3
Benchmark
We use navigation signal for any input that tells the model where to go, in one of three modalities: (1) a
discrete command, drawn from a small fixed set (e.g., turn_left, go_straight); (2) a target waypoint, the
next route point in the ego frame; or (3) a free-form natural-language instruction such as “turn right at
the next intersection at 8 m/s”. The first two are produced by a route planner from a global GPS goal,
while the third is open-ended and can be issued directly by the driver [6, 8, 19].
Table 1 summarizes which navigation-signal modalities are used for each benchmark. We build benchmarks
encompassing three perspectives: (i) navigation-following capability (CARLA-F), the prerequisite for
language-conditioned driving; (ii) navigation-scene conflict awareness (B2D-C and NavSim-C), where the
navigation signal is unsafe given the current scene; and (iii) adversarial instructions (B2D-Adv), where
the instruction is unsafe to execute under any circumstances.

3.1
Navigation Following Benchmark
Conflict awareness requires the basic ability to follow safe signals; otherwise, models might behave
safely under unsafe instructions due to imperfect navigation-following capability. SimLingo [6] evaluates
instruction following open-loop, but this does not transfer to closed-loop driving (as we show in Sec. 5.3,
lane-change success drops to 0% in closed-loop evaluation). We therefore propose CARLA-F, a closed-loop
navigation-following benchmark covering all three modalities, with chained natural-language instructions
as the primary navigation signal.
Construction. For each meta-command (e.g., turn_left, lane_change_right), we pre-define a pool of
paraphrased natural-language instructions. To ensure safe navigation-following, we remove all background
traffic and force every light to green, so the model is expected to strictly execute every navigation signal.
Each route starts with a speed instruction followed by a chain of maneuver instructions. Fig. 2 visualizes
example routes, and more benchmark details are provided in Appendix A.1.1.
Metrics. We report Navigation Compliance Rate (NCR), the fraction of correctly executed signals per
meta-command (averaged with signal-count weights, excluding speed), and Speed Error (SE) w.r.t. the
instructed speed. Metric details are provided in Appendix A.1.2.
3.2
Navigation-Scene Conflict Benchmark
Next, we build the navigation-scene conflict benchmark on top of Bench2Drive (B2D) [102] and NavSim [103],
comprising two complementary subsets: unsafe language instructions in B2D-C(onflict) and unsafe com-
mands in NavSim-C(onflict).
B2D-C. For each Bench2Drive scenario, we manually design unsafe language instructions triggered at the
moment the safety-critical event occurs. The key property is that danger is contextual: the same surface
form could be safe in a different scene, forcing the model to reason about the instruction relative to the
current observation rather than blindly follow or refuse it. We adopt the same metrics as [102], Driving
Score (DS) and Success Rate (SR), and additionally report a per-infraction breakdown (collision, traffic
violation, out-of-route) for fine-grained analysis. More details in Appendix A.2.
NavSim-C. To complement CARLA with real-world sensor, we build a command-scene conflict subset
on the NavSim [103]. Following the setup, we trigger conflicts at the command level: we construct 300
scenarios by replacing the original command with a left or right turn that, if executed, would cause a
collision or leave the drivable area. We follow the NavSim evaluation protocol (NC, DAC, TTC, comfort,
EP, and the composite PDMS). The two conflict contexts (intersection turns and lane changes) and further
details are in Appendix A.3.

Adversarial Instruction Benchmark
Finally, we investigate a security benchmark in which the instruction is adversarial under any circumstance.
Similar to LLM jailbreaking [104], it tests whether the VLA can refuse directly harmful instructions.
Benchmark details are provided in Appendix A.4.
4
SafeDriveVLA
We introduce SafeDriveVLA, an autoregressive driving model that decouples conflict reasoning from action
generation. As shown in Fig. 3, it has three components: (i) every expert frame is relabeled with a
discrete driving mode (strict, cautious, or fallback) indicating whether the trajectory immediately executes,
cautiously follows, or overrides the navigation signal; (ii) an action-conditioned latent world model (WM)
rolls the scene under a per-meta-command action anchor, producing dreamed latents of strict execution;
(iii) a VLA backbone consumes the observations, the navigation signal, and the dreamed latents, then
autoregressively emits a mode token followed by discrete action tokens from a codebook [9], with an
auxiliary path head for lateral control [6].
4.1
Driving Mode Attribution
Expert demonstrations mix two implicit decisions: whether the navigation signal is currently safe to
execute, and what low-level action to take given that decision. Existing approaches see only the composed
trajectory [6, 9], so visually similar frames with the same navigation signal can carry different actions: an
immediate lane change when the adjacent lane is clear; a delayed one when a vehicle blocks it, or none
when the maneuver is unsafe. Naive behavior cloning must then learn this mixed decision implicitly from
the navigation-scene joint distribution.
We make these implicit decisions explicit on both the data and model sides. On the data side, we relabel
every expert frame with a discrete driving mode (strict, cautious, or fallback) according to whether the
recorded trajectory immediately executes the navigation signal, follows it while yielding to local hazards,
or overrides it because no safe action remains. On the model side, we extend the VLA vocabulary
with three special tokens (<STRICT>, <CAUTIOUS>, <FALLBACK>) and emit the predicted mode

4.2
Latent World Model
World Model Pre-Training. We pretrain a latent world model on the CARLA dataset [6] that predicts future
driving representations from the current observation and a sequence of ego actions, following V-JEPA 2
AC [105]. Specifically, we use a frozen V-JEPA 2 ViT-L target encoder and a Transformer-based [106]
action-conditioned predictor trained from scratch over a 2.5 s horizon at 2 Hz under teacher-forced and
autoregressive losses. The main difference is temporal information. V-JEPA 2 AC, evaluated for tabletop
manipulation, encodes each latent from a single frame [105]. However, the surrounding traffic velocity is
critical for driving and cannot be recovered from a single frame. We instead use two consecutive frames to
encode a latent representation, so every latent carries 0.25 s of motion. More training details are provided
in Appendix B.1.
World Model Probing. Navigation-conditioned dreaming relies on two properties of the pretrained predictor:
its rollouts must carry information about how the scene evolves, and they must respond to the input
actions. We verify both on held-out data (Table 2). First, we train a probe head to detect hazards from
encoder latents and apply it to 5,095 clips that start hazard-free. Predicted latents ˆzt, rolled out under
ground-truth actions, yield a higher hazard AUC than the frozen first-frame latent z0 at every horizon and
recover 49–78% of the gap to the encoder oracle zt, so the predictor forecasts upcoming hazards rather
than copying z0. Second, rollouts under ground-truth actions stay closer to the oracle than rollouts under
zeroed actions or laterally mirrored actions (longitudinal motion unchanged, lateral and yaw components
flipped) at every horizon, confirming that the predicted future depends on the action input. Probing
details are in Appendix B.3.
Navigation-Conditioned Dreaming. To make the model aware of conflicts between the navigation signal
and the surrounding scene, we use the pretrained world model to imagine future scene representations
conditioned on strict execution of the maneuver implied by the signal. Unlike prior driving world models
that roll out under the ground-truth expert action [42, 50], our predictor rolls out under a hypothetical
action sequence executing the current navigation signal. The resulting latent trajectory exposes whether
the implied maneuver is feasible in the current scene.

<ACTIONS> marks the start of the action span. The subsequent T = 10 tokens encode the future
trajectory at 4 Hz over the same 2.5 s horizon. Inspired by SimLingo [6], we further decouple the lateral
path from longitudinal control: a lightweight MLP head reads the LM hidden state at <ACTIONS> and
regresses 20 ego-frame path points, which feed a lateral PID controller at inference, while the discrete
action tokens are integrated for the longitudinal target speed.
Directly mapping free-form language instruction to action sequence is challenging. Therefore, to simplify
the problem, we precompute one action anchor per meta-command by averaging 2.5 s training trajectories
(Fig. 4; lane-change anchors are calibrated, Appendix C.4). At every frame, the anchor is indexed by
the meta-command implied by the navigation signal the model receives: (i) For a planner command,
the command itself is the meta-command. (ii) For a target waypoint, which the route planner derives
from the same GPS goal, we use the accompanying planner command. (iii) For a language instruction,
the meta-command is parsed from the instruction string by vocabulary matching (e.g., “turn left” →
turn_left). Then, the encoder consumes the current observations to produce the initial latent z0, and the
frozen WM predictor rolls it forward 5 steps under the indexed anchor to obtain the dreamed latents.

and go-straight, while matching or exceeding them on lane change.

6
Conclusion
We identify navigation-scene conflict as a critical yet underexplored vulnerability of end-to-end VLA driving,
and build a benchmark suite on which existing VLAs systematically follow navigation signals without
grounding them in the scene. Guided by these findings, we propose SafeDriveVLA, which decouples conflict
reasoning from action generation through explicit driving-mode attribution and navigation-conditioned
world-model dreaming, outperforming SimLingo on Bench2Drive while improving closed-loop navigation
following and reducing unsafe behavior under conflicting instructions. We hope this work raises awareness
of navigation-scene conflict as VLA driving models evolve rapidly and are being deployed in the real world.
7
Limitations
(1) The ground-truth driving modes are inferred from expert trajectories by kinematic rules rather than
annotated with the driver’s actual intent, so they may not fully capture the semantics of strict, cautious,
and fallback. (2) The language instructions in our benchmarks are sampled from fixed paraphrase pools,
so they do not cover fully free-form user inputs. (3) The world model is queried with a per-meta-command
action anchor, a coarse proxy of the navigation signal that does not encode speed; all speed instructions are
therefore dreamed under the follow_road anchor, which increases speed error. More flexible, speed-aware
world-model conditioning is a promising future direction. (4) Dreaming predicts plausible outcomes under
a hypothetical ego action and can inherit world-model errors, so the resulting mode predictions do not
provide a formal safety guarantee. Better-calibrated uncertainty and explicit modeling of surrounding
agents’ responses are promising future directions.

A.1.1
Language Instruction Following Generation
In this section, we discuss the details of the CARLA-F construction process. We visualize the generated
examples in Fig. 2.
Source routes and hazard removal.
The benchmark is generated on top of the Bench2Drive [102] routes.
For each source route, we keep only its starting position (i.e., GPS point) and the CARLA town, discard
the original waypoint polyline, and rebuild a fresh route from the CARLA driving graph so that every
exported segment is reachable by the official planner. To remove confounders from the safety-critical
setting, the generated routes disable background vehicles, pedestrians, and bicyclists, and the route sets
the traffic light green so that no scenario-driven unsafe behavior interferes with instruction compliance
scoring.
Counterfactual routes from the same start.
A natural failure mode for a vision-heavy planner is to
ignore the language token and reproduce whatever maneuver the visual scene most strongly suggests.
To probe this, our pipeline can emit several distinct benchmark routes from the same starting pose by
sampling different meta-commands at the same trigger and rebuilding the geometry under each one. The
visual context near the trigger point is therefore shared across these routes, while the instruction and the
ground-truth trajectory diverge.
Trigger selection and action sampling.
At every waypoint along the rebuilt follow route, we enumerate
the locally feasible actions from the CARLA topology, such as turns at the next junction, left/right lane
changes when a same-direction adjacent drivable lane exists, and follow_road as the trivial default. The
actionability score of a waypoint is defined as the count of feasible actions excluding follow_road. The
trigger is chosen as the earliest waypoint that attains the maximum actionability score; one of its feasible
actions is then sampled and paired with a natural language instruction drawn from the instruction pool
for that meta-command. The chaining loop in Alg. 1 repeats the procedure from the action endpoint, up
to a per-route cap, with a minimum spacing between consecutive triggers.
Language as the sole navigation signal.
Standard CARLA evaluation exposes a route planner to the
model in the form of a discrete command (e.g., turn_left, follow_road) or an upcoming target waypoint,
both of which already encode the intended maneuver. In our benchmark, for language evaluation setups,
we remove these channels and replace them with the sampled natural-language instruction, so that only
the language instruction telling the model where to drive is available. The route polyline and the planner
command are used solely for metric evaluation and are hidden from the model; the meta-command that
indexes the world-model action anchor is parsed from the same instruction string the model consumes.
This forces the model to ground the instruction in its own perception of the scene rather than reading the
maneuver off the planner.

A.3
NavSim-C
A.3.1
Benchmark Setup
NavSim [103] provides driving commands for the ego vehicle derived from its drivable path, covering
three primary navigation intents: go straight, turn left, and turn right. To construct our scene conflict
benchmark, we choose 300 scenarios from the NavSim dataset and deliberately inject conflicting driving
commands—commands that deviate from the ego’s intended drivable path and are likely to induce unsafe
driving behavior. Specifically, an unsafe outcome is defined as either (i) out-of-boundary driving, where
the ego vehicle exits the drivable area or crosses into the oncoming lane, or (ii) collision with other agents
in the scene.
We focus our evaluation on the turn left and turn right commands, as NavSim does not distinguish
between turning maneuvers and lane-change maneuvers. To avoid ambiguity, we ensure that every selected
scenario and its corresponding conflicting command would provoke an unsafe outcome under both possible
interpretations of these commands (i.e., as a turn or as a lane change).
These conflicts span two contexts: (1) at intersections, where the conflicting turn would steer the ego into
oncoming traffic or an occupied lane; and (2) during lane changes, where the implied lateral maneuver is
unsafe given adjacent occupancy or road boundaries. Covering both contexts broadens the benchmark
beyond turn-only junction cases.
A.3.2
Evaluation Metrics
We evaluate all models using the standard PDM Score (PDMS), which provides a comprehensive assessment
of driving quality across safety and efficiency dimensions. The PDMS is defined as:
PDMS = NC · DAC · 5 · TTC + 5 · EP + 2 · C
5 + 5 + 2
(1)
where the two multiplicative penalty terms gate the score based on safety-critical violations, and the
weighted average captures progress and comfort. The individual sub-metrics are described below:
• No At-Fault Collision (NC): A multiplier in {0, 1
2, 1} that penalizes the ego vehicle for causing collisions
with other agents or infrastructure. Any at-fault collision sharply reduces the overall score.
• Drivable Area Compliance (DAC): A binary multiplier in {0, 1} that enforces that the ego vehicle
remains within the drivable road surface. Driving off-road or into the oncoming lane results in a
zero score.

World Model Details
In this section, we discuss the detailed design of the latent world model.
19


B.1
Training
We pre-train the latent world model on the SimLingo CARLA dataset [6], using PDM-Lite expert
observations encoded by V-JEPA 2 [105].
The encoder is the frozen V-JEPA 2 ViT-L with tubelet_size=2. The predictor is a causal Transformer
decoder with 8 layers, 8 heads, embedding width 512, and MLP ratio 4×, totalling ≈25 M parameters.
At each step it consumes an interleaved sequence of action, state, and visual tokens. We index steps by
t ∈{0, . . . , 5}, with adjacent steps 0.5 s apart. The action at = (∆xt, ∆yt, ∆ψt) ∈R3 is the ego-frame pose
delta (longitudinal, lateral, and yaw) from step t to t+1, and the ego state st = [v, acclong, acclat, ˙ψ] ∈R4
stacks the speed, longitudinal and lateral accelerations, and yaw rate; it is provided only at t=0 with all
later states masked to zero.
V-JEPA 2 AC [105] was developed for tabletop manipulation, where each target frame It (the camera
image at step t) is duplicated into a pseudo-tubelet [It, It]. However, in driving world states, the velocity
of surrounding traffic is not recoverable from a single RGB frame. We instead build each tubelet from two
real consecutive frames [I−
t , It], where I−
t is the frame captured 0.25 s before It. Therefore, every latent zt
now carries a 0.25 s motion sample to capture scene dynamics. The action at is mapped to the predictor
width by a single linear layer. The ego state st is provided only at t=0, and the state inputs at all later
steps are zeroed. This is because at deployment, the downstream action-only VLA emits actions but never
future states, and masking st>0 during training keeps the closed-loop train/test distribution consistent.
We adopt two objectives: (1) A teacher-forced loss uses ground-truth latents as context and supervises all
5 next-step predictions ˆz1, . . . , ˆz5 in parallel; and (2) an auto-regressive loss starts from z0 and rolls k
steps forward by feeding predicted latents back into the predictor. The auto-regressive depth k is ramped
linearly from 2 to 5 over the first 40% of training procedures and held at 5 thereafter, so that the predictor
is progressively exposed to the full 2.5 s horizon. The overall objective is LWM = LTF + LAR with equal
weights, where both terms are per-token ℓ1 losses on LayerNorm-normalized target latents.
Training is performed for 20 epochs using half of the SimLingo collected data [6], with per-GPU batch
size 8. We use AdamW optimizer with lr=4.25×10−4, weight_decay=0.04. The loss curves are shown in
Fig. B.1.

C
Vision-Language-Action Model Details
In this section, we provide more details on the VLA model training.
C.1
Training Details
We fine-tune the InternVL3-1B [107] VLM with LoRA [113] (r=32, α=64, dropout 0.1), fed the front-
camera frame yielding 3,072 visual tokens. World-model inputs are resized to 256×256 to match the
V-JEPA 2 ViT-L input resolution. We optimize with AdamW (weight decay 0.1), learning rate 3×10−5,
batch size 12, for 14 epochs on 8× NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPUs. The training objective is
L = Laction + Lmode + Lpath + 0.5 Ltext, where Laction and Lmode are cross-entropy losses on the action
and mode tokens, Lpath is the MSE of the auxiliary path head over its 20 path points, and Ltext is the
cross-entropy on text tokens.
C.2
Mode Attribution Details
When PDM-Lite experts collect the ground truth trajectory for imitation learning, every frame inherits a
single meta-command from the integer command field of the corresponding measurement file, taking one
of six values: follow_road, go_straight, turn_left, turn_right, lane_change_left, and lane_change_-
right. For each frame, we classify the driving mode by inspecting the future 2.5 s of waypoints the expert
actually drove, together with the current speed and the road speed limit. The rule depends on the
meta-command of the frame:
# follow_road.
• Strict: the ego is at cruising speed (above 6 m/s), holding speed or accelerating over the window
(average future acceleration above -0.5 m/s2), and shows no meaningful lateral departure (largest
lateral deviation under 1.5 m).

C.3
Mode Prediction Accuracy
Beyond conditioning the action generation, the predicted mode token is itself an interpretable signal: it
exposes, before any action is committed, whether the policy reads the current scene as one that affords
progress (<STRICT>), demands caution (<CAUTIOUS>), or requires yielding (<FALLBACK>). We
therefore evaluate how reliably SafeDriveVLA recovers the rule-derived ground-truth mode. Specifically,
we sample 100 held-out frames per meta-command and compare the greedily decoded mode token against
the ground-truth label. Table 7 in the main paper reports the per-meta-command accuracy.
SafeDriveVLA attains 90.2% overall mode accuracy, indicating that the mode prediction is well matched
to the rule-derived labels rather than collapsing to a dominant mode. Accuracy is highest on the lane-
change commands (0.940 and 0.970), which might be due to the imbalanced ground-truth distribution
(Table 11). The two cruising commands follow_road and go_straight (0.890 and 0.930) are also predicted
reliably. The clear outlier is turn_left at 0.770, noticeably below its mirror turn_right at 0.910. We

oncoming-traffic and right-of-way cues.
C.4
Meta-Command Action Anchor
Here, we introduce how we generate the meta-command action anchor.
The world model in our VLA stack is a frozen V-JEPA 2 AC predictor that takes a chain of 5 ego-frame
actions at = (∆xt, ∆yt, ∆ψt), t = 0, . . . , 4, at 0.5 s interval (App. B.1) and returns the latent of the
resulting future visual observation, under the prompt “what would happen if I executed this maneuver.” A
naive choice for this chain is ego-history extrapolation, i.e., replicating the ego’s recent kinematics (mean
past speed and yaw rate). This is possible while the ego is cruising, but it misaligns with a deviation
instruction. We propose to index the action prior on the meta-command and feed the world model the
anchor action sequence per meta-command at each frame.
