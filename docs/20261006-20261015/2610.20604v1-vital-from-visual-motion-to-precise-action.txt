Abstract
World-action models aim to turn visual anticipation into driving decisions, yet typically
train action experts on trajectory representations defined independently of visual
dynamics. This motivates deriving action targets directly from visual motion. We
first examine a simpler setting in which trajectories are reconstructed from recorded
future images. Even without the need for future prediction, reconstruction captures the
dominant maneuver but leaves centimeter-scale errors. We therefore introduce VITAL
(Vision-Induced Tokens for Action Learning), an action tokenizer that couples a visually
derived motion scaffold with a compact residual encoding the trajectory error left by
that scaffold. This representation makes visual motion the principal prediction target
and gives small metric corrections a separate, normalized learning scale. Using flow
matching, the action expert jointly predicts both components from the current image,
ego state, and navigation command, without generating future images at inference. The
same residual coordinates support bounded reinforcement post-training that refines
execution while keeping the predicted visual scaffold fixed.
With only one action
denoising step, VITAL achieves 90.8 PDMS on NAVSIM v1 navtest, within 0.13 points
of its ten-step score at 3.29× the end-to-end inference speed. Without retraining, it
achieves 91.1 EPDMS on NAVSIM v2 navtest and 38.6 on navhard.

Introduction
Learning to anticipate a driving scene should also help a vehicle decide how to move. World-action models
pursue this connection by coupling visual prediction with trajectory generation (Xia et al., 2026a; Yuan
et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026). A common design couples video and action experts in a mixture of
transformers, allowing action tokens to attend to visual features before decoding an ego trajectory (Li
et al., 2026a; Yuan et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026). As illustrated in Fig. 1, the promise is to learn how to
act from a model that anticipates how the world moves.
Yet exchanging information does not necessarily give the two experts a shared prediction target. Atten-
tion (Vaswani et al., 2017) connects their features, while the video expert learns to predict scene evolution
and the action expert often learns a code defined only by trajectory coordinates. The two experts describe
the same physical motion through separately constructed representations. We therefore ask whether action
targets can be derived directly from observed visual motion.
Recent advances in robot control suggest that this connection can be learned at the tokenizer level.
RepWAM (Wang et al., 2026b) learns latent actions from transitions between semantically aligned visual
representations, and LingBot-VA 2.0 (Zhang et al., 2026a) adopts this strategy for joint pretraining of
video and action. Their gains in control precision make visual motion a promising source of action targets.
Motion-token and continuous-motion learning offer complementary evidence for connecting videos to
control (Chen et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026a). However, these tokenizers reconstruct transitions in visual
feature space. It remains unclear how much of the metric detail needed for driving can be recovered from
visual motion alone, even before a planner has to predict that motion from the observations available at
deployment.
We therefore remove future prediction from the problem altogether. As shown in Appendix A.1, in a
simpler task than world-action modeling, the model receives recorded current and future images and
reconstructs the corresponding ego trajectory. Even with the future already in view, a precision gap
remains. As shown in Fig. 2, reconstruction leaves 123.6 mm average displacement error (ADE) and
221.6 mm final displacement error (FDE) across all NAVSIM validation scenes (Dauner et al., 2024).
Looking more closely at the recovered motion, we find that its HF fraction, the mean share of velocity
energy at high frequencies across x and y, is only 0.1%. What emerges is a smooth motion scaffold. It
captures the maneuver but still misses metric detail that matters near road boundaries, where small
deviations can make an otherwise plausible trajectory noncompliant.

Precise reconstruction is useful for planning only if the representation can be predicted before the future is
observed, consistent with recent analyses of action tokenization beyond reconstruction fidelity (Chen et al.,
2026). We train the action expert with flow matching to generate both codes jointly from the current
image, ego state, and navigation command. One flow step then produces a trajectory without generating
future images. Separating the metric correction also opens a route to policy improvement (Johannink
et al., 2019). After imitation learning, we freeze the action generator and decoder and learn bounded
updates in the residual coordinates. At deployment, a single deterministic correction refines local execution
while keeping the predicted visual scaffold fixed.

The gains extend from reconstruction to planning. On NAVSIM, VITAL strengthens visual conditioning.
With equal action training budgets, it gains 5.1 PDMS points in one step over an autoencoder trained
only on trajectories. Residual refinement brings the final policy to 90.8 PDMS in one step, within 0.1
points of ten steps at 3.29× the inference speed, including image encoding.
Our contributions bridge visual motion, precise action learning, and efficient policy refinement.
• We propose VITAL, an action tokenizer that pairs visual motion with a normalized metric residual to
preserve both visual grounding and fine trajectory precision for autonomous driving.
• We propose residual reinforcement learning that improves planning through a single correction, without
additional denoising, candidate search, or reward evaluation at inference.
• We achieve 90.8 PDMS on NAVSIM v1 with a single flow evaluation. Without retraining, the same
policy reaches 91.1 EPDMS on NAVSIM v2 and 38.6 EPDMS on reactive navhard.

Related Work
2.1
World-action models
The survey of 3D and 4D world modeling organizes video-, occupancy-, and LiDAR-based approaches
and their evaluation protocols (Kong et al., 2025b). Quo Vadis, World Modeling? broadens this view to
agent-centric proxies that provide dynamics, spatial, execution, memory, skill, and reward feedback (Yang
et al., 2026b). Together, these perspectives distinguish scene prediction from the information a policy
needs to select and improve executable actions.
World-action models connect visual prediction to control by using generated futures or their latent
representations to inform action generation. Drive-WM evaluates candidate plans through generated
multiview videos (Wang et al., 2024), while MaskGWM combines video generation with masked recon-
struction to learn generalizable driving dynamics (Ni et al., 2025). DriveLaW injects video-generator
latents into a trajectory planner (Xia et al., 2026a), and SUV lets the action expert attend to predicted
appearance, semantics, relative depth, and instance dynamics (Yuan et al., 2026). Metis and SimWAM
use attention masks to support joint video-action training while avoiding future-video generation during
action inference (Li et al., 2026a; Zhao et al., 2026).
Latent world models also support driving without reconstructing full future videos.
LAW predicts
action-conditioned future features to improve scene representations (Li et al., 2025c), while World4Drive
incorporates intention-aware physical latent modeling (Zheng et al., 2025).
Latent-WAM combines
geometry-informed scene compression with dynamic latent prediction (Wang et al., 2026d). A separate use
of future prediction is to evaluate plans: WoTE forecasts BEV states for online trajectory evaluation (Li
et al., 2025d), and DA-WAM predicts an action-specific future latent for each candidate and uses it for
scoring (Zhong et al., 2026).
WorldLens evaluates driving world models across generation, reconstruction, action-following, downstream
tasks, and human preference (Liang et al., 2026b). The associated workshop study, Is Your Driving World
Model an All-Around Player?, examines six driving world models across 24 evaluation dimensions within
the same WorldLens framework (Kong et al., 2026a). These evaluations show that visual realism alone does
not establish geometric or behavioral fidelity. This motivates assessing the control interface separately:
our recorded-future reconstruction study isolates retained action precision, while conditional prediction
and NAVSIM scores test whether that representation supports planning from current observations.
Broader surveys place both directions within agentic world modeling and vision-language-action driving
systems (Chu et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2025a). LCVN studies language-conditioned navigation through
latent imagination or joint observation-action prediction, while UniWM couples future prediction with
memory-augmented planning (Dong et al., 2026b,c). OneVL uses language and future-video supervision
to learn compact reasoning tokens, discarding auxiliary decoders during planning inference (Lu et al.,
2026a). Beyond video, DynamicCity generates dynamic occupancy, while LiDARCrafter and U4D model
controllable or uncertainty-aware LiDAR sequences (Bian et al., 2025; Liang et al., 2026a; Xu et al.,
2026b). OmniLiDAR extends diffusion-based scan generation across weather, sensor configurations, and
acquisition platforms through a shared range-image representation and domain-conditioned modeling (Liu
et al., 2026c). These scene-generation interfaces provide a broader context for our focus on visually
grounded, metric action targets.

Related strategies appear in robotic manipulation. Prediction with Action couples visual and action
prediction through joint denoising (Guo et al., 2024), and UWM uses independent video and action
diffusion timesteps within one transformer to support policy and dynamics modeling (Zhu et al., 2025).
WorldVLA studies autoregressive action-world modeling (Cen et al., 2025); VideoVLA and DreamZero
leverage pretrained video generators to jointly predict actions and visual outcomes (Shen et al., 2025; Ye
et al., 2026). Video Prediction Policy uses predictive visual representations with inverse dynamics (Hu
et al., 2025b), while DreamVLA forecasts compact dynamic, spatial, and semantic knowledge for action
prediction (Zhang et al., 2025d). V-JEPA 2-AC plans through an action-conditioned predictor in visual
feature space (Assran et al., 2025). Gen2Act transfers information from generated human videos to
manipulation (Bharadhwaj et al., 2025), and World Guidance predicts compact conditions derived from
future observations alongside actions (Su et al., 2026).
VITAL constructs the action target from recorded visual motion and explicitly encodes the metric trajectory
error left unresolved by that representation. Future images supply training supervision and target-
construction inputs; deployment predicts the paired action codes from current context without generating
future images or scoring trajectory candidates.
2.2
Representation alignment and latent actions
REPA aligns diffusion-model features with pretrained visual representations (Yu et al., 2025). For driving,
Drive-JEPA learns predictive video representations for planning (Wang et al., 2026e), ReWorld supervises
intermediate video and action representations through prediction, alignment, and geometric discrimina-
tion (Xia et al., 2026b), and RAP introduces 3D geometric supervision through rasterization (Feng et al.,
2026). Cross-modal alignment also supports 3D perception: CLIP2Scene and Seal transfer pretrained
visual semantics to point clouds (Chen et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023b), while LargeAD scales cross-sensor
pretraining and LaserMix++ exploits multimodal consistency under limited annotation (Kong et al.,
2025a, 2026b). PointLoRA adapts point-cloud representations with low-rank updates and token selec-
tion (Wang et al., 2025a); Pi3DET and 3EED examine cross-platform detection and language-grounded
3D localization (Li et al., 2025b; Liang et al., 2025).
Representation design also shapes the fidelity of driving perception. RangeFormer addresses projection-
induced semantic and geometric distortions in range-view LiDAR segmentation (Kong et al., 2023a),
while LaserMix uses spatially structured beam mixing and prediction consistency to learn from sparsely
labeled scans (Kong et al., 2023c). RoboBEV evaluates BEV perception under camera corruptions, sensor
failures, and domain shifts (Xie et al., 2025b). These works concern perception accuracy, label efficiency,
and robustness; our complementary question is how an action representation preserves metric detail and
remains predictable from visual conditioning.
Genie and LAPA infer latent actions from visual transitions, making videos without action annotations
useful for controllable generation or policy pretraining (Bruce et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2025). Moto learns
motion tokens for video pretraining and subsequently connects them to robot control (Chen et al., 2025).
UniVLA uses language-conditioned, task-centric latent actions to reduce task-irrelevant variation (Bu
et al., 2025). CoMo instead learns continuous latent motion, addressing information loss in discrete
representations and the gap to continuous actions (Yang et al., 2026a). Visual transitions can contain
exogenous changes, motivating supervision that separates action-relevant motion from distractors (Nikulin
et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a).
RepWAM (Wang et al., 2026b) and LingBot-VA 2.0 (Zhang et al., 2026a) learn latent actions as transitions
between semantically aligned visual states, with transport and residual components for reconstructing
visual changes. RLA-WM learns residual latent actions from DINO feature differences and models their
distribution through flow matching (Zhang et al., 2026b). The residual in VITAL has a different target:
the metric difference between a visually decoded trajectory scaffold and the demonstrated trajectory.
Trajectory supervision grounds the scaffold in physical coordinates, and a normalized compact residual
code retains its remaining reconstruction errors. Both components are jointly predicted for planning, and
the residual coordinates also support bounded refinement.
2.3
Action tokenization and reconstruction fidelity
FAST efficiently tokenizes labeled action sequences (Pertsch et al., 2025). VQ-BeT uses hierarchical vector
quantization for multimodal behavior generation (Lee et al., 2024), while VQ-VLA scales vector-quantized

action tokenizers (Wang et al., 2025b). QueST learns temporal skill abstractions with a flexible quantized
latent representation (Mete et al., 2024). OAT structures the token sequence for compression, total
decodability, and ordered prediction, allowing prefix-based decoding to trade inference cost for action
fidelity (Liu et al., 2026a). VITA additionally learns shared discrete codes that decode into future frames
and robot actions (Ma et al., 2026). For autonomous driving, LaPla combines quantized trajectory
representation learning with continuous latent prediction to address the discrete-to-continuous planning

VITAL instead assigns separate targets to visually grounded motion and the metric error remaining after
scaffold reconstruction.
Reconstruction fidelity is only one requirement of a useful policy interface. BAAT aligns quantized
representations of behaviorally corresponding action chunks jointly with reconstruction (Dong et al.,
2026a). ProAct explicitly studies token predictability and robustness of decoded policy outputs beyond
clean action reconstruction (Chen et al., 2026). Our analysis separates clean-code reconstruction from
conditional code prediction, while downstream planning scores test usefulness for control; improved
reconstruction alone does not establish improved planning.
2.4
Few-step action generation
Diffusion Policy models multimodal action sequences through iterative denoising (Chi et al., 2025), while
flow matching and rectified flow learn velocity fields that transport noise to data (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu
et al., 2023a).
Consistency Models learn direct noise-to-data mappings (Song et al., 2023), and Consistency Policy
and One-Step Diffusion Policy distill iterative policies into faster samplers (Prasad et al., 2024; Wang
et al., 2025c). Shortcut Models condition generation on step size and use self-consistency to support
different sampling budgets (Frans et al., 2025); FlowPolicy applies consistency flow matching to one-step
robot control (Zhang et al., 2025b). DanceOPD learns from capability-specific velocity fields queried at
student-induced states, and DiffusionOPSD converts reward guidance into refreshed diffusion post-training
targets (Zhou et al., 2026a,b). Both study on-policy target construction for generative models. MeanFlow
learns average velocity over an interval (Geng et al., 2025), and conditional optimal-transport couplings
encourage straighter flow trajectories for few-step control (Sochopoulos et al., 2025). FLUX applies
rectified flow to efficient cross-embodiment navigation and follows a static-to-dynamic curriculum with
reinforcement learning in social environments (Gong et al., 2026). Where this approach shapes the flow
and training curriculum, our approach changes the paired action targets while retaining the standard
flow-matching objective. Streaming Flow Policy permits action execution during integration (Jiang et al.,
2025).
DRIFT, the residual flow adapter for VLMs, combines a coarse base predictor with iterative flow-based
refinement of a localized residual distribution (Liu et al., 2026e). Its iterative refinement differs from
our joint prediction of scaffold and metric residual codes. In driving, DiffusionDrive uses truncated
denoising (Liao et al., 2025), while MeanFuser combines one-step generation of multiple proposals with
adaptive reconstruction (Wang et al., 2026c).
VITAL retains the standard flow-matching prediction
objective and changes the action representation supplied to it. The paired scaffold and normalized metric
residual support one-step prediction of a single executable trajectory without teacher distillation or
candidate selection.

Residual policy refinement
Reward-based refinement addresses a limitation of imitation learning: matching demonstrations does
not directly optimize deployment performance. DPPO optimizes diffusion policies through reinforcement
learning (Ren et al., 2025), and ReinFlow adapts flow-matching policies with online RL (Zhang et al.,

optimized for value (Park et al., 2025).

It emphasizes the quality of learned feedback for optimization. Simulation-grounded video–trajectory
alignment also supplies matched rendered-video supervision for reward-optimized driving trajectories (Jing

Learning Precise Actions from Visual Motion
VITAL combines visual motion scaffolds with paired metric residuals to connect target construction, action
prediction, and reward-based refinement through one executable representation.
3.1
Problem Setup and Method Overview
We study visually conditioned action planning from context c comprising observations, agent state, and
task instruction. Our driving implementation uses SimWAM’s video/action architecture (Zhao et al.,
2026). Training pairs c with recorded images V + and a demonstrated trajectory τ ∈RT ×da, with T points
and da coordinates per point. Fig. 3 summarizes the method. Future images in V + provide training
supervision, while deployment uses only c to predict trajectories. In driving, the target trajectory contains
40 ego-frame position and heading samples over four seconds. Training proceeds in three stages: fitting
and freezing the visual–residual codec, learning a causal predictor of its paired targets, and optimizing
a small residual actor around the frozen imitation policy. The target encoder and the deployed visual
conditioning network have separate parameters, so recorded future observations shape the supervision
without becoming an input to the deployed planner.

3.5
Structure-Preserving Residual Policy Refinement
The residual decoder provides a common coordinate system for imitation learning and policy improve-
ment (Johannink et al., 2019). Residual control and latent-noise steering provide related ways to improve
imitation policies (Ankile et al., 2025; Wagenmaker et al., 2025); here the intervention is confined to the
paired metric residual. After imitation learning, we freeze the action generator, its visual conditioning,
and the complete codec. A residual actor reads the frozen policy’s hidden states, current context, and
base prediction, then outputs correction coordinates qϕ ∈Rk. Reward optimization uses these coordinates
to refine execution around the predicted scaffold.
A bounded correction space. We project a family of smooth trajectory perturbations into the residual
decoder’s span. Regularized whitening of the decoded Gram matrix balances the physical scales of these
directions, giving a fixed matrix B ∈Rdr×k. A radial limiter ΠC rescales each proposed update to satisfy
task-defined bounds on decoded motion and token magnitude.
In driving, six longitudinal and lateral perturbation templates define the correction family. Their projection
and whitening are computed from the frozen decoder geometry, then calibrated using training observations.
The limiter enforces maximum positional and heading displacements of 1 m and 0.2 rad, respectively,
together with a bound on residual-token magnitude. For τIL = D(ˆzv, ˆzr), the correction uses the same

coordinates. These properties preserve the representation’s structure without guaranteeing improved
reward or task-level safety for every individual correction.
Reward optimization. We treat each training scenario as a contextual bandit, selecting one trajectory and
receiving its task reward. The frozen base policy identifies a hard-context pool H and a disjoint set of
high-scoring anchors A. Hard contexts receive reward exploration, while anchors regularize the actor
toward zero correction. Gaussian exploration replaces qϕ with qϕ + σξ, where ξ ∼N(0, I), producing the

The prior Ωpenalizes the decoded mean correction before limiting. Paired positive and negative Gaussian

details the basis, limiter, and reward estimator.
At deployment, the actor applies its deterministic mean correction once, using the same basis and
normalization as during training. It requires no reward evaluation, candidate selection, or additional
denoising.
The complete policy therefore combines one action-expert evaluation with one bounded
correction in the existing metric residual coordinates of the learned action representation.

Structure-Preserving Residual Post-Training
Residual post-training adds 0.6 PDMS at NFE= 1 and 0.7 at NFE= 10 through local corrections. As
shown in Tab. A15, three training seeds yield positive gains across the tested reward pools and prior
weights. Corrected v2 EPDMS also improves after refinement. Appendix B.2 shows that refinement
improves Stage 1 while slightly lowering Stage 2 EPDMS. Local corrections help execution but cannot
replace a change to the predicted maneuver when the continuation requires one. The refinement mechanism
keeps the action generator and predicted visual scaffold fixed, so its gain reflects execution changes within
the bounded residual space. Its behavior on reactive continuations also makes the scope of this intervention
concrete: a local correction can adjust a path around the existing plan, but a substantially different
continuation may require reconsidering the maneuver itself.

5
Conclusion
In this paper, we present VITAL, a framework for world-action planning that jointly addresses visual–action
alignment and metric precision. Guided by our reconstruction study, we pair a visual motion scaffold
with a normalized metric residual to preserve both visual grounding and trajectory precision. We further
develop residual reinforcement post-training that refines planning through bounded corrections while
preserving the predicted scaffold. With only one denoising step, VITAL achieves 90.8 PDMS on NAVSIM
v1, 91.1 EPDMS on v2 and 38.6 EPDMS on navhard. The controlled reconstruction and action-learning
studies indicate that visual grounding and metric fidelity are complementary properties of an action target.
Recorded visual motion provides a useful scaffold, while its paired residual preserves execution detail and
supplies a shared coordinate system for subsequent refinement. At deployment, both components are
predicted from current observations, and reward evaluation is unnecessary.
13


Our experiments also delineate the scope of this design. Clean-code precision does not remove conditional
prediction error, and bounded residual refinement cannot replace a different maneuver when reactive
continuations demand one. Extending the approach to broader visual contexts and more interactive
planning settings remains an important direction for future work.

Method Details and Analysis
A.1
Target Representation
Driving instantiation. We instantiate the trajectory interface in Section 3.1 with T = 40 and da = 3. Each
trajectory point contains planar position and heading in the current ego frame, sampled at 10 Hz over a
four-second prediction horizon. The deployment context contains the current front-camera image, ego
state, and navigation command. The tokenizer uses L = 4 visual tokens with dv = 64, and the complete
residual code has dimension dr = 48. Pairing each visual token with twelve residual coefficients gives a
4 × 76 interface used by the action predictor and decoder.
Tokenizer architecture and fitting. The target encoder adapts VGGT-Ω(Wang et al., 2026a) through LoRA
on five front-camera frames at 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 seconds, keeping the pretrained base weights fixed. Its learned
temporal encoder combines camera features, register summaries, inter-frame register correspondences
and relative camera-motion features. Image-estimated monocular translation is treated as non-metric,
with trajectory supervision supplying metric scale. Two temporal layers and two interval-mixing layers
produce four 64-dimensional motion tokens. A width-256 decoder predicts body-frame forward/lateral
velocities and yaw rates, bounded through its output parameterization and integrated on the 40-point,
10 Hz trajectory grid. The tokenizer is fitted using trajectory, increment and keyframe reconstruction,
with auxiliary motion-smoothness and latent-conditioning terms. Tokenizer fitting also uses noisy-code
reconstruction and an auxiliary flow task conditioned on a 256-dimensional feature from the jointly encoded
five-frame sequence. Before the tokenizer’s temporal mixer, this feature fuses the first-frame camera head
hidden state and register mean, attention pooling over the first-frame registers, and relative camera-motion
features. Latent-velocity supervision and a decoded loss on one-step noise-to-latent predictions update
the tokenizer during fitting. The deployed action learner uses the causal current-frame features described
in Appendix A.2. The visual tokenizer is selected by held-out reconstruction error. The encoder, LoRA
adapters, and decoder are then frozen to fit the paired residual to their reconstruction errors.
For reproducibility, the tokenizer’s principal reconstruction weights are 1 (position), 0.25 (increments),
0.75 (keyframes), and 0.1 (heading). The noisy-code standard deviation is 0.10, with reconstruction weight
0.15. The latent-flow and one-step decoded-probe weights are both 0.05, ramped over 3,000 updates.
Other coefficients are 0.1 for body velocity, 0.02 for yaw rate, 0.002 for acceleration, 2 · 10−5 for jerk, 0.01
for boundary continuity, and 0.01/0.02/0.05 for latent mean/variance/covariance. The planner losses use
the separate weights specified in Equation 4.
Paired metric residual coordinates. For interval j, stack its ten (x, y, ψ) pose errors into ej ∈R30. The pose

Planner training and causal conditioning. The driving-pretrained video/action foundation and isolated

Experimental Protocol and Additional Results
B.1
Data, Evaluation, and Comparison Protocol
Data partitions. Action learning uses 85,109 training scenes and a fixed, log-balanced validation panel of
4,096 scenes from 212 logs. Reconstruction uses all 18,179 validation scenes. Frozen-feature probes use a
nested panel of 512 scenes, while latency and solver diagnostics use 64 scenes. The tokenizer, normalization
statistics, residual basis, trajectory-only autoencoder, and residual actor are fitted only on training data.
Panels are selected deterministically without rewards or model errors, and each comparison uses identical
scenes.
Trajectory error in the action-learning experiment is evaluated at the eight nominal planning times
0.5, 1.0, . . . , 4.0 seconds. Clean-code reconstruction, ridge probes, and solver diagnostics instead use the
40-point, 10 Hz target. Errors from the two grids are not compared directly.
Metric components. NAVSIM v1 reports PDMS and five components comprising no at-fault collision (NC),
drivable-area compliance (DAC), ego progress (EP), time-to-collision (TTC), and comfort (C). NAVSIM
v2 reports EPDMS with nine components comprising NC, DAC, driving-direction compliance (DDC),
traffic-light compliance (TLC), EP, TTC, lane keeping (LK), history comfort (HC), and extended comfort
(EC). All reported scores are scaled from 0 to 100.
Planner configuration.
The planner uses 384 × 672 front images and the video/action backbone of
SimWAM (Zhao et al., 2026). VITAL replaces its action target with four [64 | 12] visual–residual tokens
supplied by a separately trained, frozen tokenizer. The residual actor has 1.23M parameters and outputs
six smooth correction coordinates. The imitation model, visual prefix, residual basis, decoder, and
normalization remain frozen. Across the NFE sweep, only the number of action-flow evaluations changes.
Each scene yields one trajectory from a fixed inference seed, followed by one deterministic residual
correction, with no reward-based candidate ranking or trajectory ensemble.
Released-checkpoint evaluation.
We evaluate the released SUV (Yuan et al., 2026) and SimWAM (Zhao
et al., 2026) imitation checkpoints on NAVSIM v1 navtest, using their native preprocessing and condi-
tioning. SUV consumes four front-camera observations, scene-dependent text, and 11 ego-state scalars.
Its inference jointly generates future visual tokens and actions. SimWAM uses the current front image,
a fixed text prompt, and eight ego/navigation scalars, with action-only inference. Their native image
resolutions are 384×640 and 384×672, respectively. Each checkpoint is evaluated at one and ten sampling
steps without retraining or candidate selection. Scores are computed independently for each predicted
trajectory against the PDM reference, using the same v1 metric cache and scorer configuration as VITAL.
Both use bfloat16 inference. Their native random seeds, 42 for SUV and 0 for SimWAM, are held fixed
across sampling budgets. Table 1 marks these reproduced measurements with a dagger and also reports
the imitation-trained VITAL before residual post-training.
Published comparisons and score conventions. Other results are taken from the cited papers. Inputs,
foundation models, training data, and proposal-selection mechanisms differ across these systems. Camera
counts refer to physical views, including those stitched into a panorama. Temporal counts include current
and past camera observations used for inference, excluding future prediction targets and ego-state history.
We check these settings against the cited papers and their public inference configurations. The n.r. label
identifies frame counts that are not publicly reported.

Comparison Protocol and Limitations
C.1
Protocol for the Cross-Model Comparison
Figure 5 compares released DiffusionDrive (Liao et al., 2025), SUV (Yuan et al., 2026), and SimWAM-
RL (Zhao et al., 2026) weights with VITAL on the same NAVSIM v1 scene. All models predict four seconds
of motion from their native inputs. DiffusionDrive uses the current three front-facing cameras and LiDAR,
with two DDIM steps over 20 proposals. SUV uses four front-camera frames, scene-dependent text, and
an 11-dimensional ego-state vector, jointly generating future visual tokens and actions. SimWAM-RL and
VITAL use the current front image and eight ego/navigation scalars. VITAL uses one sampling step, while
SUV and SimWAM-RL use ten. All three use bfloat16 inference. DiffusionDrive uses its native float32
implementation. All planners use inference seed zero.
Each prediction is scored independently against the same PDM reference. ADE and FDE compare
positions at eight times from 0.5 to 4.0 seconds. VITAL’s 10 Hz output contains these times exactly, so no
interpolation is required. The BEV panels share the same metric viewport, and the insets magnify the
same spatial region. Map and agent overlays use the recorded scene annotations.
As shown in Figure 5, VITAL follows the recorded path more closely and reduces endpoint overshoot relative

The residual space cannot replace the visual scaffold. The final actor changes six smooth coordinates while
holding the predicted visual token fixed. This restriction keeps exploration local around the pretrained
plan, so it cannot reliably repair a wrong high-level route choice or a large visual-prediction error. The
Stage 2 regression on reactive navhard in Appendix B.2 further motivates refinement that can revise the
scaffold when local corrections cannot adapt to a changed scene.
Perceptual grounding and environmental robustness. Our evaluation does not establish robustness to adverse
weather, sensor corruptions, or unreliable visual-language reasoning. DriveBench shows that plausible
driving answers need not be visually grounded, while Open-o3-Video explicitly localizes spatio-temporal
evidence for video reasoning (Meng et al., 2026; Xie et al., 2025a). Robo3D benchmarks corruption
sensitivity in 3D perception, and geometry-grounded weather synthesis provides a complementary way to
study adverse conditions (Kong et al., 2023b; Qian et al., 2026). These findings motivate evaluating the
visual scaffold under such perturbations: bounded metric refinement cannot compensate for arbitrary
errors in the underlying scene interpretation.
D
Potential Societal Impact & Limitations
D.1
Potential Societal Impact
VITAL connects visual motion to the metric precision needed to execute a driving maneuver. More
accurate local trajectories could help a planner respect road boundaries and negotiate nearby traffic,
while fewer flow evaluations could reduce the computation required for timely planning. The reported
gains establish improvements in benchmark scores and measured inference latency. Their contribution to
safer transportation depends on how the planner is integrated, validated, and monitored as part of an
autonomous driving system that interacts with other road users.
Safety and distributional risks. A planner can improve average PDMS while making individual scenes
less safe. As shown in Appendix C.3, a small residual correction can move a trajectory beyond the road
boundary. Errors in visual interpretation, unusual road layouts, and interactions absent from the training
distribution may therefore expose pedestrians, cyclists, and vehicle occupants to harm. Benchmark
averages do not establish equal performance across locations, weather conditions, or road-user groups.
29


Deployment requires evaluation in the intended operating domain, tests of rare interactions, independent
safety constraints, and a fallback for unreliable predictions.
Data and computational considerations. The experiments reuse released driving observations and anno-
tations. These recordings may contain information about people and places, so access, visualization,
and redistribution remain subject to the source datasets’ privacy protections and use conditions. Public
availability does not imply unrestricted reuse. Although one-step inference reduces measured latency, the
study relies on substantial pretrained models and GPU training. Assessing environmental impact requires
energy and emissions measurements across pretraining, tokenizer fitting, and planner optimization. These
measurements are not reported in the present study.
D.2
Known Limitations
Simulation and transfer. The experiments assess planning within the NAVSIM benchmark. Generalization
to unseen cities and robustness to sensor failures or prolonged interaction in real traffic remain open
challenges. The stage-dependent behavior in Appendix B.2 motivates training that accounts for how
surrounding traffic responds as the scene evolves beyond the initial observation.
Representation and control scope. The residual policy specializes in local refinement around a predicted
maneuver. As shown in Appendix C.3, this scope leaves incorrect route choices and large visual prediction
errors for future work. Broader control over the visual scaffold and explicit safety constraints could address
these limitations, as bounded corrections can still cross road boundaries.
