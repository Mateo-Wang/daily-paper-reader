ManeuverFlow: Maneuver Hypothesis Generation with Future Evidence for
Trajectory Evaluation and Refinement in End-to-End Autonomous Driving
Yuhao Wei1, Yong Chen2, Chen Yang1, Yingchen Wang1, Delin Ouyang1,
Lingfeng Qi1, Qi Lan1, Zhanhua Zhang2, Jie Li1, Guofa Li1,*
1Chongqing University
2Artificial Intelligence Center, Geely Automotive Research Institute
Abstract
End-to-end planning in complex scenes must model diverse
driving intentions and select the most appropriate trajec-
tory using scene context and future risks. However, multi-
query regression may collapse to one mode, while anchor-
based and generative planners share a scaling bottleneck:
small candidate sets limit maneuver coverage or discov-
ery of high-quality trajectories, whereas large sets increase
generation and scoring costs. We propose ManeuverFlow
to generate trajectories for distinct intentions in one step
and evaluate and refine them using current and future evi-
dence. The model selects relevant maneuver modes, draws
corresponding noise from the Gaussian mixture prior, and
maps it to intention-specific trajectories through a maneuver-
routed mixture-of-experts MeanFlow field. Current features
and future features predicted by the world model are ag-
gregated along each candidate path into trajectory-specific
evidence for PDM-factor prediction. Using the same evi-
dence, the refiner predicts local Frenet residuals for selected
candidates; refined and retained candidates are then jointly
re-scored for selection. With a lightweight TransFuser-style
ResNet-34 backbone, ManeuverFlow achieves 90.6 PDMS
and 90.1 EPDMS on NAVSIM v1/v2 navtest, respectively.
On WorldEngine’s recent 3DGS closed-loop benchmark, it
attains the highest listed PDMS∗scores of 75.19 and 74.58
under logged and IDM-controlled traffic, respectively. Code:
https://github.com/wyh-autonomy/maneuverflow.

Introduction
End-to-end autonomous driving maps sensor observations,
ego states, and navigation cues directly to future ego mo-
tion, unifying perception and planning under a single ob-
jective (Chitta et al. 2023; Hu et al. 2022b, 2023; Jiang
et al. 2023; Jia et al. 2023; Sun et al. 2025). Complex traf-
fic scenes often admit multiple plausible driving hypotheses,
so a single trajectory cannot capture the underlying deci-
sion space. Multimodal motion forecasting represents this
ambiguity with fixed anchor vocabularies or mode-specific
queries (Chai et al. 2020; Shi et al. 2022); recent planners
further use learned queries, trajectory anchors, and diffusion-
or flow-based generators to produce candidates, then select a
final plan through candidate–scene interactions (Jiang et al.
2026; Li et al. 2024b; Liao et al. 2025; Xing et al. 2025; Wang

Candidate generation also requires reliable selection.

do not explicitly query predicted future scene states along

future rollout per candidate (Fig. 1(b)), repeating future BEV

evaluate, refine, and select them?
To this end, we propose ManeuverFlow, a unified
Hypothesize–Verify–Improve framework that preserves ex-
plicit maneuver-indexed hypotheses, as shown in Figs. 1(c)
and 3. The Maneuver Gaussian Mixture Prior (M-GMP) pre-
dicts scene-conditioned weights over motion-defined com-
ponents, and M3F generates base hypotheses by maneuver-
routed one-step transport. Once per planning step, the world
model predicts one candidate-independent future BEV field;
a learned trajectory-guided probe projects each hypothesis
into the current and predicted fields to form path-specific
Trajectory Evidence Memory. Metric Prism pre-scores can-
didates through learned PDM factors. The Evidence-Coupled
Frenet Refiner (ECFR) uses the same evidence to predict
local Frenet residuals for selected hypotheses. Refined hy-
potheses replace selected bases, re-query the shared field,
and compete with retained hypotheses under the same Met-
ric Prism scoring function.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• Scene-adaptive maneuver generation. We introduce a
maneuver-structured generator that combines M-GMP
with the maneuver-routed M3F to model motion-defined
maneuver components and generate scene-adaptive,
intent-conditioned trajectory hypotheses as explicit al-
ternatives in one step.
• Future-evidence verification and refinement. A learned
probe projects each candidate onto a shared future BEV
field to extract trajectory-specific evidence for PDM-
aligned scoring and ECFR’s local Frenet refinement. Met-
ric Prism jointly re-scores refined and retained hypotheses
for final selection.
• With a lightweight ResNet-34 TransFuser (TF) percep-
tion backbone, ManeuverFlow-TF achieves 90.58 PDMS
and 90.14 EPDMS on NAVSIM v1/v2 navtest, respec-
tively. Its camera-only Latent TransFuser (LTF) variant
further obtains 75.19 and 74.58 PDMS∗under replayed
and reactive traffic on WorldEngine, delivering the high-
est listed composite planning scores across all reported
benchmark and traffic settings.

Related Work
2.1
End-to-End and Generative Planning
End-to-end planners predict ego motion from sensor obser-
vations, ego states, and navigation commands. TransFuser
fuses camera and LiDAR features, while later systems in-
troduce planning-oriented queries and sparse scene repre-
sentations (Chitta et al. 2023; Hu et al. 2023; Jiang et al.
2023; Sun et al. 2025). Generative planners model multi-
ple feasible decisions through structured latent spaces, dif-
fusion, or flow matching: GenAD learns a trajectory latent
space, Diffusion Planner and DiffusionDrive denoise trajec-
tory distributions, and GoalFlow applies goal-conditioned
flow matching (Zheng et al. 2024, 2025a; Liao et al. 2025;
Xing et al. 2025). MeanFuser combines a Gaussian-mixture
source with one-step MeanFlow and aggregates sampled pro-
posals into a single ARM output (Geng et al. 2025; Wang
et al. 2026). MM-FM jointly designs a Gaussian-mixture
source and mode-dependent coupling for general multimodal
generation (Luo et al. 2026). A challenge is to organize
driving-specific motion modes and retain diverse hypotheses
as an explicit interface for evaluation and refinement.
2.2
World Models for Planning
Driving world models predict visual observations, occu-
pancy, or latent states to support planning. MILE jointly
learns latent dynamics and a driving policy, Think2Drive
trains a planner through interaction with a compact latent
world model, and LAW learns self-supervised latent dy-
namics (Hu et al. 2022a; Li et al. 2024a, 2025a). Drive-
WM, Drive-OccWorld, World4Drive, and WorldRFT use
predicted visual, occupancy, or latent futures for planning
evaluation and optimization (Wang et al. 2024; Yang et al.
2025; Zheng et al. 2025b; Yang et al. 2026). For online
evaluation, WoTE runs a recurrent BEV world model for
each anchor-refined candidate and selects the highest-scoring
trajectory (Li et al. 2025b). Candidate-conditioned rollouts
provide trajectory-specific futures but require separate com-
putation for every candidate. Shared predictions, such as the
future occupancy used by ST-P3, amortize forecasting across
candidates (Hu et al. 2022b) but do not directly provide

2.3
Trajectory Refinement and Selection
Candidate-based planning requires both reliable discrimi-
nation and local geometric improvement. VADv2 models
a distribution over a discrete planning vocabulary, while
Hydra-MDP distills human and rule-based teachers into
metric-specific trajectory heads (Jiang et al. 2026; Li et al.
2024b). DTPP learns context-dependent costs, Gen-Drive
learns a candidate evaluator, and DriveSuprim improves
hard-negative discrimination through coarse-to-fine filtering
and self-distillation (Huang et al. 2024, 2025; Yao et al.
2026). For trajectory refinement, ThinkTwice and ResWorld
use predicted future information to update an initial plan (Jia
et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2026). These lines of work sepa-
rately strengthen candidate evaluation and trajectory refine-
ment, but their coordination within a common hypothesis
pool remains less explored. Retaining unrefined alternatives
and directly comparing retained and refined trajectories un-
der the same future-conditioned evaluator thus remains an
open design problem.

4
Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setup
Datasets and evaluation protocols.
NAVSIM is a data-
driven planning benchmark built from OpenScene/nuPlan
driving logs (Karnchanachari et al. 2024) that evaluates pre-
dicted ego trajectories against logged future scenes under
a non-reactive simulator protocol (Dauner et al. 2024; Cao
et al. 2025). We follow the official split: navtrain for
training and navtest for evaluation. We estimate M-GMP
motion families and distribution statistics using only expert
trajectories from navtrain. NAVSIM v1 reports the Pre-
dictive Driver Model Score (PDMS) with NC, DAC, TTC,
EP, and Comfort, whereas v2 adopts the Extended Predic-
tive Driver Model Score (EPDMS) and additionally reports
DDC, TLC, LK, HC, and EC. We use the official evaluators;
for v2, we reuse the v1 checkpoints, five-factor heads, and
log-PDM rule—without v2-specific training or tuning.
We complement NAVSIM’s non-reactive evaluation with
the latest WorldEngine benchmark on all 288 publicly
released high-risk scenarios reconstructed from NAVSIM
navtest logs (Li et al. 2026). The logged replay (Log)
setting replays background traffic, whereas the Intelligent
Driver Model (IDM) setting updates surrounding agents re-
actively (Treiber, Hennecke, and Helbing 2000); in both set-
tings, the ego vehicle replans from updated rendered observa-
tions. We report episode success rate (SR), closed-loop ego
progress (EP), and PDMS∗: SR is the fraction of episodes
without collisions or drivable-area violations, EP measures
progress during closed-loop execution, and PDMS∗summa-
rizes overall closed-loop driving quality, together assessing
system-level replanning under replayed and reactive traffic.
Implementation details.
TF and LTF use a ResNet-34 im-
age backbone and predict Hτ = 8 waypoints over 4 s. We
conduct three independent training runs on four NVIDIA
RTX 4090 GPUs with global batch size 32 using AdamW
(weight decay 0.1). Stage one trains the perception net-
work and M3F at 4 × 10−4; stage two jointly optimizes all
inference-time modules at 2 × 10−4, with a 0.2× learning-
rate multiplier for the perception network and M3F. The fu-
ture field predicts HB = 4 BEV states at 1-s intervals; only
the stop-gradient target branch uses future observations dur-
ing training, while inference uses the current observation.
The defaults k = 3, N = 64, and R = N/4 were fixed
before navtest; all navtest sweeps are post-hoc sen-
sitivity analyses using frozen checkpoints. Each checkpoint
is evaluated five times, and the main tables report the mean
across the three independent run averages; the corresponding
sample standard deviations are provided in the supplemen-
tary material.

Maneuver-structured hypothesis generation.
Figure 5
shows M3F samples sharing a coherent motion tendency
within each routed M-GMP component while spanning dis-
tinct, road-aligned lane and intersection alternatives across
components. In these examples, the hypotheses remain visu-

component priors before trajectory transport.
5
Conclusion
We present ManeuverFlow, an explicit Hypothesize–Verify–
Improve planner that maintains maneuver-indexed hypothe-
ses throughout generation, evaluation, and refinement.
M-GMP allocates a fixed proposal budget across scene-
relevant motion components, and M3F transports their sam-
ples into scene-adaptive, maneuver-routed hypotheses in one
step. A shared predicted future BEV field provides path-
specific evidence for Metric Prism scoring and ECFR’s local
Frenet refinement. Refined and retained hypotheses re-query
the shared field and are jointly re-scored for final selection.
Complementary non-reactive and closed-loop evaluations on
NAVSIM and WorldEngine, respectively, demonstrate the
effectiveness of the integrated planner.

4.1
Input Construction
The LiDAR-assisted TransFuser (TF) and camera-only La-
tent TransFuser (LTF) use current left, front, and right views.
All are cropped by 28 pixels vertically; the lateral views are
also cropped by 416 pixels per horizontal side before con-
catenation and resizing to 256 × 1024. TF rasterizes one
LiDAR scan over [−32, 32]2 m at 0.25 m per pixel, re-
tains z > 0.2 m, and clips then divides pixel counts by
five. LTF replaces LiDAR with a learnable latent template
Zlat ∈R1×4×256×256, where the leading singleton dimen-
sion indexes one shared template, the four channels are latent
features, and the 256 × 256 grid spans the BEV. At runtime,
the template is broadcast along the batch dimension, yielding
the actual encoder input shape [B, 4, 256, 256]. Accordingly,
the LTF ResNet stem uses a convolution-weight tensor of
shape [64, 4, 7, 7]. Both receive a 4-D command code, 2-D
velocity, and 2-D acceleration.

4.4
Deployment Lifecycle
At each planning step, the final model generates 64 base can-
didates using Top-3 largest-remainder allocation and predicts
one shared future BEV field from the current observation.
Metric Prism first ranks the bases using their trajectory ev-
idence. ECFR refines 16 selected bases and replaces their
slots, retaining the other 48. All 64 final hypotheses re-query
the same BEV field and are jointly re-scored by Metric Prism.
The candidate with the highest predicted log-PDM score is
returned as the final plan.

5
Statistical Reliability and Scoring
Diagnostics
5.1
Main-Result Variation
Across three independently trained checkpoints, the largest
observed standard deviation is 0.14 on NAVSIM and 0.92
in WorldEngine. These values describe run-to-run disper-
sion under the stated protocol; with three training runs, they
should not be interpreted as formal confidence intervals. Ta-
ble S3 provides the corresponding dispersion for every Ma-
neuverFlow row in the three main-result tables. It does not
provide matched-seed uncertainty for the component abla-
tions in the main paper.
5.2
Within-Pool Top-κ Retrieval of Metric Prism
We evaluate Metric Prism’s preliminary ordering on the same
12,146 NAVSIM v1 navtest scene tokens used by the
frozen-pool analysis. For every trajectory in the unrefined
Base64 pool, Metric Prism predicts the five PDM factors
and applies Eq. (S24). Here Base64 is shorthand for the un-
refined 64-candidate pool used by this retrieval diagnostic;
all percentages are relative to its own evaluator oracle. The
main paper’s k denotes the number of routed components,
whereas κ here denotes ranking depth. The diagnostic asks
whether the first κ positions contain the evaluator-best tra-
jectory in that particular pool, either exactly or within one
PDMS point of its oracle score.
As Table S4 shows, at rank one, Metric Prism retrieves a
trajectory within one PDMS point of the Base64 oracle in
75.28% of scenes and misses this tolerance in 24.72%; by
rank ten, coverage reaches 83.18%. These are pool-relative
retrieval statistics, not a budget-matched cross-method scorer
comparison. This diagnostic characterizes the learned scorer
on the evaluated unrefined Base64 pool; it does not isolate the
future branch or compare Metric Prism with another scorer.
5.3
Fixed Selector Transfer to NAVSIM v2
Table S3(b) evaluates the complete v1-trained checkpoints
under NAVSIM v2 while retaining the same five factor heads
and log-PDM selector. The v2-only factors contribute to
EPDMS evaluation but are not added to the selector. The
resulting EPDMS therefore provides an empirical feasibility
check for transferring the fixed selector to the extended eval-
uator, rather than a claim that v1 PDMS and v2 EPDMS are
directly comparable across evaluator versions.

6.3
Interpretation and Evidence Scope
Interpretation scope.
The paper operationalizes each in-
tention as a trajectory-derived motion hypothesis whose dis-
placement, turning, or speed-trend tendency directly affects
planning behavior; it does not claim a separate map-semantic
annotation task. The quantitative claims concern scene-
conditioned component support and downstream planning
gains, while the prior views and controlled expert sweeps
expose source organization and routed response separation.
Top-three routing may still miss rare or compositional behav-
ior, and the qualitative examples and learned PDM factors
establish neither cross-method superiority nor safety. The
candidate-independent future field also does not model in-
teractive responses to alternative ego plans.
Experimental scope.
The cumulative ablations evalu-
ate the Hypothesize–Verify–Improve stages. The Dense-XL
control matches total generator parameter count, and Pro-
posal FPS measures perception through M3F. Table S5 re-
ports post-hoc candidate-quality headroom within fixed anal-
ysis draws. Table S3 reports sample standard deviations
across independently trained checkpoints under the stated
training and evaluation protocol.
Transfer scope.
NAVSIM v2 applies the v1-trained check-
points, five-factor heads, and selector without v2-specific
training or tuning, while WorldEngine reconstructs driv-
ing scenarios from navtest logs. These protocols test
fixed-selector transfer and closed-loop replanning, not
independent-domain or real-vehicle generalization.
