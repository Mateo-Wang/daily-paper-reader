Directional Pullback Regularization for Long-Horizon Rollout of Latent
World Models in Autonomous Driving
Shatadal Mishra, Ahmadreza Moradipari, Nejib Ammar
Abstract— Learned world models for autonomous driving
operate in unconstrained latent spaces, yet the physical sys-
tems they represent are governed by strict non-holonomic
constraints: ground vehicles cannot realize instantaneous pure
lateral motion. We study long-horizon latent rollout modeling
for autonomous driving and ask whether rollout quality can
be improved by assigning a motion-aware geometry to latent
transitions, rather than by redesigning the predictor architec-
ture. Starting from an unconstrained latent rollout baseline, we
introduce Directional Pullback Regularization (DPR), a training-
time method that defines a kinematics-aware anisotropic Rie-
mannian metric in decoded state space and pulls it back through
the decoder Jacobian to the latent manifold. The resulting
latent-space metric assigns higher cost to latent directions that
decode to lateral vehicle motion, while preserving the original
encoder–dynamics–decoder backbone. This yields a clean three-
way comparison among an Unconstrained Latent Rollout model
(ULR), an Euclidean Pullback Regularization ablation (EPR),
and the full directional method (DPR). We evaluate all models
on open-loop ego-motion rollout over a common 10-second
test horizon, using training rollout lengths of 1, 3, 5, and 10
steps. Across all four training regimes, DPR achieves lower 10-
second final displacement error than both ULR and EPR. The
strongest relative accuracy gain appears for models trained
with 3-step rollout supervision, where DPR reduces the 10-
second final displacement error by approximately 44% relative
to ULR. The strongest overall control-relevant tradeoff is
obtained for longer rollout-trained models, especially at 10
steps, where DPR simultaneously improves displacement error,
lateral-motion consistency, and forward-motion preservation
relative to both ULR and EPR.
I. INTRODUCTION
Ground vehicles obey non-holonomic kinematic con-
straints: instantaneous motion must align with the vehicle
heading, so a car cannot move by pure sideways slip. Stan-
dard latent world models impose no corresponding geometric
constraint on decoded state increments, creating a mismatch
in which latent transitions can decode to kinematically in-
consistent motion. Over long roll-out horizons, this mismatch
can accumulate even when the loss of one-step prediction is
small.
We ask whether this mismatch can be corrected without
changing the predictor architecture. To that end, we introduce
Directional Pullback Regularization (DPR), which defines a
kinematics-aware anisotropic Riemannian metric in decoded
state space and pulls it back through the decoder Jacobian
to latent space. In the spirit of prior work on decoder-
induced Riemannian geometry and pullback metrics for

Building on a mature lineage of latent world models
[5]–[11], the dominant trend in driving world models has
been toward richer architectures, larger generative stacks,
and more specialized scene representations. Autonomous
driving is a particularly compelling setting for world models
because safe decision-making depends on anticipating the
consequences of ego motion in complex, interactive, and
partially observed scenes. Recent surveys emphasize that
world models are becoming a central tool for simulation,
forecasting, and planning in driving [13]. Representative
examples include TrafficBots for data-driven traffic sim-
ulation and motion prediction [14], GAIA-1 and GAIA-
2 for controllable generative driving world models [16],
[17], Copilot4D for tokenized discrete-diffusion world mod-
eling [18], BEVWorld for multimodal bird’s-eye-view latent
simulation [19], and Drive-OccWorld for action-conditioned
four-dimensional occupancy forecasting and planning [20].
Collectively, these works show that learned latent represen-
tations can support increasingly realistic and controllable
autonomous-driving world models. However, they rely on
heavyweight video-generation pipelines, specialized scene
representations, or auxiliary components that make it difficult
to isolate a simple geometric mechanism for improving ego-
motion rollout. As a result, they do not directly answer a
narrower control-oriented question: can long-horizon latent
rollout be improved by shaping the local geometry of latent
transitions themselves?
This question is especially important because long-horizon
latent rollout remains fragile. A model may reduce one-step
prediction loss while still drifting toward motion patterns that
are difficult to reconcile with the local geometry of feasible
vehicle motion [12]. In driving, this issue is particularly acute
because local motion is anisotropic: forward translation,
heading evolution, and speed change are natural directions of
motion, whereas pure lateral displacement is kinematically
forbidden by the non-holonomic constraint. Recent efforts
have therefore begun to inject stronger physical or kinematic
structure into learned world models. Examples include physi-
cally informed driving world models, physically interpretable
world models, and kinematics-aware latent world models
for autonomous driving [21]–[23]. These works reinforce
an important point: unconstrained latent prediction is often
insufficient in safety-critical dynamical domains.

However, existing physically grounded approaches often
introduce structure through auxiliary modules, architectural
changes, additional supervision, or specialized representa-
tions [21]–[24]. In contrast, this paper asks a more tar-
geted question: can one improve a latent rollout model by
regularizing the decoded tangent geometry induced by the
latent transition itself, without redesigning the predictor?
This question is attractive from a control perspective because
the local rollout tangent directly determines how a latent
model moves through decoded state space and how error
accumulates during open-loop prediction, which is central to
planning and model- based control. If that tangent is geomet-
rically misaligned with feasible motion, long-horizon rollout
can degrade even when one-step fitting appears acceptable.
The proposed framework yields a clean three-way decom-
position. The first model is Unconstrained Latent Rollout
(ULR), which serves as the baseline trained only with predic-
tion loss. The second is Euclidean Pullback Regularization
(EPR), obtained by removing directional preference from
the pullback metric; this ablation penalizes overall decoded
tangent magnitude but does not distinguish between feasible
and lateral motion. The third is the full Directional Pullback
Regularization (DPR) model, which adds explicit directional
cost to the lateral decoded component. This decomposition
separates three distinct questions: whether pullback reg-
ularization improves over unconstrained training, whether
any gain can be explained by generic smoothing alone,
and whether an explicitly directional geometric penalty is
necessary.
Our central hypothesis is that a pullback metric aligned
with vehicle-motion geometry can improve long-horizon
rollout quality more effectively than either unconstrained
training or Euclidean pullback regularization alone. To test
this hypothesis, we evaluate ULR, EPR, and DPR using
long-horizon open-loop ego-motion rollout metrics that are
directly relevant to control-oriented world modeling, in-
cluding displacement error, lateral-motion error, curvature
consistency, and preservation of forward motion.
The contributions of this work are threefold:
• To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work
to study directional pullback regularization for long-
horizon latent ego-motion rollout in autonomous driv-
ing, using an explicit projector-based motion metric that
penalizes decoded lateral motion while preserving the
original encoder–dynamics–decoder backbone.
• We provide a clean experimental decomposition among
the unconstrained baseline (Unconstrained Latent Roll-
out, ULR), the Euclidean pullback ablation (Euclidean
Pullback Regularization, EPR), and the full directional
method (Directional Pullback Regularization, DPR).

• ULR: unconstrained latent rollout, with prediction loss

• EPR: Euclidean pullback regularization, corresponding

• DPR: directional pullback regularization, corresponding

This decomposition is important because it allows us to
distinguish gains due to generic smoothing from gains
specifically for imposing a directional geometric penalty on
decoded lateral motion.
E. Interpretation
The proposed method is a lightweight geometric bias
on latent rollout rather than a hard kinematic model. The
encoder, latent dynamics, decoder, and rollout mechanism
remain unchanged. What changes is the geometry used
to score latent transitions during training. ULR learns by
prediction loss alone. EPR penalizes the overall size of the
decoded tangent. DPR goes further by shaping the latent
manifold with a pullback metric derived from a motion-aware
decoded-space metric, thereby making latent directions that
decode to lateral motion more expensive. In this sense, DPR
encourages motion-consistent rollouts by acting directly on
the geometry of latent transitions.

III. EXPERIMENTS
A. Experimental Setup
We evaluate the proposed pullback-regularized model on
long-horizon open-loop ego-motion rollout prediction. The
physical state is xt = [px, py, θ, v]⊤, where (px, py) is
planar position, θ is heading, and v is speed. The model
input consists of the previous K = 4 state increments, the
current state, and the local road curvature. All models use
the same encoder–dynamics–decoder backbone and the same
latent dimension dz = 64. The backbone uses a lightweight
feedforward architecture consisting of a two-layer MLP
encoder (K·4+4+1 →256 →dz), a two-layer MLP latent
dynamics model (dz+1 →256 →dz), and a two-layer MLP
decoder (dz →128 →4), with dz = 64. The only difference
between models is the regularization applied during training.
We intentionally use a lightweight shared backbone to isolate
the effect of the proposed geometric regularizer; evaluating
DPR with stronger sequence architectures is an important
direction for future work.
All experiments are conducted on ego-motion trajectories
derived from the NVIDIA open-source driving dataset [25].
We use the released egomotion streams and construct trajec-
tory windows from the physical state and its corresponding
increment
∆xt = [∆px, ∆py, ∆θ, ∆v]⊤.
The raw egomotion timestamps are irregular, so all sequences
are resampled to a uniform rate of 10 Hz before preprocess-
ing. We then extract fixed-length windows of 20 seconds,
yielding 200 steps per trajectory window, and compute the
local road curvature at each step as an additional model input.
We use the standard training /validation / test split provided
with the data set, consisting of 126,839 training clips, 65,971
validation clips, and 35,175 test clips. All quantitative results
reported in this paper are computed on test windows that are
disjoint from the training and validation data. We compare
three model variants:
1) ULR (Unconstrained Latent Rollout): the baseline
model trained only with prediction loss;
2) EPR

proposed method, in which β > 0 assigns additional
cost to the lateral component of the decoded tangent.
We train separate models with rollout training lengths U ∈
{1, 3, 5, 10}. We chose U ∈{1, 3, 5, 10} to span short,
intermediate, and long self-fed rollout regimes while keeping
the training sweep tractable. Here, U denotes the number
of self-fed training steps used during optimization, not the
evaluation horizon. At test time, all models are rolled forward
in open loop for 100 steps, corresponding to a 10-second
horizon with ∆t = 0.1 s. All test results are reported on the
same set of 2000 held-out windows.

B. Main Quantitative Results
Table I summarizes the 10-second open-loop rollout re-
sults. Among the regimes tested, the largest relative reduction
in 10-second final displacement error appears for the 3-step
rollout-trained model. At the same time, the strongest overall
control-relevant tradeoff is obtained for longer rollout-trained
models, especially at U = 10, where DPR combines lower
displacement error with improved lateral consistency and
forward-motion preservation. The main conclusion is that
DPR improves over both baselines across all four training
rollout lengths. In particular, for every training rollout length
U, the selected DPR model achieves the lowest 10-second
final displacement error among the three compared models.
Figure 2 shows the full average displacement error tra-
jectories over the evaluation horizon for ULR, EPR, and
DPR across all four training rollout lengths. The one-step
training regime (U = 1) already shows a large gain in long-
horizon rollout error. Relative to ULR, DPR reduces the 10-
second mean displacement error from 31.366 m to 19.254
m and the final displacement error from 80.646 m to 47.172
m. It also reduces the 95th percentile ground-truth-frame

gain comes with a clear forward-motion tradeoff: the median

m/s in DPR. Thus, for one-step training, directional pullback
regularization substantially improves geometric consistency
and final error, but also makes the long-horizon rollout more
conservative.
The three-step training regime (U = 3) provides a stronger
and more balanced result. DPR with β = 50 reduces the 10-
second mean displacement error from 33.913 m to 17.057
m and the final displacement error from 84.163 m to 47.160
m. It also reduces the 95th percentile lateral error from
32.377 m/s to 13.748 m/s and the curvature-consistency error
from 1.868 rad/s to 0.902 rad/s. Unlike the one-step model,
the three-step DPR model also improves median forward
velocity relative to ULR, from 1.712 m/s to 3.115 m/s,
while increasing the fraction of rollout steps with meaningful
forward motion from 0.530 to 0.599. The five-step and ten-
step training regimes yield the strongest overall tradeoffs.
For U = 5, DPR with β = 3 improves the 10-second
mean displacement error from 7.022 m to 6.894 m and the
final displacement error from 22.470 m to 19.399 m. More
importantly, it dramatically reduces the 95th percentile lateral
error from 12.096 m/s to 5.593 m/s while also improving
forward behavior, increasing the median forward velocity
from 6.554 m/s to 8.185 m/s and the forward-valid fraction
from 0.666 to 0.980. For U = 10, DPR with β = 5 gives
the strongest overall result among all models tested: the 10-
second mean displacement error decreases from 6.839 m to
5.962 m, the final displacement error decreases from 22.980
m to 17.322 m, the lateral error drops from 30.114 m/s to
4.971 m/s, and the median forward velocity increases from
7.268 m/s to 9.189 m/s. Figure 3 summarizes the key 10-
second trends across training rollout lengths, highlighting the
tradeoff among final displacement error, lateral consistency,
and preservation of forward motion.
Overall, these results show that the pullback metric is
effective not only in reducing displacement error, but also
in shaping the rollout toward lower lateral drift and better
kinematic consistency. The most favorable balance between
geometric correction and preservation of forward motion is
obtained for the longer self-fed training rollouts U = 5 and


U = 10.
C. Ablation: Euclidean Versus Directional Pullback Regu-
larization
A central question is whether the observed gain is due
merely to adding any regularization term, or whether the
directional lateral penalty is the crucial ingredient. The
comparison between EPR and DPR answers this question
directly.
EPR already changes the behavior of the latent rollout
model because it penalizes the overall magnitude of the
decoded tangent. In some cases this is beneficial. For ex-
ample, when the training rollout length is 10, EPR reduces
the final displacement error from 22.980 m in ULR to
18.063 m. However, Euclidean pullback regularization alone
is not sufficient. It does not consistently produce the best
long-horizon rollout and can even hurt performance. For
training rollout length 5, EPR increases the 10-second final
displacement error from 22.470 m to 33.475 m, even though
DPR reduces it to 19.399 m.
The directional term consistently improves on the Eu-
clidean ablation. For training rollout length 1, the final
displacement error decreases from 50.927 m with EPR to
47.172 m with DPR, and the 95th percentile lateral error
decreases from 13.689 m/s to 9.395 m/s. For training rollout
length 3, the final displacement error decreases from 65.300
m to 47.160 m, and the lateral error decreases from 29.309
m/s to 13.748 m/s. Notably, for U = 3, EPR yields a negative
median forward velocity (−0.623 m/s), indicating that back-
ward motion occurs in more than half of the evaluated rollout
steps; this failure mode reinforces that isotropic shrinkage
alone can distort motion realism and that directional structure
is necessary. For training rollout length 5, DPR reverses the
failure mode of EPR and improves both displacement error
and forward behavior. For training rollout length 10, DPR
again improves over EPR, reducing final displacement error
from 18.063 m to 17.322 m and increasing median forward
velocity from 5.546 m/s to 9.189 m/s.
This ablation is important because it shows that the gains
do not come from generic damping alone. The extra penalty
on the lateral decoded tangent is the component that makes
the regularizer effective. In other words, the model benefits
not just from shrinking decoded motion, but from shrinking
it in a geometrically meaningful direction.
D. Effect of Training Rollout Length
The duration of the training rollout changes how the
pullback regularizer interacts with long-horizon prediction.
One-step training can already produce large reductions in
long-horizon displacement error, but the selected DPR model
becomes overly conservative in forward motion. As the
training rollout length increases, the selected pullback model
becomes better at balancing displacement accuracy, lateral
consistency, and preservation of forward motion.
This trend is especially clear when comparing the selected
DPR models across U = 1, 3, 5, 10. The one-step model
achieves a large reduction in final displacement error but
nearly stalls by the end of the rollout. The three-step model
offers a better balance, reducing both long-horizon error and
lateral drift while preserving more forward motion. The five-
step and ten-step models are stronger still: they improve
long-horizon position error, reduce lateral drift substantially,
and maintain high fractions of forward-valid rollout steps.
Among the models tested, the ten-step DPR model provides
the strongest overall 10-second result.
These observations suggest that pullback regularization is
most effective when the model is trained under sufficiently
long self-fed rollout, so that the geometric penalty acts on
the same type of error accumulation that appears at test time.
Short rollout training can still benefit from the regularizer,
but is more prone to the tradeoff between reduced lateral
drift and reduced forward aggressiveness.
E. Summary of Experimental Findings
The experiments lead to three main findings. First, DPR
improves long-horizon rollout over the unconstrained latent
baseline ULR. Second, the gain is not explained by Euclidean
pullback regularization alone; the directional lateral penalty
is necessary for the strongest results. Third, the quality of
the final tradeoff depends on the training rollout length:
short-rollout training can over-regularize motion, whereas
longer self-fed rollout training allows the proposed method
to improve displacement accuracy, geometric consistency,
and preservation of forward motion simultaneously. Among
the regimes tested, U = 3 shows the strongest relative
improvement in 10-second final displacement error over
ULR, while U = 10 provides the strongest overall trade-
off among displacement accuracy, lateral consistency, and
forward-motion preservation.
IV. LIMITATIONS
Several aspects of this study remain open. First, the di-
rectional weight β is selected by validation and its preferred
value varies with the training rollout length, indicating that
the strength of the geometric bias interacts with the amount
of self-fed rollout. Second, stronger lateral-motion control
can trade off against preservation of forward motion, partic-
ularly for shorter rollout-trained models. Third, the pullback
metric acts locally on decoded tangents and does not by
itself guarantee global long-horizon accuracy or closed-loop
stability. Finally, the experiments are limited to ego-motion
rollout; multi-agent interaction, action-conditioned planning,
and closed-loop evaluation are left for future work.
V. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
This paper introduced Directional Pullback Regulariza-
tion (DPR) for long-horizon latent ego-motion rollout in
autonomous driving. By defining an anisotropic metric in
decoded state space and pulling it back through the decoder
to the latent space, the method assigns a motion-aware ge-
ometry to latent transitions without changing the underlying
encoder–dynamics–decoder backbone. The resulting exper-
imental comparison among Unconstrained Latent Rollout

extend the present formulation beyond ego-motion rollout to
