# A Survey on End-to-End Autonomous Driving Training from the Perspectives of Data, Strategy, and Platform 

Chengkai Xu , Yiming Cui , Jiaqi Liu , Yicheng Guo , Cheng Qin ,Geyuan Zhang, Xinwei Dong, Shiyu Fang , Peng Hang , Senior Member, IEEE, and Jian Sun 

Abstract —Autonomous driving is a cornerstone technology for the future of intelligent transportation, where end-to-end learning has emerged as a transformative paradigm that directly maps multimodal sensory inputs to driving actions through unified differentiable models. While offering advantages, the effectiveness of end-to-end autonomous driving (E2E-AD) is ultimately determined by the quality of its training ecosys-tem. This paper provides a comprehensive review of training methods and ecosystem for E2E-AD. We introduce a Data-Strategy-Platform taxonomy that conceptualizes training as an interdependent system. The data layer defines what can be learned, the strategy layer governs how learning aligns with driving objectives, and the platform layer supports scalability and continuous evolution. Within this framework, we survey recent advances across data-centric pipelines, learning paradigms, and training infrastructures, and analyze their interplay in shaping model performance, robustness, and deployability. Finally, we reflect on current limitations and articulate a forward-looking vision that emphasizes a shift from data quantity to data value, from isolated optimization to foundation-driven generalization, and from static training to integrated training-testing loops, aiming toward robust, scalable, and trustworthy autonomous driving systems. We maintain a continuously updated repository tracking cutting-edge literature and works at Our Project Page. 

Index Terms —Autonomous Vehicle; End-to-End; Artificial In-telligence; Intelligent Transportation System

For example, frameworks such as LMDrive and OpenDriveVLA align vi-sion, language, and action in a shared semantic space, thereby facilitating the natural integration of high-level instructions [24], [25]. Meanwhile, world models such as DriveDreamer and GAIA generate scene rollouts that capture rare or cross-domain conditions [26]–[29]. These advances mark a transition from narrowly engineered control policies toward models with enhanced capacities for reasoning, generalization, and even imagination. Alongside architectural evolution, research trends reveal deeper transformations in how E2E-AD is conceptualized. The data paradigm is moving from scale expansion toward scenario prioritization, with increasing attention to rare, high-risk events and generative supplementation [10], [30], [31]. Training objectives are shifting from perception accuracy to consistency with planning and safety, motivating the explo-ration of imitation learning, reinforcement learning, and gener-ative models [32]–[34]. Evaluation protocols are evolving from offline metrics to closed-loop benchmarks such as CARLA and nuPlan, which emphasize safety, comfort, and compliance under continuous deployment cycles [35]–[38]. These trends illustrate that the progress of E2E-AD is not merely an archi-tectural evolution but a systematic reconfiguration of training logic.

B. Supervision and Alignment of Labels 

Supervision transforms raw data into learnable signals, enabling E2E-AD systems to distinguish between behaviors that should be learned and those that should be avoided. As raw sensor data lacks semantic or behavioral grounding, it is annotation that serves as the mechanism for assigning value and meaning to each scenario within the operational design domain (ODD) [10]. Therefore, the goal of supervision is not simply to label data exhaustively, but to produce training signals that are consistent with scenario value and aligned with the system’s planning and safety objectives. Manual annotation provides high-fidelity supervision by capturing precise object semantics, trajectories, and interaction cues [83], [84]. However, it is inherently costly and difficult to scale to the volume required by E2E-AD. Automatic annotation based on pre-trained models, cross-modal fusion, or pseudo-labeling [85]–[88] offers scalability but introduces risks of misalignment, temporal drift, and noise [7], [10]. In practice, an effective annotation strategy should combine manual annotation for high-value scenarios that are safety-critical or rare and automated or semi-supervised approaches for low-value, high-frequency scenarios where approximate supervision is sufficient. However, not all labeled data contribute equally to strategy improvement. Unlike previous approaches that equated long-tail scenarios with high-value scenarios, this paper argues that scenarios with higher value can bring greater perfor-mance gains, especially those that expose decision ambiguities, complex interactions, or boundary conditions of the driving policy, as depicted in Fig. 3. C. Data Valuation and Governance 

As the control system of the data layer, data valuation and governance aim to allocate resources toward the most informative and safety-relevant scenarios, thereby accelerating model convergence and improving overall training efficiency [89]–[91]. While early industrial practices often pursued data accumulation through sheer volume [77], the complexity and long-tail characteristics of real-world driving environments suggest that massive low-value samples yield rapidly dimin-ishing marginal returns. Consequently, the paradigm must shift from scale expansion to value density, where the training benefit is determined by the quality of scenario coverage rather than total sample count. To implement this shift, a quantifiable scenario value system is essential. It summarizes recurring ideas in scenario selec-tion, uncertainty-based mining, and data governance into a simple value-scoring view. We define the value of a scenario 

V (s) not as a static property, but as its marginal contribution to the model’s current capability boundary [92]. In practice, this can be operationalized through a composite scoring function that integrates risk, uncertainty, and rarity: 

V (s) = wr · R(s) + wu · U (s|θ) + wd · D(s|D train ) (1) where R(s) quantifies safety-criticality based on physical metrics, U (s|θ) represents the model’s epistemic uncertainty regarding the scenario and D(s|D train ) Measures the dis-tributional distance of scenario s from the existing training distribution Dtrain .Critically, scenario value is dynamic; as the model θ evolves, previously difficult scenarios ( U (s|θ) ↓) lose their training utility, while new edge cases gain importance [62], [93]. Effective governance requires a data engine that continuously re-evaluates samples. This involves establishing version trees that map specific data subsets to model checkpoints, ensuring that every performance improvement or degradation can be traced back to the addition or removal of specific high-value scenarios [94], [95]. By rigorously managing data lineage and prioritizing resources based on the V (s) metric, the training ecosystem transforms from a passive repository into an active optimization engine, ensuring that computational resources are concentrated where they yield the greatest robustness benefits. IV. S TRATEGY LAYER 

The E2E-AD strategy layer maps multimodal perception to control actions, governing closed-loop stability in mixed traf-fic. To transcend the efficiency-robustness trade-offs inherent in Imitation Learning (IL) and Reinforcement Learning (RL), the field is pivoting toward generative paradigms. Approaches such as diffusion policies, multimodal language models, and world models introduce advanced reasoning and adaptability capabilities. As shown in Table III, these paradigms possess distinct, complementary advantages. 

A. Imitation Learning 

IL trains an E2E-AD policy directly from expert demon-strations, avoiding explicit reward engineering and yielding strong data efficiency at initialization [96]. Canonical instances include behavior cloning (BC) [97]–[99] and distribution-matching variants such as inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) [100], [101] and generative adversarial imitation learning (GAIL) [102], [103], which collectively demonstrate that policy-level knowledge can be distilled from human drivers. Despite their practicality, IL methods face three structural limitations in autonomous driving. First, they exhibit a high dependence on expert data, where demonstrations are costly to collect, often noisy, and may contain suboptimal behaviors [6], [104]. Second, they suffer from covariate shift, as models trained on expert trajectories may encounter unseen states at deployment [105], [106], leading to compounding errors and potential loss of control. Third, real-world driving data follow a long-tail distribution, where critical but rare events are under-represented, leaving IL-trained policies fragile under safety-critical conditions [107], [108]. These challenges constrain the reliability and generalization of IL in real deployment. To address these limitations, substantial effort has been devoted to optimizing IL training. On the data side, augmen-tation strategies such as viewpoint transformation, trajectory perturbation, and synthetic counterfactual construction enrich the training distribution and mitigate overfitting to narrow ex-pert trajectories [109]–[113]. On the algorithmic side, dataset aggregation (DAgger) introduces iterative corrections by al-lowing the agent to query experts in newly visited states [114], while SafeDAgger further reduces expert intervention costs by activating corrective supervision only in high-risk conditions [115]–[117]. Adversarial extensions, such as GAIL and its robust variants, improve generalization by aligning trajectory distributions rather than replicating individual actions [103], [118]. More recently, IL has been integrated with generative models. B. Reinforcement Learning 

RL optimizes a driving policy through interaction, im-proving long-horizon returns under an environment-defined feedback signal (MDP/POMDP) [121]–[123]. In E2E-AD, RL’s distinctive value reflects on refining a policy toward long-horizon objectives (safety margins, comfort, compliance) that are difficult to fully encode in supervised labels rather than learning to drive from scratch [18], [124]. However, the direct application of RL to E2E-AD encoun-ters four systemic challenges. First, sample inefficiency re-mains a central bottleneck, as convergent policies often require millions of interactions, while real-world data collection is costly and unsafe [90], [125], [126]. Second, safe exploration is particularly problematic in driving, since unconstrained trial-and-error may lead to collisions, boundary violations, or traf-fic infractions, which are unacceptable in deployment [127], [128]. Third, reward misalignment arises because real driving objectives are multi-dimensional which covers safety, comfort, efficiency, compliance and so on, but are often compressed into scalar reward signals, producing biased optimization and divergence between offline metrics and online safety [129], [130]. Finally, cross-domain generalization is difficult because policies trained in one environment may degrade severely when transferred across cities, weather, or traffic cultures, while sim-to-real gaps further amplify the distribution shift [131], [132]. To mitigate these issues, diverse optimization strategies have been proposed. For sample efficiency, offline RL leverages large-scale replay datasets to learn policies without costly online exploration, often regularized to prevent extrapolation errors on out-of-distribution actions [133]. Hybrid approaches combine imitation learning with reinforcement learning, where demonstrations provide fast initialization and RL subsequently fine-tunes policies through interaction [134]–[136]. World models further enhance efficiency by enabling imagination-based rollouts in latent space, thereby generating synthetic trajectories that extend coverage of rare or dangerous scenarios [137]–[140]. Regarding safety, constrained RL incorporates explicit constraints such as collision avoidance and lane keep-ing into the optimization process, while risk-sensitive meth-ods based on Conditional Value at Risk prioritize robustness under tail events [141]–[144]. Shielding techniques, such as control barrier functions or model predictive control filters, can override unsafe actions during execution, reducing risk without halting policy improvement [145]–[147]. For reward alignment, preference-based methods such as RLHF align training signals with human judgments of safe and natural driving behaviors, while hierarchical RL decomposes tasks into high-level decision planning and low-level control [90], [148]–[150], improving interpretability and multi-objective balance. Finally, generalization is strengthened by domain randomization and style perturbation in simulation, continual learning to preserve knowledge across deployments, and cur-riculum learning strategies that progressively expose policies to increasingly complex scenarios [151]–[153]. As a result, RL is best positioned as a policy alignment and 

refinement engine in the training ecosystem for its ability to enforce long-horizon objectives beyond labels. Therefore, RL is often used in conjunction with world models or to fine-tune other paradigms such as IL and LLM. If used directly for E2E-AD, RL requires high-fidelity evaluation environments and guardrails that bound exploration and reward misspecification.

1) Diffusion Policies : Diffusion-based policies represent a generative paradigm for end-to-end autonomous driving, mod-eling multimodal action distributions via a denoising process that refines random noise into structured trajectories or control sequences [154], [155]. Unlike deterministic mappings in clas-sical policy learning, diffusion policies inherently capture the stochasticity of driving behavior, allowing policies to represent multiple feasible actions under uncertainty [156]–[158]. This property makes them particularly suitable for highly interactive mixed traffic, where safe and robust behavior often depends on anticipating diverse outcomes rather than committing to a single trajectory. The strengths of diffusion policies lie in their robustness and expressivity. By modeling the full action distribution rather than a single deterministic path, these methods can naturally account for uncertainty, generate diverse yet plausible driving maneuvers, and avoid mode collapse in decision-making [159], [160]. Such diversity provides resilience in ambiguous or multi-agent interactions, where multiple safe behaviors may coexist. Moreover, the probabilistic nature of diffusion models facilitates uncertainty-aware planning, offering a principled mechanism to balance risk and exploration in safety-critical driving scenarios. Despite these advantages, diffusion policies also face sev-eral critical challenges that recent research is working to address. The iterative denoising procedure is computationally expensive, elongating training and increasing resource demand [161]. Correspondingly, efficiency-oriented diffusion variants aim to reduce sampling steps and amortize generation costs. Real-time deployment is further challenged by multi-step sampling latency [155], motivating guided or controlled gen-eration that concentrates probability mass on safety-compliant regions and reduces wasted sampling [162]–[164]. Finally, while diffusion is powerful for proposing trajectories, align-ing generated outputs with planning objectives and vehicle dynamics is non-trivial, raising concerns about interpretability and integration with safety-critical controllers [165]–[167]. Scenario-conditioned diffusion and planner-oriented adapta-tions address this gap by injecting structured context and optimizing generations to satisfy kinematic constraints and downstream safety metrics [168]–[172]. In the training ecosystem, diffusion policies are best po-sitioned as a multimodal proposal and planning component 

rather than a standalone E2E controller. They are most ap-plicable when interaction uncertainty is high and multiple safe futures must be explicitly represented, but they remain constrained by sampling latency, controllability under hard constraints, and the difficulty of certifying safety from agenerative distribution. In practice, these properties make diffusion particularly well-suited to the role of a trajectory-level planner that converts high-level decisions into diverse and feasible candidates. Moreover, diffusion policies are often most effective in hybrid training pipelines, serving to preserve multimodality and uncertainty awareness in other paradigms. 

2) Multimodal Large Language Models : Multimodal large language models (MLLMs), including vision-language-model (VLM) and vision–language–action (VLA) systems, represent an emerging direction for training E2E-AD policies, whose underlying principle is to leverage the grounding capabilities of large-scale language and vision–language models to connect raw perception inputs with natural language instructions and low-level vehicle actions [15], [16], [173]. By aligning sensory observations with symbolic or linguistic abstractions, these models aim to bridge high-level reasoning with control ex-ecution, thereby enabling autonomous vehicles to understand and respond not only to the physical environment but also to human commands expressed in natural language. The key strengths of MLLMs lie in their generalizability and interpretability [174]–[176]. Owing to large-scale pretraining across diverse multimodal corpora, these models exhibit strong transferability to new scenarios with minimal task-specific data, a property especially valuable for cross-domain driving deployment [25], [177], [178]. Furthermore, their ability to process natural language enables command reasoning, which allows human operators to guide vehicle behavior intuitively. The integration of linguistic representations also provides a degree of transparency and interpretability absent in purely black-box policies, while their modular pretraining structure 8

supports flexible adaptation to varied downstream driving tasks [179], [180]. Nonetheless, significant challenges constrain their practical deployment in autonomous driving. First, maintaining tem-poral consistency is difficult, as LLM-driven policies must integrate sequential sensor data and reason over long hori-zons, whereas most pretrained models are optimized for static image–text pairs [168], [181]. Second, low-level grounding remains limited, as translating high-level linguistic reasoning into precise, real-time control commands requires robust align-ment across abstraction layers [182], [183]. Third, safety and reliability are unresolved concerns, since language models may hallucinate, misinterpret ambiguous instructions, or generate unsafe control suggestions [184]–[186]. Finally, issues of real-time performance pose engineering hurdles, as the inference latency of large multimodal architectures is often incompatible with the stringent time constraints of closed-loop driving [187]–[190]. To mitigate these limitations, researchers have proposed specialized training methodologies. Supervised pretraining on paired perception–language–action datasets improves ground-ing between natural language and driving trajectories [75], [85], [191], [192]. Instruction tuning further enhances the abil-ity to follow human commands in structured traffic contexts [33], [193], [194]. Beyond supervised alignment, RLHF and its variants provide preference-driven optimization, allowing policies to align with human judgments of safety, comfort, and naturalistic behavior [195]. More recently, chain-of-thought and visual chain-of-thought reasoning have been introduced to enhance interpretability and robustness, by decomposing multimodal inputs into explicit intermediate steps that link perception, linguistic reasoning, and action selection [196]– [198]. This not only improves transparency in decision-making but also reduces error propagation by anchoring reasoning in visually grounded cues. In the training ecosystem, MLLMs are best interpreted as semantic decision and coordination modules , which are effective at mapping complex perception into structured in-tents, rules, and explanations. However, due to temporal in-consistency and the risk of hallucinated outputs, deploying MLLMs directly without safeguards remains difficult. Practi-cally, MLLMs complement other paradigms by providing ver-ifiable intermediate representations that condition downstream planners and controllers, such as diffusion planners or IL/RL policies. 

3) World Models : World models (WMs) represent a gener-ative paradigm that aims to endow E2E-AD systems with the ability to learn compact latent representations of environment dynamics and to use these representations for “imagination”-based planning [199]–[201]. Instead of directly mapping sen-sory observations to actions, world models first construct a predictive latent space that encodes the spatiotemporal evolu-tion of the driving environment. Policies can then be optimized within this latent space by simulating counterfactual trajec-tories or multi-agent interactions, thereby reducing reliance on costly real-world sampling [202]–[204]. This principle fundamentally shifts the training process from purely reactive behavior learning toward proactive reasoning over possible futures. The primary strengths of world models lie in sample ef-ficiency and scenario generalization. By replacing expensive environment interactions with latent rollouts, world models dramatically reduce the need for real-world data collection while still supporting long-horizon policy optimization [205], [206]. Furthermore, their capacity to generate counterfactual scenarios allows the model to explore safety-critical events that may be scarce in naturalistic datasets but crucial for robust deployment [207]. As a result, world models are well-suited for addressing the long-tail problem in E2E-AD. Nevertheless, significant challenges hinder their practical application. A central issue is the fidelity of generated rollouts, as imperfect latent predictions can diverge from real-world dynamics, undermining policy reliability. Hallucination risks, where the model produces implausible or inconsistent environ-ment states, pose safety concerns when imagination outputs are used for policy training [208]–[211]. Moreover, ensuring stability over long horizons remains difficult, as small errors in latent prediction often accumulate, leading to compounding deviations that degrade policy performance [212]. Recent advances in training strategies seek to address these challenges. Predictive modeling methods focus on learning latent state transitions that preserve physical plausibility and semantic consistency, often through self-supervised or multi-task objectives [26], [60], [213]. Imagination-augmented re-inforcement and imitation learning integrate world model rollouts into standard policy optimization, expanding the range of training scenarios without requiring direct real-world ex-ploration [139], [214], [215]. Finally, closed-loop adaptation connects imagined experiences with real-world feedback, con-tinuously refining the latent dynamics model to align imagi-nation with observed behavior. These approaches collectively aim to transform world models from auxiliary simulators into central engines of policy training [216], [217]. In the training ecosystem, world models are best positioned as imagination and evaluation backbones . As clarified in Section III-A, their first role is to serve as data engines that generate, replay, and diversify rare or high-value scenarios. Re-cent works further show that unifying visual scene generation with motion representation can make world-model represen-tations transferable from generation to planning [218]–[222]. However, their effectiveness is ultimately bounded by fidelity, especially with respect to physical rules, causal interaction, and motion consistency rather than visual appearance alone. However, their effectiveness is ultimately bounded by fidelity, especially in terms of physical rules rather than optical visual. It can serve as a joint optimization tool for other paradigms, provide more valuable data for IL, LLM, and other paradigms, and provide closed-loop simulation environment for RL train-ing or evaluation. A. Distributed and Parallel Training Platform 

For large-scale end-to-end models, the memory and throughput requirements of training exceed the capacity of a single computing device. Consequently, distributed and 9

parallel training has become a central issue in the infras-tructure layer. The design objectives of such systems can be summarized in three dimensions: first, scalability, main-taining near-linear throughput improvement as device scale grows; second, efficiency, achieving efficient overlap and load balancing between communication and computation; third, stability and reproducibility, ensuring numerical consistency and process traceability during long-cycle training. Around these objectives, five complementary parallel paradigms have been developed, which are often combined in hybrid forms in engineering practice. 

1) Data Parallelism (DP) : DP is able to replicate full models across devices, partition training data into separate forward and backward computation, and synchronize gradients via all-reduce [223]–[225]. It offers ease of implementation, insensitivity to model architecture, and near-linear throughput scaling. However, its drawbacks include high memory over-head for the reason that parameters and optimizer states must be stored on each device and sensitivity to network band-width during gradient synchronization. Gradient accumulation, compression, and delayed synchronization are usually used to alleviate bandwidth pressure in engineering practices [226], [227]. 

2) Tensor Parallelism (TP) : TP partitions models at the operator or weight level, distributing large-scale matrix multi-plications and convolutions across devices [228]. Each device stores only parameter subsets, reducing memory pressure and enabling training at ultra-large parameter scales, whose main cost is fine-grained cross-device communication, where mismatched partitioning and topology can amplify overhead. Efficient implementation requires fusion and scheduling at the operator level to maximize overlap with computation. 

3) Pipeline Parallelism (PP) : PP divides models into stages by network depth, enabling micro-batches to flow through the pipeline across stages, which can reduce peak memory via staged storage and checkpointing and improve utilization in deep networks [229]. Its effectiveness, however, is constrained by idle ‘bubble’ time and load imbalance, which can be alleviated by finer-grained stage partitioning, careful tuning of micro-batch scheduling, and applying recomputation at stage boundaries to further reduce activation storage [230], [231]. 

4) Sequence Parallelism (SP) : SP distributes activations and contexts along time or token dimensions, distributing memory and communication costs of long sequence training across devices [232]. It is especially effective for Transform-ers, extending trainable context length while reducing long sequence memory peaks. Typical implementations, such as DeepSpeed Ulysses [233], use all-to-all communication to partition attention computation. SP integrates well with DP, TP, and PP, though boundary exchanges introduce additional communication. For the excellent performance optimization, the DeepSpeed architecture is widely used for most existing model pre-training or fine-tuning [234]–[237]. 

5) Expert Parallelism (EP) : Mixture-of-experts models expand effective parameter scale without proportionally in-creasing memory and computation by introducing multiple experts in feedforward layers and performing sparse routing

While DP and TP typi-cally form the foundation for parameter scaling, PP and SP are increasingly critical for optimizing deep architectures and long-context multimodal streams. B. Testing and Evaluation Platform 

As an essential component of the E2E-AD training system, the testing platform is also responsible for the performance, safety, and reliability of models in real-world or simulated environments [243]–[245]. 

1) Simulation-Based Testing and Closed-Loop Valida-tion : Simulation-based testing plays a pivotal role in eval-uating E2E-AD models in a controlled, repeatable environ-ment. High-fidelity simulators such as CARLA, LGSVL, and NVIDIA DRIVE Sim allow models to be tested across a wide range of predefined scenarios, including edge cases and rare occurrences that would be costly or dangerous to replicate in real-world trials [35], [38], [246]–[249]. Publicly available descriptions indicate that Waymo emphasizes simulation-first validation, using its simulation platform [250] to replicate billions of virtual miles prior to limited on-road deployment. These platforms provide virtual environments that can ac-curately model road conditions, traffic patterns, and weather variations, ensuring comprehensive coverage of possible driv-ing situations. However, simulation-to-real transfer remains a critical challenge because differences between simulated and real-world environments can lead to discrepancies in model performance, especially when dealing with rare events [251], [252]. 

2) Real-World Testing and Real-Time Evaluation : While simulation provides valuable insights, real-world testing is in-dispensable for validating E2E-AD systems under actual driv-ing conditions [253], [254]. On-road testing exposes models to dynamic, unpredictable environments, including complex human behaviors, varying traffic conditions, and environmen-tal factors such as weather or road surface variations [255]. The challenge in real-world testing is to ensure safe operation of the vehicle while evaluating the model’s adaptability to real-time data streams and decision-making. Closed-loop real-world testing integrates real-time sensor data with the model’s decision-making process, allowing for feedback on how well the model adapts to unforeseen situations. A robust real-world testing platform must incorporate monitoring and safety mech-anisms to mitigate risks associated with algorithm instability, though these safeguards may sometimes mask the model’s true performance. Therefore, hybrid virtual-real testing platforms may offer a promising solution for balancing safety with accurate performance evaluation [252], [256]–[258]. Based on publicly available descriptions, industrial E2E-AD platforms appear to differ along two broad tendencies, al-though the internal details of proprietary systems are not fully observable. A verification-driven, simulation-centric regime, represented by Waymo, treats safety as a pre-deployment prop-erty that must be demonstrated through repeatable, scenario-addressable evidence; accordingly, it prioritizes high-fidelity simulators, rule-based scenario generation, and deterministic regression pipelines that support reproducibility, interpretabil-ity, and regulatory traceability. In contrast, an experience-driven, fleet-centric regime, represented by Tesla, treats safety and generalization as emergent properties that improve through large-scale exposure to real operating conditions; it there-fore prioritizes event-triggered data capture, rapid data-to-Data Online Collection Cloud Federated Learning and Distributed Training Asynchronous Update Perception Decision Planning

3) Testing Metrics and Performance Validation : The effi-cacy of testing platforms is fundamentally determined by the evaluation metrics used to measure the model’s performance [35], [36]. Traditional metrics, such as accuracy in object detection or classification, are insufficient to fully capture the complexity of autonomous driving tasks [260]. Instead, testing platforms must incorporate a broader range of metrics that assess safety, comfort, and efficiency. Key performance indi-cators include collision rates, the ability to perform emergency maneuvers, and compliance with traffic laws. As E2E-AD models grow in complexity, testing platforms must scale accordingly, handling increasing amounts of data and higher computational require-ments. However, most existing tests discover problems through massive testing, which often wastes excessive resources. How to accelerate testing and training by leveraging concepts such as scenario value may be an important research topic for the future. 

C. Cloud–Edge Collaboration and Continuous Training Plat-form 

As autonomous driving systems move from research and development to actual deployment, models face increasingly complex environmental dynamics and regional differences [265]. Relying solely on offline training can no longer meet long-term stability and cross-domain adaptability require-ments. Public information suggests that XPeng and Huawei ADS [266] have explored cloud-edge collaboration frame-works related to Fig. 5, including event-driven collection, staged updates, and cloud-supported training pipelines. Cloud-edge collaboration and continuous training thus become key 11 

paths for improving system evolution capability [37], [267]– [269]. Their core objective is to establish closed-loop pathways between vehicle-side and cloud-side: the vehicle side focuses on event-triggered data collection and lightweight processing, while the cloud side provides large-scale computing support, enabling asynchronous incremental model updates and cross-regional adaptation. Thus, cloud-edge collaboration not only achieves decou-pling and coordination between training and deployment but also provides dynamic adaptation and continuous optimization capabilities for autonomous driving models, serving as core technical guarantees for system large-scale deployment. 

1) Online Collection and Edge Preprocessing : After de-ployment, vehicle-side systems should use event-driven collec-tion strategies as core, prioritizing the recording of samples with high marginal value for safety and robustness, such as emergency braking, sharp turns, close encounters, abnormal yielding, and perception divergence. Vehicle sides complete preliminary quality filtering and redundancy removal, includ-ing time synchronization correction, basic calibration veri-fication, and compression encoding, performing lightweight anonymization and desensitization processing locally as much as possible, then uploading in grades according to bandwidth and priority. Compared to traditional offline collection, this mechanism continuously supplements long-tail scenarios at low additional cost and provides high-frequency fresh samples during regional migration and seasonal changes. 

2) Asynchronous Updates and Model Governance : Con-tinuous training needs to break hard constraints of synchronous cycles, forming asynchronous mechanisms where data flow, training flow, and deployment flow advance in parallel. After data enters training platforms, incremental training jobs are automatically triggered, producing candidate models and reg-istering them in model repositories. Each candidate version must be bound to clear data versions, configurations, and checkpoints, completing offline metrics and replay set evalua-tion before entering online validation. To shorten improvement realization time, stable job orchestration and resource schedul-ing strategies should be established, enabling deep overlap of decoding, augmentation, communication, and computation to improve throughput and timeliness. Meanwhile, simple and clear promotion criteria should be set to ensure update rhythms are predictable and results interpretable. 

3) Gray Update and Rollback Mechanisms : New models should not go fully online at once. Gray release follows principles of small scale and controllable scope, first enabling new versions in limited fleets or specific regions, contin-uously observing safety-related metrics, interaction quality metrics, and system loads, then gradually expanding coverage in phases. To prevent occasional mismatches from evolving into systematic risks, one-click rollback channels must be established: when degradation alerts are triggered or preset thresholds are exceeded, immediately switch to the previous stable version and automatically archive related data to support subsequent localization and review. The coordination of gray release and rollback makes trial and error controllable, enables rapid damage control for failures, while providing high-value failure samples for continuous training. Publicly reported practices from companies such as Waymo, Tesla, Cruise, Baidu Apollo, XPeng, and Huawei suggest different philosophies regarding generalization, safety validation, and update efficiency. VI. O PEN CHALLENGES AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS 

E2E-AD is currently transitioning from scientific explo-ration to large-scale engineering deployment. To address the constraints of application, the training ecosystem should evolve from isolated algorithmic optimizations to systemic synergy. Future developments will synchronize across three axes: increasing data value density, establishing foundation model capabilities, and enabling continuous platform evo-lution, which can reinforce each other to define the next generation of robust and scalable autonomous systems. 

A. Scenario Prioritization and Data Engine: From “Data Volume” to “Value Density” 

Building on the value-driven governance defined in Sec-tion III, the primary engineering challenge remains the con-struction of automated value scoring systems that can operate at scale. While the necessity of ”value density” is clear, implementing it requires overcoming significant bottlenecks in current data infrastructures. First, collection processes rely on fixed routes or random strategies, with insufficient coverage of key scenarios and redundant samples occupying substantial resources. Addi-tionally, annotation processes are inefficient, especially in multimodal data processing where spatiotemporal alignment problems remain unsolved, and lack of hierarchical man-agement leads to high-cost annotation consumption on low-value samples. Finally, governance processes are fragmented, with incomplete version records and difficult traceability of additions and removals, thus limiting reproducibility of model iteration. To achieve continuous progress, systematic break-throughs are urgently needed in the following aspects:  

> •

Construct scenario value systems. Establish quantifi-able and operational scenario value scoring systems that comprehensively consider risk, rarity, learnability, and coverage gaps to form unified priority lists. Implement hierarchical treatment and resource allocation based on thresholds, making collection and annotation focus on scenarios with the highest marginal returns.  

> •

Implement event collection and controllable synthesis. 

In event-triggered collection, rely on vehicle-side shadow operation around trigger conditions such as emergency braking, sharp turns, and close encounters to prioritize recording “near-miss events” and capture critical decision samples from human driving. B. Universal Driving Foundation Models and Regional Adap-tation: Strong Transfer, Weak Customization 

E2E-AD is moving from scenario-specific optimization to-ward universal foundation models capable of operating across cities, road types, and driving cultures. Such models must provide consistent multimodal, long-sequence representations that align training objectives with planning and safety metrics, reducing the gap between offline accuracy and online reliabil-ity. They also need to reconcile efficient regional customization through lightweight adaptation, stable cross-domain transfer without catastrophic forgetting, and interpretable decision-making for safety assurance. However, current foundation models degrade sharply in new domains in practice. Differences in signage, rules, and driving habits often cause planning drift, while existing adaptation methods rely on costly retraining or extensive annotation and struggle with efficiency. Performance instability and catas-trophic forgetting remain common, and the lack of clear criteria for when to retrain versus fine-tune further impedes deployment. Limited interpretability compounds these issues, making adaptation outcomes difficult to assess and verify. Future research needs technological breakthroughs in the fol-lowing key directions:  

> •

Cross-domain knowledge transfer and unified rep-resentation learning. Construct unified representation frameworks with cross-modal and cross-temporal con-sistency, enabling models to maintain stability across different cities and road conditions. Future work can com-bine contrastive learning, domain adaptation, and causal representation learning to enhance model transfer capa-bilities under changes in road rules, signage styles, and driving habits, thereby reducing needs for re-annotation and training.  

> •

Lightweight regional adaptation and parameter-efficient updates. Achieve rapid updates for new cities or road styles through lightweight adaptation layers or prompt tuning methods. Research how to use small-scale incremental data for parameter low-rank updates or sparse adaptation while ensuring performance while reducing computational and storage overhead.

C. Continuous Learning and Training Operations: Closed-Loop Training Solutions with Training-Testing Integration 

To enhance E2E-AD as they move from closed sites to urban environments, it is essential to address the increasing complexity of scenarios, environmental variability, and strict safety requirements. A critical challenge arises from the per-sistent separation of training and testing processes: traditional methods rely on static datasets and one-time evaluations, which cannot capture the frequency and diversity of real-world changes. The resulting disconnect creates a significant gap between models that perform well in offline tests but fail to generalize effectively to unpredictable, real-world situations. Addressing these limitations requires training-testing inte-gration shown in Fig. 6. As illustrated in the left panel ,the process begins with data virtual-real fusion , where real-world corner cases are augmented by controllable genera-tion to ensure high value density. These inputs feed into the central Platform layer , which manages the distillation 

of large foundation models into vehicle-embedded versions. Finally, the right panel depicts strategy continuous evolution ,where techniques such as evolutionary algorithms and forget-ting suppression are applied to ensure that updates improve performance without degrading previously learned capabilities. However, constructing such a system requires solving the following core tasks:  

> •

Construct unified data-model-evaluation lineage sys-tems. Currently, training, testing, and deployment often belong to different platforms, lacking unified metadata structures and process records, making model perfor-mance difficult to trace and version comparisons difficult to reproduce. Future work needs to establish consistent lineage management mechanisms among training sam-ples, model versions, and evaluation results, clarifying data inputs, optimization configurations, and validation outputs that each update depends on, supporting scenario-level reproduction verification, thereby providing clear technical basis and audit traceability for iteration paths. 14  

> •

Develop model evaluation systems based on closed-loop metrics. Traditional evaluations mostly focus on accuracy metrics, making it difficult to cover strategy reasonableness and safety redundancy in end-to-end de-cision chains. Future work needs to construct task-level closed-loop evaluation metric systems around “systematic stability, decision reasonableness, risk sensitivity,” com-bining high-risk case replay, counterfactual perturbation verification, and simulation result comparison to achieve systematic evaluation of model behavior stability and robustness, enabling training results to truly map to online performance.  

> •

Establish reproducible minimum closed-loop mech-anisms for training-validation-deployment. Minimum training closed-loop paths centered on problem-driven, metric-constrained, and resource-sensitive approaches should be constructed. Starting from failure sample trig-gers, determining problem attribution through scenario replay and label verification, executing targeted retraining with minimal data increments, conducting performance confirmation through standardized validation sets, and finally supporting phased deployment and automatic roll-back strategies. This mechanism needs three major char-acteristics: controllable processes, controllable resources, and controllable benefits, serving as underlying capa-bilities supporting large-scale evolution of autonomous driving. VII. C ONCLUSION 

Training constitutes the cornerstone of E2E-AD, determin-ing both performance boundaries and the trajectory of capa-bility evolution. While unified modeling promises advantages over modular pipelines, the training process remains con-strained by long-tail data scarcity, unstable optimization, and platform bottlenecks. The central insight of our Data-Strategy-Platform analysis is that these challenges are not isolated hurdles but systemic dependencies that require a synchronized evolution of the training ecosystem. Future progress, therefore, depends less on individual breakthroughs in any single layer and more on the synergy across all three. It is this collaborative optimization of data quality, strategic alignment, and platform scalability that will unlock the reliability required for large-scale, sustained real-world deployment.
