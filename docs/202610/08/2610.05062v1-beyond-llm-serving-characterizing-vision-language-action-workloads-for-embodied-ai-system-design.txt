## Abstract 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models translate multimodal observations into low-level robot actions. During robot op-eration, each control period sets an inference deadline, and overruns leave the robot acting on stale observations, re-ducing task success. Meeting this deadline motivates on-device or nearby edge execution, where a single robot re-quires batch-1 inference outside the design point of LLM serving systems. Although VLA architectures combine fa-miliar vision-language, autoregressive, and diffusion-style components, their runtime behavior in this batch-1 control setting remains uncharacterized. We characterize four rep-resentative VLA models on an edge GPU server and two onboard SoCs, using single-inference profiling and 43,200 closed-loop episodes. Action tensor dimensionality deter-mines whether a stage is memory- or compute-bound, plat-form balance can shift that bottleneck, and GPU frequency scaling yields a platform-dependent energy–latency sweet spot. In closed-loop operation, overlapping inference with action execution creates an accuracy–speed–energy tradeoff, and no configuration is Pareto-dominant across deployment SLOs. These results guide joint design of VLA model archi-tectures, hardware, and runtime policies.

## 1 Introduction 

Embodied action generation models, often referred to as vision-language-action (VLA) models [5, 7, 8, 11, 21, 22, 25, 27, 33, 44, 45, 52, 60], translate multimodal observations into low-level robot control signals for manipulation tasks. Real-world manipulation requires smooth and continuous mo-tion, so models designed for real robots increasingly pro-duce not a single next command but an action chunk , a fixed-length sequence of future commands that together form a short trajectory segment [19, 59]. To generate such chunks, these models typically combine a large perception backbone with a specialized action-generation head , a neu-ral network that converts the backbone’s latent represen-tation into the action chunk. Recent architectures generate action chunks through iterative denoising over compact ac-tion tensors [5, 7, 8, 11, 25, 33, 44, 52], single-pass parallel decoding [21, 27, 45], or unified diffusion over spatiotem-poral video latents [22]. These action-generation choices produce different latency and resource profiles that shape deployment decisions. During deployment, these models operate in a closed loop: the robot continuously senses the environment, invokes the model, executes the resulting commands, and updates its next input based on the physical outcome [31, 42]. The con-trol period, roughly 33 ms for contact-rich manipulation, therefore acts as a recurring deadline rather than merely a per-request latency target: inference that falls behind it leaves the robot acting on stale observations, and task success degrades. Meeting this deadline favors on-device hardware or nearby consumer-grade edge servers because remote serv-ing adds round-trip delay to the same latency budget. In the single-robot setting studied here, inference therefore runs at batch size one.

LLM serving research has produced a mature toolbox, including continuous batching, paged KV caches, and prefill-decode disaggregation, designed around concurrent autore-gressive requests and token-level execution [24, 62]. These assumptions hold only partially in this setting: batch-1 serv-ing provides no request concurrency, the action-chunk mod-els we evaluate produce their chunks non-autoregressively, and the dominant stage varies with the architecture rather than with an autoregressive phase. Standard LLM serving optimizations therefore apply selectively (Section 6). VLA model families draw on familiar vision-language, autoregres-sive, and diffusion-style components, but how these com-ponents interact under batch-1 closed-loop serving has not been characterized. To fill this gap, we conduct a system-level workload charac-terization of VLA serving. We study four representative mod-els, namely GR00T N1.6 [33], 𝜋 0.5 [4], OpenVLA-OFT [21], and Cosmos-Policy [22], that span three action-generation paradigms: iterative denoising, parallel decoding, and video generation. We evaluate these models on three platforms that span over an order of magnitude in power budget: RTX 4090, Jetson Thor, and Jetson AGX Orin [37]. Our methodology combines four measurement techniques that target different levels of the system stack: stage-level latency decomposition, roofline-based bottleneck attribution, DVFS [41, 47, 51] sensi-tivity analysis, and closed-loop deployment on MuJoCo [48] with LIBERO [30] benchmarks, totaling 43,200 closed-loop episodes. Our single-inference analysis shows that action tensor dimensions separate memory-bound stages from compute-bound ones. Memory-bound stages tolerate lower GPU clocks. Reducing memory bandwidth, however, raises la-tency and energy on both SoCs. The same bottleneck dif-ferences shape throughput per joule in deployment. Over-lapping inference with action execution [6, 44, 46] creates an accuracy–speed–energy tradeoff. Accuracy degradation depends on model architecture, relative speedup is stable across platforms, and energy scaling depends on platform characteristics. To our knowledge, this is the first system-level workload characterization to connect stage-level latency and roofline analysis with cross-platform DVFS sensitivity and closed-loop deployment. Our contributions are as follows: 

Cross-model characterization. We show that VLA serv-ing has no uniform bottleneck profile: torch.compile 

speedups differ by over 5× between action-head-dominated and backbone-dominated models. This implies that compiler and kernel-level optimizations must be applied selectively per architecture rather than uniformly. 

Microarchitectural analysis. We show that the memory-versus compute-bound split is determined by action ten-sor dimensionality. Denoising-head GEMMs fall below the 163.9 FLOP/B ridge point, whereas large-sequence backbone and video-generation DiT GEMMs exceed it. This provides a basis for accelerator bandwidth-to-compute provisioning in VLA-targeted hardware. 

Platform and energy insights. We show that GPU fre-quency scaling yields an energy sweet spot whose benefit depends on platform balance, as the same sweep saves 24– 32% energy on Orin but only 1–4% on Thor, while memory-bandwidth throttling provides no energy benefit on either SoC. We further show that, when evaluated in isolation, as-signing each inference phase its own GPU frequency can reduce both latency and energy when the phases exhibit different frequency sensitivities. However, in end-to-end ex-ecution, the overhead of switching GPU frequencies between phases can offset these gains. 

Closed-loop tradeoff analysis. We quantify a three-way tradeoff in which accuracy degrades by up to 56 percentage points, speed improves by up to 3.9 ×, and energy scales by up to 5.1 × under increasing overlap, with no Pareto-dominant configuration across SLOs. This establishes that runtime systems must support SLO-aware operating-point selection rather than static configurations. These observations motivate bottleneck-aware co-design across model architecture, hardware platform, and runtime policy for embodied AI system design. 

## 2 Background & Motivation 

2.1 Embodied Action Generation Models 

Embodied action generation models produce low-level ro-bot actions from visual observations and language instruc-tions [31, 42]. Recent architectures first encode the multi-modal observation and the instruction into an internal rep-resentation, and then generate actions from it. They differ in the architecture that builds this representation and in the action-generation interface. In the VLM-based models, the perception backbone typi-cally combines a vision encoder (e.g., ViT [13], SigLIP [58]) with a language model (e.g., Llama 2 [49], PaliGemma [2]) to produce contextual embeddings that capture both spa-tial and semantic information. This stage performs a single forward pass through billions of parameters, inheriting the computational profile of large vision-language models. The action-generation interface takes one of two forms: a sequence of discretized action tokens generated autoregres-sively, or an action chunk , a fixed-length sequence of future robot commands produced jointly in one inference [19, 59]. 

Autoregressive action-token interface. Token-based VLAs such as RT-2 [64] and vanilla OpenVLA [23] represent a robot action as a sequence of discretized action tokens generated sequentially by the backbone’s language-model decoder. In the OpenVLA formulation we profile, the target robot’s action space has 𝐷 dimensions, and each dimension maps to one token. One action therefore requires 𝐷 sequen-tial token-generation steps: one prefill step followed by 𝐷 − 1cached-decode passes. This sequential decode path is difficult to amortize under high-rate control. On the RTX 4090, its six cached-decode passes take 140.90 ms in our 7-dimensional setting. This is 3.2 × the LLM prefill latency and accounts for 67% of the 209.56 ms inference (Table 1). Cached decode therefore dom-inates the latency of autoregressive action generation. The same decode path exhibits the familiar LLM resource split. Prefill is mostly compute-bound, whereas cached de-code is entirely memory-bound (Section 4.2). However, the fixed decode horizon leaves little opportunity for KV-cache reuse. The cache grows by only 𝐷 − 1 positions within an action and is discarded when the action completes. Because this interface emits one 𝐷 -token action per infer-ence, producing 𝐾 future actions repeats those 𝐷 sequential steps 𝐾 times. OpenVLA-OFT [21] instead emits an entire 

𝐾 -action chunk in one pass. Its reported action throughput on LIBERO is roughly 26 × that of autoregressive OpenVLA. This contrast motivates the action-chunk interface, which amortizes one inference over 𝐾 future commands. Action-chunk models use this interface but generate each chunk through one of three representative paradigms. 

Iterative denoising heads. This paradigm generates the action chunk through a multi-step denoising process that invokes the same neural network 𝑁 times (typically 4–10) per action prediction. Representative models refine a random initial tensor into the action trajectory, using flow-matching objectives [29] (GR00T [33], 𝜋 0 [5], SmolVLA [44], GR-3 [8], GraspVLA [11]) or denoising diffusion [10, 18] (RoboDual [7], CogACT [25], DiffusionVLA [52]). Regardless of the train-ing objective, the computational signature is shared: the state being denoised is a compact action trajectory tensor of shape [B, action_horizon , action_dim ] (batch size 1 in single-robot deployment), so each denoising step runs the network over this short, low-dimensional sequence. 

Parallel decoding heads. This paradigm generates the entire action chunk in a single forward pass using parallel to-ken prediction or regression. Representative models include OpenVLA-OFT [21] (L1 regression MLP), PD-VLA [45], and CogVLA [27].

2.2 Closed-Loop Policy Serving 

When these policies drive a robot in closed loop, each infer-ence must complete within a fixed control period, typically 33 ms or less for the 30 Hz rates required by contact-rich ma-nipulation tasks. Current policy models are far from meeting this budget [21, 50, 54, 61]: in our measurements, inference takes 117–396 ms on an edge-server GPU (RTX 4090) and 304–2603 ms on an edge SoC (Jetson AGX Orin), yielding ef-fective control frequencies of only 2.5–8.5 Hz and 0.4–3.3 Hz, respectively. In a synchronous execution scheme, the control loop cap-tures a sensor observation, invokes the policy model, and dispatches the resulting action chunk to the actuator before the next cycle begins [4, 5, 14, 21, 33, 64]. This design maxi-mizes observation freshness but, when inference overruns the control period, forces the pipeline to stall, repeating stale actions and introducing visible discontinuities in the robot’s motion trajectory. Unlike offline evaluation, where a simula-tor simply pauses during inference, real-world deployment offers no mechanism to stop time, so every millisecond of excess latency directly degrades control quality [6]. To bridge this gap, deployed systems employ asynchro-nous overlap [6, 44, 46]: the system issues the next inference request while the robot is still executing actions from the cur-rent chunk, hiding inference latency behind action execution (Figure 2). However, asynchronous overlap introduces a tradeoff: be-cause inference is initiated before the current chunk com-pletes, the observation used for the next prediction ages while the robot continues executing [50]. We refer to this lag as observation staleness , the interval between observation capture and the execution of actions derived from it: 

𝑠 = 𝑡 execute − 𝑡 capture . (1) More aggressive overlap enables faster control but forces the robot to act on increasingly stale observations, potentially degrading task accuracy [43]. We refer to this as the accuracy– staleness tradeoff .

2.3 Motivation 

The workload described in Section 2.1 and Section 2.2 di-verges from the assumptions that underlie LLM serving in-frastructure in three ways. Each divergence motivates a dis-tinct dimension of system-level characterization. 

M1. Action-generation heterogeneity requires stage-level bottleneck analysis. LLM serving analyses decom-pose cost along the autoregressive phases of prefill and de-code. That decomposition carries over to the action-token in-terface, which uses the same prefill–decode mechanism. The action-chunk models described in Section 2.1, however, use heterogeneous iterative-denoising, parallel-decoding, and video-generation stages, which vary further within each par-adigm through tensor shape and GEMM dimension choices. Characterizing them requires a stage-level decomposition organized around action-generation design. 

M2. Latency and task accuracy are coupled, not orthog-onal. In LLM serving, latency is a service-level property and response accuracy is a model property, and the two can be optimized independently. The accuracy–staleness trade-off described in Section 2.2 removes this separation: per-inference latency affects the feasible overlap, which changes observation staleness and task success. Understanding this coupling requires measurement at the deployment level, not the per-inference level alone. 

M3. Deployment frequently spans heterogeneous plat-forms. LLM serving has primarily targeted datacenter-class GPUs. Embodied action generation often runs on edge SoCs in addition to server GPUs, because autonomous opera-tion may occur where network connectivity is unreliable or round-trip latency exceeds the control period. These plat-forms differ in compute, bandwidth, and power, so a charac-terization on a single tier does not readily transfer to others.

Table 3 covers an edge server and two onboard SoCs, with power budgets ranging from 15 W to 450 W. Jetson Thor and AGX Orin [37] serve as deployment targets representing next- and current-generation mobile robots. All platforms run PyTorch 2.10. The RTX 4090 uses CUDA 13.1, while Thor and AGX Orin use JetPack 7.0 and 6.2 respectively, each bundling matched CUDA [34], cuDNN [9], and TensorRT [38] versions for its SoC generation.

4.3.3 Phase-Aware Frequency Policy. Sections 4.3.1 and 4.3.2 show that the phases of one inference place dif-ferent demands on host dispatch, memory bandwidth, and GPU compute. We derive three rules from these measure-ments and evaluate them together. (i) CPU at maximum frequency. Iterative action heads repeatedly dispatch small kernels and gain most from compilation and kernel fusion (Takeaway 1), so keeping the CPU at maximum frequency reduces host-side dispatch delay. (ii) EMC at maximum fre-quency. EMC throttling increases per-inference energy on both SoCs (Section 4.3.2), so we keep EMC at maximum fre-quency. (iii) GPU frequency selected for each phase. Phases with different resource demands respond differently to GPU clock (Takeaway 3), so we allow each phase to use a separate clock. Together, the fixed CPU and EMC settings and phase-specific GPU settings form the phase-aware policy evaluated below. 

Sweep and selection. With the first two rules active, we sweep five candidate GPU clocks over the range used by each platform’s governor: 306–1300 MHz on Orin and 315–1575 MHz on Thor. A two-phase model assigns one of the five clocks to each phase independently, so we evalu-ate all 25 clock combinations. The baseline is the default platform DVFS configuration, which leaves the GPU, EMC, and CPU frequencies under their default governors and en-ables CPU idle. We select the minimum-energy phase-wise assignment whose latency is lower than this baseline. Staleness-aware VLA serving abstraction. Our measure-ments motivate a runtime interface that exposes four kinds of state: per-stage cost, action-chunk progress, observation freshness, and deployment SLOs. Concretely, the interface re-ports each stage’s latency and compute/bandwidth demand, the number of unexecuted actions in the current chunk and the time at which they run out, the timestamp and staleness budget of the latest observation, and the target SLOs for la-tency, energy, power, and task success. A scheduler could use this state to select execution timing, overlap ratio, resource settings, and where each stage executes. Architecture implications. The measured bottlenecks suggest several hardware features that could improve VLA inference, although we do not propose a specific accelerator design. Large GEMMs in backbones and video-generation DiTs benefit from high tensor-compute throughput. Denois-ing loops instead issue many small, fixed-shape GEMMs, so they benefit from hardware that sustains high utilization on small matrix operations. Memory-bound action heads benefit from higher memory bandwidth, and iteration-heavy action heads benefit from low kernel-launch and control overhead. A denoising loop also reloads the same weights at every step, so cache or local memory that keeps them on chip could reduce this repeated traffic. Finally, faster frequency transitions would reduce the measured overhead that limits our phase-aware DVFS policy. 

Selective applicability of existing optimizations. The control-period deadline favors on-board or nearby edge exe-cution because remote execution adds network latency and jitter to the observation-staleness budget (Section 5). In the single-robot setting studied here, requests arrive one at a time, so inference runs at batch size one. We assess the op-timizations discussed below using three workload proper-ties: request concurrency, autoregressive generation, and the dominant execution stage. Techniques that optimize computation within a single in-ference (kernel fusion and graph capture, step distillation, feature caching, weight-only quantization) remain applica-ble at batch size one, although their benefit depends on the dominant stage. Among these, we directly evaluate compila-tion, which delivers 4.4–7.4 × speedups on iteration-heavy action heads (Section 4.1.2). Request-level and KV-cache optimizations are more con-strained in this setting. Because a single robot issues only one request at a time, continuous batching offers little ben-efit unless a server handles requests from multiple robots. The autoregressive baseline still uses a KV cache, but its decode length is short and fixed by the action format, so it is known before decoding starts. This leaves little opportunity for paged KV-cache management to reduce fragmentation or improve memory utilization. The cache is also discarded after each action is generated, so the profiled implementation retains no prefix to reuse across inferences (Section 2.1). Techniques specific to autoregressive decoding, such as speculative decoding, have a still narrower scope. Among the models we study, they apply only to the autoregressive baseline. Even there, the decode is so short that the overhead of running a draft model may outweigh the benefit. Except for compilation, these conclusions are based on workload structure rather than measured speedups. 

Scope and limitations. System-design scope. We charac-terize the constraints a VLA runtime must handle, but we do not implement a scheduler. A deployable phase-aware policy must account for transition overhead and phase duration. Stage co-execution must account for model dependencies and shared-memory contention. 

Simulation scope. Across 40 LIBERO tasks, we compare the models under identical MuJoCo dynamics in 43,200 episodes spanning model, platform, and overlap configurations. Mu-JoCo provides controller-mediated actuation dynamics, infer-ence timing comes from the target hardware, and each con-trol period includes a conservative 1.5 × margin over the mea-sured worst-case RTT. This setup relates control frequency and observation staleness to task success under controlled conditions. It does not replace real-robot or hardware-in-the-loop validation: sensor pipelines, network jitter, multi-camera synchronization, unmodeled contact dynamics, and physical disturbances are outside our scope. 

Model scope. We profile autoregressive OpenVLA as the discrete action-token baseline. The closed-loop evaluation covers the four action-chunk models used for continuous high-rate control. The OpenVLA profile retains the known memory-bound LLM decode behavior. Alternative autore-gressive control interfaces are outside our scope. Takeaway 2 may extend to other models in the same para-digm, although exact stage proportions depend on model size and chunk length. Hardware scope. We evaluate one discrete edge server and two unified-memory onboard SoCs, all from NVIDIA. Sen-sitivity varies across them with compute-to-bandwidth bal-ance. Non-NVIDIA accelerators are not evaluated. 

Energy-measurement scope. Cross-platform energy com-parisons reflect relative trends rather than absolute ratios due to differing measurement boundaries (Section 3), while within-platform comparisons are directly comparable. 

## 7 Related Work 

Algorithmic perspectives on VLA efficiency. Recent sur-veys organize the VLA landscape along algorithmic axes: Kawaharazuka et al. [20] provide a full-stack review span-ning architectures and robot platforms, Yu et al. [56] propose a taxonomy of efficient VLAs across model design, training, and data collection, and Guan et al. [15] categorize efficiency techniques by architecture, perception, action generation, and training/inference strategy. These works describe pro-posed methods but provide limited empirical characteriza-tion of how deployed systems behave on specific hardware. Deployment-level bottleneck attribution, DVFS-sensitive en-ergy behavior, and closed-loop operating-point analysis re-ceive less empirical attention. 

LLM serving systems. LLM servers improve throughput and goodput for concurrent autoregressive requests through scheduling and batching [1, 55], paged KV-cache manage-ment [24], and prefill–decode disaggregation [39, 63]. VLA serving changes both the scheduling unit and the objec-tive. A single-robot batch-1 loop schedules backbone and action-head stages instead of prefill and decode phases across independent requests. Placement and queuing delay also in-crease observation staleness, coupling task success to control-period, accuracy, and energy constraints. 

ML inference characterization. Roofline models explain memory-bound LLM decoding and guide serving optimiza-tion [57]. RooflineBench [3] extends operational-intensity comparison to on-device LLMs. Roofline analysis also ex-plains the memory-bound autoregressive OpenVLA decode loop in Section 2.1. We apply it to heterogeneous action-chunk computation across iterative denoising, parallel de-coding, and video generation, and separately measure closed-loop staleness in batch-1 serving. 

## 8 Conclusion 

This work presents a system-level workload characteriza-tion of embodied action generation model serving across four models, three platforms, and 40 LIBERO tasks. At the single-inference level, we show that the compute- versus memory-bound behavior is largely governed by action tensor dimensionality. Under closed-loop overlap, accuracy degra-dation depends on model architecture, while energy scaling depends on the hardware platform, with no configuration remaining Pareto-dominant across SLOs. These results high-light the need for architecture-aware resource allocation and staleness-aware operating-point selection. Together, our findings call for VLA systems that jointly consider model ar-chitecture, hardware resource balance, and runtime policies, providing a foundation for future accelerators and serving systems tailored to embodied AI.
