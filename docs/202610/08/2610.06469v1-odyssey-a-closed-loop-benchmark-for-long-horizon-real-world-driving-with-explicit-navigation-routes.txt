## ABSTRACT 

Closed-loop evaluation of end-to-end driving requires continuous rollouts that re-veal how earlier decisions affect subsequent driving. However, existing bench-marks evaluate only short segments and fail to capture later consequences. Am-biguous directional commands also obscure the intended navigation objective. We introduce Odyssey, a closed-loop benchmark for long-horizon driving comprising 100 scenarios, each reconstructed from a 100-second nuPlan driving log to pre-serve the context of navigation maneuvers and traffic interactions. To provide a consistent navigation objective, Odyssey replaces directional commands with explicit standard-definition (SD) map routes that specify which roads to follow, while sensor-based planning determines local driving actions. Throughout these rollouts, diffusion-based refinement of 3DGS-rendered images reduces rendering artifacts along the ego trajectory. To assess how effectively planners follow these

A central challenge in autonomous driving is reli-able navigation through complex traffic environ-ments that ensure safety and comfort, as well as efficient progress toward an intended destina-tion. With the growing availability of large-scale driving data, planning-oriented end-to-end (E2E) paradigms have gained prominence (Hu et al., 2023; Liao et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2026; Kirby et al., 2026), in which driving systems directly map onboard sensor observations to driving de-cisions. Such planners should be evaluated by their ability to sustain safe driving as the vehicle interacts with its environment over time. Since open-loop evaluation independently compares de-cisions at individual time steps against recorded driving logs, it cannot capture their downstream effects. Accounting for downstream effects via closed-loop evaluation is essential to assess driv-ing performance under evolving traffic conditions. For E2E planners, closed-loop evaluation requires sensor observations that reflect the consequences of the ego vehicle’s actions. Neural rendering provides this feedback by reconstructing 3D real-world scenes from driving logs and rendering new observations from the simulated ego pose. Early approaches relied on neural radiance fields (NeRFs) (Yang et al., 2023; Tonderski et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024), whose volumetric rendering in-curs substantial computational cost. To reduce this cost, recent methods adopt 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) (Zhou et al., 2024b; Hess et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024a; Chen et al., 2025) for faster rendering while maintaining high visual fidelity. These developments have supported growing re-search on neural-rendering-based closed-loop benchmarks (Ljungbergh et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025; NVIDIA et al., 2025) for E2E planners. However, existing benchmarks are limited in assess-ing a planner’s ability to handle long-term driving tasks (see Figure 1 (a)). One limitation of existing closed-loop benchmarks (Ljungbergh et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025) is their short evaluation horizon, typically limited to approximately 20 seconds. Such short rollouts are insufficient to fully assess the navigation capabilities of E2E planners, particularly when navigation requires a sequence of coordinated maneuvers. For example, as shown in Figure 2 (a), completing an upcoming right turn may require changing lanes in advance. However, the evaluation may termi-nate before the turn occurs, making it hard to assess the planner’s ability to complete the maneuver sequence. Furthermore, high-level meta-action commands used in many E2E benchmarks (Cae-sar et al., 2020; Dauner et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026) provide ambiguous guidance for navigation in real-world driving scenarios. As illustrated in Figure 1 (a), a directional command such as “turn right” may correspond to multiple possible maneuvers. For example, if two right exits occur in succession, the command alone does not indicate which exit the planner should take. Annotating these commands in real-world driving datasets may not accurately 2Preprint reflect the driver’s intended maneuver (OpenScene Contributors, 2023), as shown in Figure 2 (b). Reliable long-horizon evaluation therefore requires more explicit navigation guidance than high-level meta-action commands alone can provide. Another challenge is maintaining high rendering quality throughout long-horizon closed-loop simu-lations. When the ego vehicle deviates from the trajectory recorded during data collection, rendering quality may deteriorate because novel viewpoints lack sufficient observational support. The result-ing artifacts can distort the visual input to E2E planners, influence their subsequent decisions, and undermine the reliability of both planning and evaluation. In addition, existing evaluation setups do not accurately model changes in traffic signals or pedestrian motion, further limiting their ability to represent realistic driving scenarios. In this paper, we introduce Odyssey , a real-world closed-loop benchmark for evaluating E2E driv-ing models over long-horizon rollouts. Odyssey uses neural rendering to reconstruct 3D scenes and support realistic closed-loop simulations. Each scenario includes an explicit navigation route repre-sented on a standard-definition (SD) map. By combining long-horizon evaluation with explicit route guidance, Odyssey enables a comprehensive assessment of models’ ability to navigate complex road structures safely—a capability that existing benchmarks have not adequately evaluated. For these extended rollouts, Odyssey builds on the explicit geometric representation of 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023), followed by diffusion-based refinement for improving visual fidelity. Using OmniRe (Chen et al., 2025), we reconstruct continuous 100-second driving environments with rigid vehicles and deformable pedestrians whose motion is controlled independently of the static background. We further apply its time-conditioned deformation field to traffic lights to model changes in their color states throughout the sequence. To reduce blur and recover missing detail at novel ego poses, we apply single-step diffusion refinement (Wu et al., 2025; NVIDIA, 2025b) to the rendered camera images without modifying the underlying scene representation or simulated state. Odyssey provides navigation routes as explicit guidance for E2E planners. By specifying the in-tended sequence of road segments, they resolve the ambiguity inherent in directional commands and eliminate the need to determine when to issue individual high-level meta-action commands. For each scenario, we construct an SD-map route from trip data of the data-collection fleet. How-ever, this road-level representation encodes only coarse road-level topology and does not specify lane-level structure or the precise goal location that a driving model should reach. E2E planners must therefore interpret the high-level route jointly with the scene perceived by onboard sensors and translate it into a concrete, scene-consistent navigation goal. This requires the planner to reason about how an abstract road-level route is translated to appropriate lane-level maneuvers within the current road geometry. Odyssey establishes a foundation for evaluating such reasoning capability of E2E planners. We design the closed-loop benchmark to enable systematic comparison of different E2E planners. We curate 100 scenarios from the raw nuPlan data (Karnchanachari et al., 2024), covering sequences of complex navigation maneuvers and diverse road topologies. We further introduce two evaluation metrics, termed Pre-Lane Change Score and RouteDS , which assess whether the vehicle is appro-priately positioned for upcoming navigation maneuvers, as well as driving safety and progress along the intended route. We evaluate a set of baseline planners adapted from recent state-of-the-art E2E and vision-language-action (VLA) models, incorporating navigation routes as additional input. Our main contributions are as follows: • We introduce Odyssey , a high-fidelity real-world closed-loop benchmark for evaluating whether E2E planners can sustain safe and goal-directed driving under explicit route guid-ance over long-horizon rollouts. • We evaluate planners over continuous rollouts based on recorded 100-second sequences, with explicit SD-map routes that resolve ambiguities in high-level driving commands and enable consistent assessment of navigation performance across complex road topologies. • We develop a closed-loop simulation framework that combines the structural consistency of 3DGS with diffusion-based refinement to improve rendering fidelity when ego-vehicle motion reveals poorly reconstructed or previously unobserved regions. • We will release the benchmark, evaluation code, and baseline results to enable reproducible comparison and facilitate further research on long-horizon closed-loop evaluation for au-tonomous driving. 3Preprint Table 1: Comparison of driving benchmarks and simulation frameworks. ✓, △, ×, and - denote support, partial support, no documented support, and not applicable, respectively.

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Open-loop displacement metrics overlook how planning actions affect subsequent driving (Caesar et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2022; 2023). NAVSIM introduces simulation-based driving metrics (Dauner et al., 2024), and NAVSIM v2 adds pre-rendered feedback (Cao et al., 2025). Continuous replanning further requires observations from the ego poses reached after executing successive actions. Neural simulators provide this feedback in reconstructed environments (Yang et al., 2023; Tonderski et al., 2024; Ljungbergh et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025; NVIDIA et al., 2025). However, short evaluation sequences can end before earlier lane choices affect later navigation (Ljungbergh et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025). Assessing these consequences also requires a consistent navigation objective. Directional com-mands can leave consecutive branches ambiguous (Hu et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2023; Dauner et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025), obscuring whether route deviations reflect planning errors or insufficient guidance. Nearby target points provide spatial guidance but limited context for upcoming route choices (Dosovitskiy et al., 2017; Jia et al., 2024). Odyssey addresses these temporal and navi-gational limitations through extended closed-loop rollouts with explicit SD-map routes, assessing whether successive decisions support safe progress along the intended road sequence. 

## 3 ODYSSEY 

Odyssey provides a closed-loop simulation platform for evaluating long-horizon navigation along explicit routes on an SD map. Within each scene reconstructed from a 100-second driving log, the planner predicts a local driving trajectory based on camera observations and route guidance. The simulator advances the ego vehicle along the predicted trajectory and uses a neural rendering engine to generate new camera observations from its updated pose. We evaluate planner performance in terms of driving safety, route progress, adherence to the road-level route, and lane selection before intersections. 3.1 SCENARIO CURATION 

Odyssey comprises 100 driving scenarios curated from nuPlan (Karnchanachari et al., 2024). To capture diverse driving situations over extended sequences, we combine RefAV (Davidson et al., 2026)-based tagging with human review of navigation maneuvers and traffic interactions. The dis-tribution of individual tags is reported in Figure 3 (a). The selected scenarios contain multiple maneuver and interaction tags, with the number of tags per scenario shown in Figure 3 (b). The se-lected set includes 13 scenarios involving traffic lights and 47 involving pedestrians, covering both signal compliance and safety around vulnerable road users. Within this set, we prioritize scenarios with complex road layouts and sequences of naviga-tion maneuvers. Figure 3 (c) shows representative routes involving successive turns, branching roads, and lane changes. SD maps represent the road network through coarse polylines and connectivity at intersections and branches. A navigation route specifies the sequence of roads to follow. To construct each route, we match the full logged ego trajectory to OpenStreetMap (OSM) using HMM-based map matching (Newson & Krumm, 2009), yielding a single polyline from the scenario start to the destination. We manually check the matched roads and route continuity against the logged trajectory and SD map. During closed-loop simulation, we update the route segment provided to the planner according to the ego vehicle’s current po-sition. 3.3 CLOSED -L OOP SIMULATIONS 

3D G AUSSIAN SCENE RECONSTRUCTION . Odyssey reconstructs each 3D sequence using Om-niRe (Chen et al., 2025), with rigid transformations for vehicles, SMPL-based deformation (Loper et al., 2015) for pedestrians, and a learned deformation field for other actors. Traffic lights have fixed geometry and position, with time-dependent spherical-harmonic coefficients and opacity cap-turing signal changes. To stabilize road geometry, we incorporate ground modeling inspired by HUGSIM (Zhou et al., 2025), representing the road surface separately using thin Gaussian disks initialized from LiDAR. Their heights and normals follow a fixed LiDAR-derived height field as we optimize their in-plane geometry and appearance. Opacity regularization suppresses overlapping background Gaussians near the road surface. When training these scene representations over long sequences, uniform image sampling concen-trates updates in regions where the recording vehicle stops or moves slowly. We therefore apply 

coverage-aware sampling , weighting each view by aggregating inverse observation counts over its visible scene voxels. This increases sampling probabilities for views covering less-observed regions. 

RECONSTRUCTION -C ONDITIONED VISUAL REFINEMENT . Rendering from ego poses beyond the logged viewpoints can expose blur and missing detail in poorly observed regions. We reduce these artifacts using Fixer (NVIDIA, 2025b), which adapts the diffusion-based refinement of Di-fix3D+ (Wu et al., 2025) to a Cosmos backbone (NVIDIA, 2025a). Fixer refines each image in a single denoising step at a fixed timestep without adding random noise. We fine-tune it offline on paired degraded renderings and recorded images, following the data preparation strategy of Difix. During simulation, the model refines rendered observations before they are passed to the planner. 

EGO VEHICLE CONTROL . The ego vehicle tracks the planner’s trajectory at 10 Hz using a linear quadratic regulator (LQR) (Anderson & Moore, 2007). At each step, the LQR computes acceleration and steering rate that a bicycle model (Kong et al., 2015) uses to update the ego state. 3.4 TRAFFIC SIMULATION

SECTION -B ASED TRAFFIC TRIGGERING . When the ego vehicle travels faster or slower than the logged vehicle, traffic playback on the original timeline can cause it to miss recorded interactions. We therefore introduce section-based traffic triggering to align traffic activation and traffic light signal timing with ego progress in both non-reactive and reactive settings. Since traffic interactions are concentrated around intersections, we place section boundaries between intersections. As the ego vehicle approaches a section, we activate its traffic using a fixed time offset determined by the difference between the logged and simulated ego entry times. The same offset is applied to the section’s traffic light signals, which then advance through their recorded states at the original rate. We introduce SD Route Compliance , Pre-Lane Change Accuracy , Pre-Lane Change Score , and 

RouteDS to assess navigation performance. We also report Efficiency and Comfortness. 

SD R OUTE COMPLIANCE (SDC). We map-match the executed ego trajectory to assess adherence to the provided SD-map route. A rollout scores 1 if all matched road segments belong to the route and 0 otherwise, even if the vehicle stays on drivable roads. SDC is the mean score across rollouts. 

PRE -L ANE CHANGE ACCURACY (PLCA) & S CORE (PLCS). Long-horizon navigation re-quires timely lane positioning to follow the route through successive intersections. We assess this ability via PLCA and PLCS at intersections where continuing along the route requires specific ap-proach lanes. PLCA averages scores over evaluated intersections encountered across all rollouts. PLCS averages scores over all evaluation intersections along the full scenario route, counting un-encountered intersections as 0 to account for incomplete rollouts. At each stop line, the vehicle scores 1 if it crosses in a route-compatible lane and has maintained compatible lane occupancy for the preceding 10 m. A later entry scores 0.5, and crossing outside a compatible lane scores 0 (Fig. 4 (b)). 

ROUTE DS. We extend the Driving Score of Bench2Drive (Jia et al., 2024) with penalties for SD-route deviations and failed pre-lane change evaluations. These additions complement route comple-tion and penalties for at-fault collisions (Dauner et al., 2024), lane deviation, and red-light violations. For each scenario, the route completion ratio is multiplied by a cumulative penalty factor initialized to 1, with multipliers in [0 , 1] applied for infractions. Each failed pre-lane change evaluation incurs a separate penalty. We report the mean RouteDS across scenarios. ## 4 IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

4.1 SIMULATOR IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

We reconstruct the entire 100-second sequence as a single scene using OmniRe (Chen et al., 2025) and accelerating optimization with FastGS (Ren et al., 2026). Each scene contains 8,000 images captured at 10 Hz across eight cameras and is optimized for 140,000 iterations. We additionally apply per-image affine color corrections, as used in MTGS (Li et al., 2025a), to compensate for exposure variations. We fine-tune pretrained Fixer (NVIDIA, 2025b) on paired degraded renderings and corresponding recorded images, following the data preparation strategy of Difix (Wu et al., 2025). Details of training data generation and fine-tuning are provided in the Appendix. 4.2 SD-R OUTE -C ONDITIONED BASELINES 

We adapt existing planners to SD-route guidance and compare their closed-loop driving performance over long-horizon rollouts. The baselines comprise LTF (Chitta et al., 2023), DiffusionDrive (Liao et al., 2025), DrivoR (Kirby et al., 2026), SafeDrive (Kim et al., 2026), and ReCogDrive (Li et al., 2026b). All baselines predict a 4-second future trajectory sampled at 2 Hz and are trained on the NAVSIM training set (Dauner et al., 2024). At each planning step, we sample 120 route points at 1-meter intervals ahead of the ego’s projection onto the SD-route polyline. Their positions, directions, and headings are expressed in the ego frame to provide navigation guidance in place of driving commands. For vision-based planners, a linear projection and max pooling over groups of five consecutive points produce 24 route tokens. Fourier embeddings of group starting poses and learned order embeddings encode their spatial placement and sequence. Each planning decoder layer attends to these tokens as keys and values through an additional cross-attention module before its feed-forward network. For ReCogDrive, we summarize the route into a single token added to the ego-status features and supply a route-derived navigation sentence to the VLM. Model-specific adaptations are detailed in the Appendix. These comparisons mo-tivate evaluating how planners follow the intended route over successive decisions, including the driving situations resulting from their own actions. 5.4 DO OPEN -L OOP GAINS TRANSFER TO CLOSED -L OOP DRIVING ?Recent research has explored trajectory scoring and reinforcement learning as ways to improve driv-ing safety. In NAVSIM-based training, trajectory scorers learn to predict simulation-based driving scores, while reinforcement learning uses these scores as rewards. Both objectives evaluate individ-ual trajectories in an open-loop setting, without directly optimizing how the planner responds to new observations after execution. Table 5 shows the limits of this approach. For SafeDrive, IL scoring outperforms safety scoring despite similar PDMS values. Safety filtering further improves performance by selecting the highest IL-scored trajectory among candidates that pass safety checks. For ReCogDrive, GRPO improves PDMS but lowers closed-loop performance relative to IL-only training. These results suggest that optimizing individual trajectories does not ensure better continuous driving. Odyssey closes this gap by evaluating planners on the consequences of their own decisions, not on isolated trajectories. 5.5 WHY EVALUATE OVER LONG HORIZONS ?Short-horizon evaluation assesses immediate driving behavior but may not reveal whether a planner can sustain performance over successive decisions. Figure 8 (a) shows that RouteDS decreases as the evaluation horizon grows, highlighting the challenge of sustaining safe, route-following behavior over longer rollouts. Some decline is expected as infraction penalties accumulate, but the relative performance of planners also changes. DrivoR achieves higher RouteDS than SafeDrive at short horizons but falls below it as driving continues. This rank reversal shows that conclusions drawn from short rollouts may not hold over longer ones, motivating evaluation of successive decisions and their downstream consequences. 9Preprint Collision Score

## 6 FUTURE WORKS 

Surrounding vehicles can appear blurred or distorted when viewed from angles not covered by the driving logs. Using diffusion guidance during 3D Gaussian Splatting optimization could improve reconstruction in these regions. Future work could also improve the temporal consistency of diffu-sion refinement. Beyond visual quality, adversarial agents could introduce more challenging traffic interactions for safety evaluation. 

## 7 CONCLUSION 

We introduced Odyssey to evaluate whether E2E planners can sustain safe navigation under explicit route guidance. Odyssey provides SD-map routes and continuous sensor feedback in environments reconstructed from 100-second real-world driving logs. This setting supports closed-loop evaluation under a consistent navigation objective. Our experiments show that planner rankings change with the evaluation horizon and that improvements in open-loop metrics do not necessarily translate into better closed-loop driving. Explicit route guidance improves road-level adherence, but these gains do not consistently extend to lane preparation or collision avoidance. Odyssey supports research on these remaining challenges by evaluating how planners use route guidance and sensor observations to select appropriate lanes and interact safely with surrounding traffic over extended closed-loop rollouts. 10 Preprint

## A RELATED WORK 

A.1 END -TO -E ND DRIVING MODELS 

Planning-oriented E2E driving integrates perception, prediction, and planning (Hu et al., 2022; 2023), with subsequent work improving scene representation and task interaction (Jiang et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2025; Weng et al., 2024; Jia et al., 2025). Building on these representations, planners jointly predict trajectories and controls (Wu et al., 2022; Jia et al., 2023) or separate path geometry from speed prediction (Jaeger et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2025). To handle multiple feasible actions, dif-fusion models generate diverse trajectories (Liao et al., 2025), and candidate-based planners learn to score alternatives (Li et al., 2024b; Yao et al., 2026). World models further inform planning through predicted future states and action risks (Li et al., 2025b; Kim et al., 2026), and provide imagined interactions for reinforcement learning (Li et al., 2024a). Vision-language-action (VLA) models extend sensor-based planning with language understanding and reasoning. SimLingo aligns language instructions with driving actions (Renz et al., 2025), and DriveVLA-W0 incorporates future-image prediction to enrich visual learning beyond action super-vision (Li et al., 2026a). ReCogDrive and AutoDrive-R 2 further combine reasoning with reinforce-ment learning to improve driving decisions (Li et al., 2026b; Yuan et al., 2026). Odyssey adapts vision-based and VLA planners to SD-route guidance for evaluating sustained navigation. 16 Preprint A.2 EVALUATION OF E2E P LANNING 

Evaluating E2E planners requires assessing the consequences of their actions, which open-loop displacement-based metrics overlook (Caesar et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2022; 2023). NAVSIM (Dauner et al., 2024) moves beyond imitation accuracy through simulation-based driving metrics. NAVSIM v2 (Cao et al., 2025) further incorporates pre-rendered observations to approximate subsequent feed-back. Extending this feedback to continuous replanning requires observations that reflect the ego vehicle’s evolving state. Graphics-based simulators provide such observations but introduce a visual domain gap (Dosovitskiy et al., 2017; Li et al., 2022; Jia et al., 2024). Planning simulators using real logs evaluate driving behavior without sensor rendering (Caesar et al., 2021; Karnchanachari et al., 2024; Gulino et al., 2023). Neural-rendering-based simulators bring sensor feedback to recorded driving environments by re-constructing scenes and rendering observations in response to ego actions (Yang et al., 2023; Ton-derski et al., 2024; NVIDIA et al., 2025). Within this paradigm, NeuroNCAP (Ljungbergh et al., 2024) examines collision avoidance in NeRF-reconstructed scenarios, and HUGSIM (Zhou et al., 2025) provides closed-loop evaluation through 3DGS rendering and controllable traffic. However, deviations from the logged trajectory can expose poorly reconstructed regions (Zhou et al., 2024b; 2025; Hess et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025), producing artifacts that compromise the reliability of sen-sor feedback. Odyssey addresses this issue by refining 3DGS-rendered observations with diffusion to reduce artifacts at novel ego poses. A.3 DRIVING INTENTION FOR END -TO -E ND MODELS 

Navigation guidance specifies the intended path when multiple driving maneuvers are feasi-ble (Codevilla et al., 2018). Benchmarks based on real driving logs (Hu et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2023; Dauner et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2025; Ljungbergh et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026) commonly express this intent through driving commands, but this compact representation cannot uniquely distinguish consecutive branches sharing the same direction. CARLA-based bench-marks (Dosovitskiy et al., 2017), including Bench2Drive (Jia et al., 2024), provide explicit guidance through target points or route waypoints in synthetic environments. However, guidance limited to nearby targets provides insufficient route context for planning across multiple maneuvers over longer horizons. Odyssey uses OSM-derived SD-map routes to provide this guidance in reconstructed real-world environments. These routes specify the intended road sequence throughout the rollout and reduce reliance on detailed HD maps as planner inputs. Local driving decisions remain based on sensor observations.

B.1 3D G AUSSIAN RECONSTRUCTION 

RECONSTRUCTION FRAMEWORK . We build on OmniRe (Chen et al., 2025) to jointly recon-struct the static background and dynamic actors, and model traffic lights separately to reproduce signal changes. Pose noise in nuPlan logs degrades 3D Gaussian reconstruction quality, motivating pose refinement with COLMAP (Sch¨ onberger & Frahm, 2016). We keep the logged x and y coordi-nates fixed to preserve alignment with the HD map used for drivable-area evaluation, and optimize only z, roll, pitch, and yaw. To separate scene components, we generate sky and dynamic-object masks using SegFormer (Xie et al., 2021). We initialize background Gaussians by back-projecting MoGe-2 pseudo-depth (Wang et al., 2025) after excluding dynamic pixels. Rigid and deformable actors are initialized from accumulated LiDAR points, and pedestrians from SMPL templates (Loper et al., 2015). For traffic-light modeling, we obtain head masks using Mask2Former (Cheng et al., 2022) pretrained on Mapillary Vistas v2 (Neuhold et al., 2017).

Ground Gaussians are placed on this height field within the HD-map road regions; heights of unobserved road cells are filled by harmonic inter-polation from the observed cells. COVERAGE -AWARE SAMPLING . Sampling images uniformly over a long driving sequence con-centrates training updates on places where the recording vehicle stops or moves slowly. To balance spatial coverage, we back-project the LiDAR depth of each training image, excluding dynamic ob-jects, into 0.5 m voxels and count how many training views observe each voxel. The weight of an image is the mean inverse observation count of its visible voxels, normalized by the 90th percentile over all images and clipped to [0 .1, 1] . Images that mainly see less-observed regions therefore re-ceive higher weights, and frequently observed regions are revisited less often. Rather than drawing images at random, we build a sampling cycle in which each image appears a number of times pro-portional to its weight, so that every training view is used at least once per cycle. Images containing dynamic objects are given a higher minimum weight of 0.25 to preserve the temporal coverage of moving actors. Each reconstruction update uses a single image. 

TRAFFIC -L IGHT MODELING . To capture signal changes, we represent each traffic-light head as a separate deformable Gaussian node. Using head masks predicted by Mask2Former (Cheng et al., 2022) and depth estimated by MoGe-2 (Wang et al., 2025), we back-project masked pixels from calibrated views to initialize Gaussian positions and colors. Each node has a fixed installation position and orientation, and its geometry is kept fixed up to a small tolerance of 5 cm that absorbs residual calibration error. Signal changes are therefore modeled through appearance: an MLP takes Gaussian positions, time, and a learned embedding for each traffic light, and predicts temporal residuals to the spherical-harmonic coefficients and opacity. OPTIMIZATION . We jointly optimize each complete 100-second sequence as a single scene for 140k iterations, sampling one training image per iteration. We use eight cameras at 10 Hz with 1920×1080 images, starting at half resolution and switching to full resolution at iteration 1,166. To balance training across the sequence, we assign higher sampling weights to views covering less frequently observed 0.5 m spatial voxels, while every training view is still served at least once per sampling cycle. FastGS, GPU-resident caching of images and supervision signals, and a reduced update cadence for the background parameters of Gaussians reduce training overhead. We apply FastGS density control (Ren et al., 2026) to background and rigid-actor Gaussians, com-puting scores from 120 views sampled across cameras and the full sequence. To account for uneven visibility over long sequences, we normalize densification scores by each Gaussian’s visible-view count. Table 6 lists the settings. 

TRAINING OBJECTIVES . We jointly optimize appearance and geometry using a weighted sum of the losses. Our appearance objective combines RGB L1 and SSIM losses with color-correction regularization. RGB L1 compares color-corrected renderings with recorded images, and SSIM su-pervises the uncorrected renderings to preserve image structure (Lin et al., 2024). The regularization penalizes colors outside the valid range, limits frame-dependent corrections, and discourages abrupt changes between adjacent frames. To account for exposure and white-balance differences, we ad-just rendered RGB values using a learnable 3 × 4 affine transformation following MTGS (Li et al., 2025a). The first three columns transform the RGB channels, and the last column adds a per-channel offset. A shared matrix captures persistent differences between cameras, and a residual matrix for each camera and frame captures temporal variations. Our geometry losses regularize scene structure and reduce reconstruction artifacts. LiDAR depth provides additional geometric supervision, and edge-aware smoothness reduces depth noise without smoothing across image boundaries (Chen et al., 2026). Sky-opacity BCE encourages transparent sky regions, and a line-of-sight loss suppresses opacity at least 0.8 m in front of LiDAR returns (Hess et al., 2025). Opacity entropy further encourages values near zero or one. For actors, we apply bounding-box regularization to both rigid and deformable Gaussians, extending OmniRe’s regular-ization to rigid actors. We separately penalize local displacement of traffic-light Gaussians to limit deformation around their fixed installations. Table 7 summarizes the loss terms and their weights. ## C EVALUATION METRIC DETAILS 

This section defines the rollout termination criteria, pre-lane change metrics, RouteDS components, and additional driving metrics. 21 Preprint C.1 ROLLOUT TERMINATION CRITERIA 

Each rollout evaluates progress toward a designated destination. Termination criteria bound its du-ration and prevent continued simulation far from the intended route. A rollout ends under one of three conditions: • DESTINATION ARRIVAL . The ego reaches the end of the designated SD route. • TIME LIMIT . The rollout is limited to 200 s , twice the duration of the recorded ground-truth driving sequence. • SD-ROUTE DEVIATION . The rollout terminates as soon as the ego vehicle is more than 

30 m from the designated SD route. C.2 PRE -L ANE CHANGE METRICS 

We select evaluation intersections where the logged vehicle travels in route-compatible lanes throughout the 10 m before the stop line. To detect when vehicles approaching through oncom-ing lanes or off-road areas pass an evaluation intersection, we extend each stop line by 25 m at both ends and check for crossings. Crossings along these extensions, outside the original stop-line segment, are treated as failures to cross the stop line correctly. C.3 ROUTE DS C OMPONENTS 

For each scenario, we compute 

RouteDS = 100 · RC SD · PPLC · PSD · Pcol · Poff · PTL , (2) where RC SD ∈ [0 , 1] denotes route completion and the remaining factors are penalties in [0 , 1] ,defined below. We report the mean RouteDS across scenarios. • ROUTE COMPLETION . RC SD is the fraction of the designated SD route completed by the ego vehicle. After each rollout, we apply HMM map matching (Newson & Krumm, 2009) to the executed trajectory to identify the SD-route segments traversed by the ego. If the ego deviates from the route, we determine where the ego left the route by repeating HMM matching from the start, extending the evaluated trajectory by 1 m each time. We count progress only up to the last point before the matching indicates that the ego is off route. • PRE -L ANE CHANGE . For each pre-lane change evaluation, we multiply PPLC by 0.7 for a score of 0 and by 0.9 for a score of 0.5. Unencountered evaluation stop lines incur no pre-lane change penalty. • SD-ROUTE COMPLIANCE . We set PSD = 0 if the rollout terminates because the ego exceeds the route-distance threshold defined above, or if post-rollout HMM matching iden-tifies travel outside the designated route. If neither condition holds and matching succeeds, 

PSD = 1 . Near the start and end of a rollout, limited trajectory coverage around a junction can make connected roads difficult to distinguish. We allow an 8.5 m tolerance for such boundary matches, excluding travel opposite to the route direction. • COLLISION PENALTY . We penalize at-fault collisions with pedestrians, vehicles, and bi-cycles using nuPlan-style criteria (Caesar et al., 2021), excluding stationary-ego and active rear collisions. Front collisions and collisions with stationary agents count, with lateral col-lisions counted only when the ego spans multiple lanes or extends outside drivable areas. For each counted collision, we multiply Pcol by 0.5 for pedestrians and 0.6 for vehicles or bicycles (Jia et al., 2024). If no collision is counted, Pcol = 1 . The same object is counted again only after contact breaks and either displacement exceeds 5 m or elapsed time exceeds 5 s .• OFF -ROAD PENALTY . We compute Poff = 1 − Doff /D total , where Dtotal is the total trav-eled distance and Doff is the distance driven outside drivable areas or against the direction of traffic. Driving-direction violations are not counted within intersections. 22 Preprint • TRAFFIC -LIGHT PENALTY . Each red-light violation multiplies PTL by 0.7. A violation occurs when the ego occupies an intersection roadblock whose traffic signal is red. Each violation is counted once, even if it persists across multiple evaluation steps. C.4 ADDITIONAL DRIVING METRICS 

We adapt Efficiency and Comfortness metrics from Bench2Drive (Jia et al., 2024) to our simulation setting, as detailed below. 

EFFICIENCY . Efficiency measures whether the ego maintains driving speed relative to other traf-fic. We compare its speed with vehicles that exceed 1 m /s at least once during the rollout. At each evaluation step containing these vehicles, we compute their average speed. Efficiency is 100 times the sum of ego speeds divided by the sum of these average vehicle speeds over the same steps. A score of 100 indicates equal average speeds.

## D SD-R OUTE CONDITIONING OF BASELINE PLANNERS 

This section details the model-specific adaptations of the baselines in Section 4.2. We keep each original architecture and training recipe, and add only the route encoder and the modules that read it. We first transform the route points into the ego frame, then encode their positions relative to the first route point. Headings in the ego frame are encoded by their cosine and sine. The added cross-attention has its own normalization and residual connection. Unavailable route points are masked, and a learned token represents the absence of route guidance when all route points are masked. • LTF. We use the camera-only latent variant. The trajectory query attends to the route in all three decoder layers, while the 30 agent queries do not. The ego status keeps only velocity and acceleration. Trained for 100 epochs. • DiffusionDrive. The 20 anchor queries attend to the route in both decoder layers after the ego-query cross-attention. The route therefore conditions each of the two denoising steps. We use the camera-only latent variant. Trained for 100 epochs. • DrivoR. The 64 proposal tokens attend to the route in all four refinement blocks. The ego history encoding is reduced from 11 to 7 channels (pose, velocity, and acceleration). Trained for 25 epochs. • SafeDrive. Every layer of both planning decoders attends to the same route tokens from a shared encoder. The ego status is reduced from 8 to 4 channels. We use the camera-only variant with pedestrian detection. • ReCogDrive. A two-stage point encoder with max pooling compresses the 120 route points into one token. A Fourier embedding of the route start is added to this token, and the result is added to the ego-status embedding of the diffusion planner. The navigation line of the VLM prompt is generated from the same route, naming the next maneuver with its distance and the one after it (e.g., “turn left in 30 m, then follow the road”). When the remaining route is too short to describe, the driving command is used instead.
