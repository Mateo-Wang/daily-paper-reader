# Preference-Adaptive Control in Autonomous Driving 

Zhongyu Mo 1, Shanting Wang 1, Huaijin Hu 1, and Andreas A. Malikopoulos 2, Senior Member, IEEE 

Abstract — In this paper, we present a preference-adaptive receding-horizon control framework for autonomous driving that accounts for passenger preferences and motion-sickness susceptibility. We formulate a finite-horizon optimal control problem with adaptive weights for speed, acceleration comfort, and motion sickness, while maintaining a fixed weight for collision risk. We predict motion sickness online using an individualized model on the Motion Illness Symptoms Classifi-cation (MISC) scale and update the preference weights offline from emotion-derived pairwise comparisons using Bayesian inference. A deterministic safety supervisor checks the planned trajectory and modifies the control command when necessary. We evaluate the framework using three simulated passenger profiles under three motion-sickness susceptibility levels. The learned weights yield distinct closed-loop behaviors, and the mean evaluation emotion score improves in seven of nine scenarios. No collisions occur in the full-method experiments, while the safety supervisor intervenes in 1.621% of the learning frames. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Autonomous vehicles can improve safety and mobility, but passengers may differ substantially in their desired tradeoff between speed and comfort and in their susceptibility to motion sickness. Thus, a fixed control objective may not adequately represent an individual passenger’s preferences. Personalized vehicle control has been studied using in-verse reinforcement learning [1], trajectory comparisons and preference learning [2], [3], and Bayesian optimization for controller adaptation [4], [5]. These approaches provide mechanisms for adapting vehicle behavior, but they do not directly account for individualized, history-dependent motion sickness when learning passenger preferences. Motion sickness depends on the accumulated interaction between vestibular and visual information and cannot, in general, be represented by instantaneous acceleration alone. Sensory-conflict models have been developed for motion-sickness prediction [6], [7], while Kotian et al. [8], [9] introduced individualized accumulation models based on the framework of Oman [10]. Recent studies have further inves-tigated motion-sickness prediction and mitigation for vehicle passengers [11], [12], [13], [14]. However, these models do not address how to infer an individual passenger’s tradeoff among motion sickness, speed, and acceleration comfort

We address this gap with a preference-adaptive receding-horizon control framework that learns the relative importance assigned by a passenger to speed, acceleration comfort, and motion sickness. Because motion sickness evolves through history-dependent accumulation, we incorporate its indi-vidualized prediction directly into finite-horizon planning. The passenger-dependent weights are updated offline from emotion-derived pairwise comparisons using Bayesian infer-ence and Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling as in [2]. During online operation, an independent deterministic safety supervisor modifies the planned command when short-horizon collision or road-boundary checks fail [16], [17], [18], [19]. The contributions of this paper are the following. First, we formulate a passenger-adaptive finite-horizon optimal control problem that incorporates individualized, history-dependent motion-sickness prediction with speed and acceleration com-fort. Second, we adapt pairwise Bayesian preference learning to update the passenger-dependent weights from realized closed-loop episodes and use these weights directly in the receding-horizon controller. Finally, we evaluate the frame-work for three simulated passenger profiles under three levels of motion-sickness susceptibility. The results yield distinct passenger-dependent closed-loop behaviors and improve the mean evaluation emotion in seven of the nine scenarios while exposing limitations associated with threshold-sensitive pref-erences. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section II, we present the vehicle and passenger models and formulate the finite-horizon optimal control problem. In Section III, we present the preference-learning framework, online planning approach, and safety supervisor. In Sec-tion IV, we present the simulation setup and results. C. Receding-Horizon Implementation 

At each control step, we solve Problem 1 using Sequential Least-Squares Programming (SLSQP) [21]. We propagate the ego and passenger states using (1) and (7), respectively, and predict surrounding vehicles using the Intelligent Driver Model (IDM). 

Assumption 6. The passenger-specific vision policy is known to the controller. This assumption avoids introducing a separate model of future gaze behavior. In our simulations, the known vision policy is evaluated at each predicted step using the corre-sponding predicted MISC state. We enforce (3)–(6) throughout the prediction horizon and execute only the first control command. If SLSQP does not return a feasible solution, we use a fallback sequence with zero longitudinal acceleration and steering toward the global-forward heading. Otherwise, we shift the previous solution and append a neutral command to initialize the next optimization. Because this fallback is generated directly rather than by constrained optimization, satisfaction of the nominal planning constraints is not guaranteed. In either case, the safety supervisor evaluates the planned trajectory before execution.

A. Learned Passenger Preferences 

We first examine how the learned preference weights vary across passenger profiles and susceptibility levels. Table II reports the final preference weights. The results show distinct learned preference vectors across the three passenger profiles and susceptibility settings. For Alice, the learned preference vector becomes strongly speed-oriented in all three susceptibility settings, with wv ∈

[0 .865 , 0.983] . This behavior is consistent with the construc-tion of Alice’s emotion signal in (19), which depends only on vehicle speed. The learned controller consequently assigns only a small fraction of the preference weight to motion sickness and acceleration. For Bob, the learned weights exhibit a different pattern. In the low- and medium-susceptibility cases, the largest weight is assigned to motion sickness, whereas in the high-susceptibility case most of the weight is distributed be-tween speed and acceleration. These weights should not be interpreted as estimates of the coefficients in (20). The emotion model uses linear speed and absolute accelera-tion, whereas the controller uses a squared speed deficit, normalized squared acceleration, and the nonlinear motion-sickness cost in (11). In addition, acceleration influences both the instantaneous acceleration cost and the subsequent evolution of motion sickness. Thus, the learned weights represent the relative contribution of the controller features in explaining the observed episode comparisons rather than a direct recovery of the coefficients in Bob’s simulated emotion function. For Zoey, increasing susceptibility changes the learned allocation primarily from speed toward acceleration. In par-ticular, wv decreases from 0.874 in the low-susceptibility case to 0.610 in the high-susceptibility case, while wa

increases from 0.032 to 0.331 . The explicit weight assigned to the MISC cost remains relatively small. This result illus-trates an important feature of the learning problem: because vehicle acceleration also affects the subsequent MISC state, correlated trajectory features can produce different weight allocations that explain similar episode-level preferences. The weight trajectories also exhibit empirical stabilization over the 32 preference updates. Across the last three updates, the largest scenario-wise mean ℓ1 change in the prefer-ence vector is 0.030 for Bob under high susceptibility. We emphasize that this observation indicates limited late-stage variation in the reported runs rather than convergence of the learning procedure in a theoretical sense. A post-hoc four-chain diagnostic run using the same posterior and proposal settings yields a worst-case bR = 1 .0048 and a minimum effective sample size of 492 , providing additional evidence that the posterior sampling procedure mixed adequately in the reported experiments. 

B. Closed-Loop Performance 

We next examine how the learned preferences change closed-loop behavior relative to the initial uniform weights. Table III compares the initial and final controllers using the fixed evaluation seeds. The mean emotion improves in seven of the nine passenger–susceptibility scenarios. However, the seed-level results show that an improvement in the four-episode mean does not necessarily imply improvement in every evaluation episode. For Alice, the final controller increases the mean speed in all three susceptibility settings. This increase is accompanied by higher mean MISC scores and RMS acceleration, which follows directly from the speed-oriented preference signal in (19). For example, in the low-susceptibility case, the mean speed increases from 17 .34 to 18 .81 m/s, while the mean MISC score increases from 0.244 to 0.388 . The seed-level results also reveal substantial variability: for Alice-low and Alice-medium, only one of the four evaluation seeds improves in emotion, but the corresponding increase is sufficiently large to yield a positive four-seed mean. For Bob-low, the learned controller reduces the mean MISC score from 0.254 to 0.229 while maintaining approxi-mately the same mean speed, resulting in a small increase in mean emotion. For Bob-high, the controller accepts increases in both MISC and acceleration in exchange for a higher mean speed, and the mean emotion increases from 13 .781 to 

14 .089 . Bob-medium exhibits the opposite outcome: speed, MISC, and RMS acceleration all decrease, but mean emotion decreases from 15 .846 to 15 .095 . In this case, one evaluation seed incurs an emotion decrease of approximately 4.3, which outweighs the improvements observed in the other three seeds. For Zoey-low and Zoey-medium, the learned controller increases both mean speed and mean emotion while the MISC state remains below the noticeable-symptom threshold in the evaluation episodes. The high-susceptibility case ex-poses a limitation of the learned smooth weighted objective. Although the mean speed increases from 18 .64 to 18 .89 m/s, one of the four evaluation episodes crosses the threshold 

M = 1 , reaching a maximum MISC value of 1.079 . During this episode, M ≥ 1 for 7.75 s, which activates the large penalty in (22) and produces a mean emotion of −135 .207 .Consequently, the four-seed mean emotion decreases from 

0.746 to −33 .250 . The maximum MISC values in the other three final evaluation episodes are 0.833 , 0.864 , and 0.750 .This result highlights the distinction between learn-ing a smooth weighted surrogate objective and satisfy-ing a threshold-sensitive passenger preference. C. Safety and Constraint Satisfaction 

We then examine whether safety constraints are preserved. No physical collision is observed in the 2304 learning episodes or the 1188 evaluation episodes of the full method. The safety supervisor intervenes on 1.621% of all learning control steps. For the final learned controllers, the mean intervention rate across the four evaluation episodes ranges from 0.625% to 4.271% , as reported in Table IV. The supervisor reaches the best-effort branch in (18), where no admissible candidate is found in the finite candidate set, on 34 learning control steps and 15 evaluation control steps. Thus, the absence of observed collisions should be interpreted as an empirical result of these simulations and not as a formal guarantee of collision avoidance. The logged TTC is calculated from the instantaneous relative velocity and collision ellipse at the pre-control state, whereas the planner evaluates collision risk along its pre-dicted trajectory. Therefore, the reported minimum TTC can fall below the 5 s penalty threshold without violating a hard TTC constraint, since Problem 1 does not impose such a constraint. Among the final-weight evaluations, Alice-high has the smallest mean episode-minimum TTC of 1.550 s. The simulations also expose a limitation of the current fall-back and supervisor implementation. D. Feature Ablation 

We next investigate whether the acceleration and motion-sickness features provide distinct information to the preference-learning procedure. We repeat the complete 256 -episode learning protocol and fixed-seed evaluation with two reduced preference models. The VA variant retains only speed and acceleration, while the VM variant retains only speed and MISC. In each reduced model, the two preference weights are initialized uniformly at 0.5. The full method retains all three preference features. Removing the acceleration feature in the VM variant increases the aggregate RMS acceleration to 0.719 m/s 2,compared with 0.561 m/s 2 for the full model and 0.554 m/s 2

for VA. This result indicates that the explicit acceleration feature influences the smoothness of the resulting closed-loop trajectories. Removing the MISC feature in the VA variant results in two motion-sickness threshold crossings among the 12 

Zoey evaluation episodes, compared with one crossing for the full model and none for VM. Interestingly, VA avoids threshold crossing in all four Zoey-high evaluation episodes. Thus, acceleration regulation can indirectly reduce motion-sickness accumulation in some realizations, but the results do not support treating acceleration as a consistent replacement for the explicit MISC feature. The ablation results also reveal a limitation of the current learning formulation. The full three-feature model achieves the highest four-seed mean emotion in only one of the nine scenarios, while VA and VM each do so in four scenarios. Therefore, the results do not show that adding all three features necessarily improves the learned pref-erence objective. Rather, they show that acceleration and MISC have distinct effects on closed-loop behavior. Since acceleration also influences subsequent motion sickness, the corresponding features are correlated over an episode, and different preference weight allocations can explain similar pairwise comparisons. More data, richer preference feedback, or additional structure in the preference model may be needed to improve identifiability. All final-weight evaluation episodes for both reduced mod-els are collision-free. The VM variant recorded one collision among its 2304 learning episodes. VI. C ONCLUDING REMARKS 

In this paper, we presented a preference-adaptive receding-horizon control framework that combines individualized motion-sickness prediction with emotion-derived Bayesian preference learning and independent safety supervision. The learned weights produce distinct passenger-dependent closed-loop behaviors across the nine simulated passenger– susceptibility scenarios, with mean evaluation emotion im-proving in seven scenarios and no collisions observed for the full method. The results also expose limitations of the current formu-lation: threshold-sensitive preferences can be poorly rep-resented by a weighted objective, and fallback or safety-supervisor interventions can violate nominal motion con-straints. Future work will investigate human-subject feed-back, uncertainty in motion-sickness estimation, and pref-erence models that better represent threshold-dependent and time-varying passenger responses.
