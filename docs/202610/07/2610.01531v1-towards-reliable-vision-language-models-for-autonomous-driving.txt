Abstract 

Vision-Language models (VLMs) are increasingly being explored in autonomous driving for tasks such as scene understanding, driving reasoning, decision-making, and end-to-end driving. As their role becomes more prominent, ensuring their robustness and reliability is increasingly important. In real-world conditions, visual inputs may be degraded by sensor imperfections and environmental conditions, potentially affecting both model predictions and their associated confidence. Such degradation is especially concerning in autonomous driving, where safety-critical decisions require models to make accurate predictions and recognize when their predictions may be unreliable. In this work, we evaluate five VLMs (Qwen3.5-9B, Gemma4-E4B, LLaVA-OneVision-7B, DriveFusion/DriveFusionQA-4B, and NVIDIA Alpamayo-1.5-10B) across four driving-related QA datasets with dif-ferent visual input settings, including single-frame, multi-view, multi-frame, and monocular inputs. Our results show that the effects of visual corruption vary across models, datasets, and input settings, with changes in accuracy and confidence reliability and also differing across conditions. We then apply Visual Evidence Augmentation ( VEA ), a recent inference-time method to examine whether it can improve model reliability under degraded visual conditions. We find that VEA 

improves performance for some models and datasets, although the gains are not consistent across all settings. 

1 Introduction 

Autonomous driving is an emerging area in intelligent transportation systems that offers safer mobility, improved traffic efficiency, and new forms of urban transport [1]. Traditionally, a modular pipeline architecture is followed, in which perception, prediction, planning, and control are separated into distinct subsystems [2–4]. However, these modular systems are prone to error accumulation between interfaces and struggle to achieve global optimization in highly interactive traffic environments [5]. In response, end-to-end autonomous driving (E2E-AD) has attracted attention, in which multimodal sensory inputs are directly mapped to driving actions via unified differentiable models [6–9]. It reduces system coupling and allows combined optimization of perception, decision-making, and control [10]. Despite the progress, the current E2E approaches still have gaps in handling long-tail and safety-critical situations, where there is a need for sparse supervision and the need high-level reasoning [11].

With visual and language understanding, VLMs offer a promising direction for many parts of E2E-AD [12–17]. They can help with tasks like perception, scene understanding, and context-aware decision-making. However, their reliability in real-world driving is still unclear. Unlike benchmark datasets, real-world driving contains many rare and difficult cases that may not be well represented in large-scale training data. For instance, in real world driving, camera inputs are rarely ideal and are affected by environmental and sensor-related challenges such as illumination changes, adverse weather etc. These perturbations/corruptions can affect not only prediction accuracy but also model confidence and calibration, which are important in safety-critical settings. As an example, Figure 1 shows how Gemma4-E4B’s response change when the input is perturbed compared with the clean input. 

Figure 1: Gemma4-E4B responses to clean and perturbed inputs. To examine the camera perturbation effects in autonomous driving, we evaluate recent vision-language models with 10B parameters or fewer (considering the importance of inference latency and computational cost for deployment). Our evaluation includes Gemma4-4B [18], LLaVA-OV-8B [19], Qwen3.5-9B [20] , DriveFusionQA-4B [21] , and NVIDIA Alpamayo-1.5-10B [22]. DriveFusionQA-4B is a driving-specific VLM, while Alpamayo-1.5 is a driving-specific vision-language-action model with visual question-answering capabilities and is included because of its production-oriented design. We evaluate these models on four autonomous driving VQA benchmarks, namely DrivingVQA [23] , STRIDE-QA Bench [24] , NuScenes-QA-mini [25, 26, 4] , and Open Spatial Reasoning [27] . These benchmarks differ in both the reasoning tasks they cover and the way visual information is presented, including single images, consecutive frames, and multi-view camera inputs. This diversity allows us to examine whether the effect of visual corruption is consistent across different driving tasks and input settings, rather than being specific to one benchmark or one data format. To capture different sources of visual degradation, we consider four common camera perturbations, namely glare, fog, motion blur, and lens occlusion. Each one represents a broader category of real-world visual degradation. Glare represents illumination changes, fog represents adverse weather, motion blur represents sensor and motion-related effects, and lens occlusion represents partial visibil-ity. This provides coverage of different corruption types while keeping the evaluation focused and controlled. We further investigate if VEA [28], an inference-time attention-based visual enhancement method can transfer to autonomous driving and improve model performance under corrupted inputs. This leads to the following research questions. 

Research Questions. (1) How do real-world visual corruptions affect the accuracy and calibration of vision-language models across different autonomous driving tasks and visual input settings? (2) To what extent can inference-time visual enhancement improve model robustness under real-world visual perturbations? 

2 Related Work 

Corruption Robustness in VLMs The corruptions in visual inputs presents a significant challenge to the reliability of VLM-based autonomous driving systems. DriveBench [29] was one of the first 2benchmarks specifically designed examining this problem, assessing VLMs under 15 different types of corruption across perception, prediction, planning, and behaviour tasks and showing that these systems have a considerable dependence on language priors when the visual input is degraded. But there has been less investigation into robustness in more diverse spatial, temporal, and complex reasoning tasks. USB [30] takes this approach further by applying it to unstructured driving situations with 20 different perturbations involving illumination, weather, occlusion, and sensor reliability, while at the same time evaluating both decision accuracy and reasoning quality. Other recent work has focused on naturally occurring adverse conditions: AWDB [31] looks at video-based scene understanding in winter driving situations, while ObsDriveBench [32] assesses multimodal reasoning using camera, LiDAR, and radar in real-world conditions such as fog, rain, snow, and low light. Yet despite this advancement, current robustness benchmarks generally focus on performance degradation, while uncertainty estimation and calibration remain largely underexplored. 

Inference-Time Methods for Improving Visual Grounding Several approaches have been pro-posed to improve how VLMs use visual information at inference time. Some methods modify the decoding process to improve visual grounding and reduce the influence of language priors, such as VCD [33] and OPERA [34]. Other methods improve visual evidence utilization through prompting or intermediate reasoning. For example, CGR [35] first extracts detailed visual information and then uses this intermediate representation to generate the final answer. A growing line of work instead refines the visual input using signals from the model itself. VAR [35] uses attention scores from the final layer to create a binary mask that highlights salient image regions, while AGLA [36] uses GradCAM-based signals to mask irrelevant regions and combines predictions from the original and masked images. VEA [28] follows training-free direction by using attention-based visual enhance-ment to improve the use of relevant image evidence during inference. Other approaches operate on internal representations, such as VTI [37] and Activation Steering Decoding [38], while fine-tuning methods such as HA-DPO [39] improve visual grounding through additional training. 

3 Experimental Setup 

In this section, we describe the datasets and models, evaluation metrics, and visual corruptions used in our experiments. 

3.1 Datasets and Models Datasets 

We evaluate the models on four driving VQA datasets, DrivingVQA [23], NuScenes-QA-mini de-rived from NuScenes-QA [25], STRIDE-QA Bench [24], and Open Spatial Reasoning (OSR) [27]. DrivingVQA contains 3,931 multiple-choice questions derived from challenging real-world scenarios in French driving theory tests, with two to four answer options per question. NuScenes-QA is a multi-camera driving VQA dataset collected in Boston and Singapore, with questions covering existence, counting, object identification, object status, and comparison. We use NuScenes-QA-mini and formulate QA as open-ended text generation, rather than classification over the predefined answer set used in the original NuScenes-QA evaluation. STRIDE-QA Bench focuses on numerical and spatial reasoning in driving scenes, including distance and count estimation. OSR is a challenging monocular 3D spatial-reasoning benchmark containing 50 multiple-choice questions. Although small, we include OSR as a targeted diagnostic benchmark because it directly probes monocular 3D spatial reasoning that is less explicitly evaluated by the larger datasets. For evaluation, we use 1,000 samples from DrivingVQA and STRIDE-QA Bench, 1,117 samples from NuScenes-QA-mini, and all 50 OSR examples. 

Models 

We evaluate five models including general-purpose and driving-specific vision-language architectures. The general-purpose models include Qwen3.5-9B , Gemma4-E4B , and LLaVA-OneVision-7B ,while DriveFusionQA-4B is specifically fine-tuned for visual question answering and reasoning in autonomous-driving scenarios. We additionally evaluate NVIDIA Alpamayo-1.5-10B , a driving-specific vision-language-action model designed for autonomous driving. Although primarily devel-oped for reasoning and trajectory prediction, Alpamayo-1.5 also explicitly supports visual question 3answering through its generate_text interface, allowing it to be evaluated under the same question-answering setting. Its inclusion examine whether the observed robustness and intervention effects extend beyond conventional VLMs to models explicitly optimized for autonomous-driving reasoning. 

3.2 Evaluation Protocol and Metrics Dataset-specific evaluation 

The evaluation protocol follows the answer format of each benchmark. DrivingVQA and Open Spatial Reasoning (OSR) use multiple-choice questions and are evaluated using exact-match accuracy. For NuScenes-QA-mini , which contains open-ended answers, we additionally use GPT-4 judge to account for semantically correct responses that may differ from the reference answer in wording. For STRIDE-QA Bench, we follow the benchmark’s original localization criterion. A prediction is considered correct when the distance error is within ±25% of the ground-truth value and the heading error is within ±10 ◦ [24]. 

Metrics 

We evaluate model performance using Accuracy , AUROC , Expected Calibration Error (ECE) 

and the Brier score . Accuracy measures task performance, while AUROC evaluates how well uncertainty distinguishes correct from incorrect predictions. For AUROC, we use VAUQ (Vision-Aware Uncertainty Quantification) [40], a training-free uncertainty score that combines predictive entropy with an Image-Information Score measuring the contribution of salient visual evidence to the prediction. Unlike entropy alone, which can be strongly influenced by language priors, VAUQ accounts for whether model confidence is supported by relevant image regions, making it well suited for visually grounded driving VQA. We further assess calibration using ECE and the Brier score. ECE measures the gap between predicted confidence and empirical accuracy, while the Brier score evaluates the overall quality of probabilistic predictions by penalizing both overconfident errors and poorly calibrated confidence estimates, with lower values indicating better calibration.

Following the Unstructured Scene Benchmark (USB) [30], corruptions are applied independently using a fixed random seed, with severity parameters sampled per image from predefined ranges.

On NuScenes-QA-mini, which relies more on object-level, counting, directional, and relational reasoning, the effect of corruption is stronger. Qwen3.5-9B, Gemma4-E4B, and LLaVA-OV-7B retain above-chance AUROC VAUQ under several corruptions, suggesting that some visual information is still being used, although the drop from the clean setting shows that this signal becomes less reliable. DriveFusionQA-4B shows a different pattern. Its accuracy is slightly higher under the corrupted conditions and its ECE decreases, while AUROC VAUQ consistently drops. This suggests that the small gain in task accuracy does not reflect better visually grounded reliability. Compared with [29] and [30], which show that VLMs can still give plausible answers when the visual input is degraded, our results suggest that models can still make some use of the corrupted visual input. However, their confidence does not always reflect when this visual evidence leads to a correct prediction. AUROC VAUQ can decrease even when accuracy changes only slightly, showing that stable task performance does not necessarily mean reliable visually grounded reasoning. We also find that no model performs consistently well across accuracy, calibration, and AUROC VAUQ . Some models are better calibrated but have weak discrimination between correct and incorrect predictions, while others show stronger discrimination but poor calibration. 4.2 Visual Evidence Enhancement 

Table 3 summarizes the effect of VEA on accuracy across all four datasets. We use these results to examine whether VEA helps on clean inputs and whether any gains remain under visual corruptions. Overall, its effect depends on the model and task. In some models, improvements on clean inputs are maintained under perturbations, as with Qwen3.5-9B on DrivingVQA and STRIDE-QA Bench and Alpamayo-1.5-10B on DrivingVQA and NuScenes-QA-mini. When VEA is not helpful on clean inputs, the same limitation often remains under corruption, as seen for LLaVA-OV-7B on NuScenes-QA-mini and Alpamayo-1.5-10B on STRIDE-QA Bench. This suggests that VEA can remain effective under degraded visual inputs, but not consistently across all model and dataset combinations. STRIDE-QA Bench also shows an important limitation. VEA re-weights spatial evidence but does not add temporal motion information, so it cannot directly address reasoning that depends strongly on temporal cues. This explains why Alpamayo-1.5-10B, despite benefiting strongly on DrivingVQA and NuScenes-QA-mini and mostly improving on OSR, consistently loses accuracy on STRIDE-QA Bench. 5 Conclusion 

In this work, we evaluated general purpose and driving specialized VLMs under clean and corrupted visual conditions using accuracy and confidence. We found that model performance and confidence reliability do not always change in the same way, and no model performs consistently well across accuracy, calibration, and AUROC. We also evaluated VEA as an inference time method and found that it improves performance for some model and dataset combinations, with many of these gains remaining under corruption. However, its effect is task and model dependent, and improved accuracy does not always come with more reliable confidence.

A Supplementary Results 

For STRIDE-QA Bench, Table 4 shows the lowest overall accuracy among the four datasets, which reflects the difficulty of its spatiotemporal reasoning setting. Under corruption, the changes are mixed and should not be interpreted as clear gains or losses from a single metric. Qwen3.5-9B and DriveFusionQA-4B often show lower AUROC VAUQ , while Gemma4-E4B and Alpamayo-1.5-10B sometimes show better ECE or Brier despite limited or lower accuracy. LLaVA-OV-7B remains below chance in AUROC VAUQ across all conditions, suggesting that its confidence is not reliably aligned with visually grounded correctness in this setting. For OSR, Table 5 also shows strong disagreement between accuracy, AUROC VAUQ , and calibra-tion under corruption. Some conditions produce higher accuracy or lower calibration error, while AUROC VAUQ moves in the opposite direction, showing that these changes do not necessarily reflect more reliable reasoning. Since OSR contains only 50 samples, the condition level differences are particularly noisy and are best treated as supporting evidence rather than firm conclusions. Across NuScenes-QA-mini Figure 3, STRIDE-QA Bench Figure 5, and OSR Figure 4, VEA does not produce a uniform change in reliability. Its effect depends on the model and task, and gains in accuracy or calibration are not always matched by better AUROC VAUQ . B VEA Computation Time 

We also measure the additional evidence extraction time introduced by VEA , as shown in Figure 6. For DrivingVQA, NuScenes-QA-mini, and OSR, the added time is generally below one second across models. The overhead is higher on STRIDE-QA Bench, reaching about 1.3 seconds for Qwen3.5-9B and 3 seconds for DriveFusionQA-4B. This shows that VEA can add only a small cost in many settings, but the cost is not consistent across models and tasks. For real time deployment, this additional latency is important because VEA is added on top of the original model inference time. Therefore, while the current results show that VEA can improve performance and reliability in several settings, reducing its computation cost would also be necessary for applications with strict real time requirements.
