# Optimal Control with Learned Critics under Unmodeled State Dependencies 

Philipp Schoch, Markus Ryll 

Autonomous Aerial Systems Lab Technical University of Munich 

{philipp.schoch,markus.ryll }@tum.de 

Abstract: Model Predictive Control (MPC) provides a structured and constraint-aware mechanism for decision-making, but its reliance on optimization-friendly analytical dynamics models limits its use in tasks with contacts and other hard-to-model state dependencies. Model-free reinforcement learning avoids explicit modeling assumptions but typically requires large amounts of interaction data. We present a learning-based MPC framework that combines the data efficiency and structure of local model-based planning with learned components that compen-sate for incomplete dynamics and finite-horizon myopia. The method augments a nominal analytical model with a residual dynamics network that learns miss-ing state-dependent effects from data and combines the resulting planner with a learned action-value critic that injects long-horizon MDP structure into the local iLQR optimization. To make this practical at reinforcement-learning scale, we develop a GPU-accelerated batched iLQR solver that evaluates learned dynam-ics and critic networks inside the optimal-control loop and solves thousands of trajectory-optimization problems in parallel. The complete system is integrated into a robotics simulator, enabling scalable model-based reinforcement learning under incomplete dynamics. Experiments on biased and incompletely modeled control tasks show that the approach improves closed-loop control performance while preserving the model-based structure needed for efficient constrained tra-jectory optimization. 

Keywords: Model Predictive Control, Model-Based Reinforcement Learning, iLQR, Value Function Approximation 

# 1 Introduction 

Computing optimal control policies for agents acting in stochastic environments is a central chal-lenge in sequential decision making. Formally, this amounts to solving a Markov Decision Process (MDP) [1]: finding a policy that maximizes expected cumulative reward over potentially infinite horizons. The field has produced a rich landscape of approaches, broadly organized into trajec-tory optimization, model-free reinforcement learning (RL), and model-based reinforcement learning methods [2]. Model Predictive Control (MPC) performs trajectory optimization in a receding-horizon fashion, enabling precise control and explicit satisfaction of state and input constraints. However, MPC re-quires an optimization-friendly analytical dynamics model, often difficult or impossible to derive for systems involving contacts or other highly nonlinear effects, and breaks down if relevant state vari-ables remain unmodeled. Contact-rich tasks are a representative case, because making and breaking contact introduces discontinuous phase boundaries over which gradient information can become un-informative [3], while classical multi-phase or mixed-integer formulations lead to large nonlinear programs that are brittle and rarely compatible with real-time replanning [4]. A second limitation is the finite horizon itself. MPC applies a finite-horizon surrogate to an infinite-horizon MDP, so 

10th Conference on Robot Learning (CoRL 2026), Austin TX, USA. arXiv:2610.05359v1 [cs.RO] 4 Oct 2026 a horizon that is too short can make the controller greedy, trap it in local minima, or prevent task completion when intermediate subgoals must be reached before the final objective becomes attain-able [5]. A third limitation is that standard MPC formulations operate on explicit state variables and analytical models, making it nontrivial to use high-dimensional sensor observations such as rgb or depth images directly inside the planner. Model-free RL sidesteps these limitations: learned policies are cheap at inference and can consume high-dimensional observations. However, model-free methods are notoriously sample-inefficient, scale poorly with state-space dimensionality, and struggle with long-horizon tasks where reward-sparsity impedes exploration [1, 6]. Model-based RL promises to combine the data efficiency of planning with the flexibility of learn-ing [2]. A compelling paradigm in this space is to use MPC as a critic: the planner provides a structured, locally accurate estimate of the action-value function, which can be used to bootstrap a global Q-approximator which after training supplies long-horizon guidance back to the MPC, al-lowing even to correct for mismatches in the dynamics model. [7] demonstrates this in the setting of gradient-free sampling-based control. A key remaining challenge is that MPC critics still require an accurate dynamics model, a requirement that fails whenever relevant state variables lack a tractable analytical description, e.g. object contact dynamics in manipulation. In this work, we develop a learning-augmented constrained MPC framework that embeds two learned components directly into the planner. A residual dynamics model augments the nominal analytical model and exposes otherwise unmodeled state dependencies to optimization, while a learned critic injects global MDP information into the finite-horizon objective to reduce myopia and improve guidance under multi-phase cost-structure; unlike in sampling-based MPC [7], the critic enters the Riccati recursion through its first- and second-order derivatives. Following the DDP formulation, we realize this approach with a GPU-accelerated batched ALM-iLQR solver that inte-grates both learned components inside the optimal-control loop and scales to thousands of parallel environments, enabling efficient model-based reinforcement learning. 

Core contributions: (i) a constrained MPC formulation that combines residual dynamics learning with a learned critic, and (ii) a learning-based DDP-style solver that makes this formulation practical for model-based reinforcement-learning, scaling to thousands of parallel environments.

# 5 Results 

We evaluate the proposed framework in four complementary experiments. We begin with a runtime benchmark to verify our solver is amenable to the large-throughput requirement of reinforcement learning. We test the solver on randomly generated optimal-control problems to characterize how our implementation scales with batch size, hardware, and the method of derivative computation. Next, we isolate the effect of the critic blend on a point-mass task, where the optimal critic is available in closed form. We then study a quadrotor task with a biased dynamics model and an unmodeled suspended payload, which tests whether joint residual-dynamics learning and critic learning can compensate for model bias, capture missing state couplings, and remain compatible with quaternion kinematics. Finally, we consider Franka cube stacking as a contact-rich manipulation task to assess whether the framework can plan over incompletely modeled contact interactions and benefit from visual and haptic information for grasp detection. 

Speed Benchmarking. The experimental setup and detailed timing curves are given in Ap-pendix C. The main result is that GPU execution becomes decisive once learned dynamics and critic models are part of the solve: on the tested hardware, batched GPU execution is roughly one to two orders of magnitude faster than CPU execution over the relevant batch-size range. In the purely non-learning setting, CPU execution is competitive only for the smallest batches, after which GPU execution overtakes it as batching exposes more parallel work. In this experiment, a quadrotor starts in a random pose and must hover to a target position while carrying an unmod-eled payload suspended by a rod. With all learning components disabled, the nominal MPC baseline reaches a success rate of only 0.05 and a mean cost of 2.6e4, with most failures caused by crashes. The learned critic variant, which combines residual-dynamics learning with the blended critic ob-jective, raises the success rate to 0.79 and lowers the mean cost to 4.7e3. As an adaptive-control baseline, an idealized incremental nonlinear dynamic inversion (INDI) controller [17] without sen-sor noise or delay reaches a higher success rate of 0.94 , but a higher mean cost of 5.6e3, since it overshoots more and settles more slowly around the goal (Appendix E.1). In our setting, learning the dynamics model alone is not sufficient to solve the task reliably. We attribute this to the short look-ahead of 1s for the MPC, not capturing the fatal swing-up motion of the pendulum early enough, and the approximation bias of the learned dynamics model, requiring careful alignment of planning frequency, integration scheme, and look-ahead distance to trade off approximation bias with real-time-ness. However, we observe that combining the learned dynamics model with the learned critic, which injects infinite-horizon MDP information into the MPC, yields more conservative approaches towards the goal, keeping the oscillations bounded and avoiding crashing. The learned critic can drive the system away from high action-value regions that lead to failure, including the ones with high pendulum deflection. This observation is broadly in line with the remarkable success of model-free policy-gradient methods, which achieve high task performance not by meticulously modeling the underlying dynamics but by directly learning mitigation strategies that avoid task failure [18]. Learning to Plan over Contacts Using Visual and Haptic Information. In this experiment, a 6-DOF manipulator must pick up a cube and stack it precisely on top of another cube using only a sim-ple kinematic robot model and no explicit contact model. Unsurprisingly, a naive grasp-uninformed nominal MPC with a single global cost function fails completely (success rate of 0, stuck in pre-grasp pose) since it cannot reason over the grasp. Following Appendix F.1, we add to the state vector the cube’s position and a grasp-score, which is initialized from an external grasp detection using tactile sensors, and replace the global cost with a multi-phase cost function. This modification 8raises the nominal baseline to 0.361 success rate. Further learning a residual to the grasp-score state lifts the success rate to moderate 0.401 . Employing a learned critic, which is conditioned on visual depth and haptic observations (cf. Figure 9) improves task performance significantly to 0.94 success rate. We observe more delicate and better-informed recovery behaviors than with nominal MPC, including corrections after failed grasp attempts and poor approaches that tip over the target cube. More insights into the task setup and further discussions of the results are given in Appendix F.1 and Appendix F.2. 

# 6 Limitations 

A first limitation is that the solver relies on first- and second-order derivatives of the learned resid-ual and critic models, and optimization remains sensitive to irregular derivatives. This restricts the blend: with the exact critic of Section 5, lower values of λ consistently recover the performance of the optimal policy, whereas with learned critics only modest reductions from λ = 1 , roughly in the range 0.98 to 0.9, outperformed the nominal baselines in our experiments. Since the weight of the nominal rollout decays as (γλ )k along the horizon (Eq. (3)), low λ removes the model information that would compensate imperfect critic derivatives, particularly at long horizons (cf. Appendix F.2). We therefore start training at λ = 1 , lower λ only once the critic has acquired sufficient state-space coverage, and mitigate irregular derivatives by disagreement-based attenuation of the critic, smooth-ing, and clipping. Nevertheless, poor gradients can still steer the optimizer toward uninformative regions of the state space, degrade data collection early-on, and ultimately stall training; stronger training-time regularization, efficient off-policy target construction and priority-sampling from the replay-buffer should therefore be investigated. A second limitation concerns real-time deployment on a single system. While all solver computations are batched across environments, and costs and derivatives are evaluated in batch over all time steps, the forward rollout, the Riccati recursion, and the line search remain sequential over the horizon, which precludes replanning at rates above 

100 Hz in the current implementation. However, iLQR returns the time-indexed local feedback pol-icy ˆui = ui + Ki dx(ˆ xi, x i) around the planned trajectory, so the planner can run at a low rate while this policy closes the loop at the control rate. 

# 7 Conclusion 

To conclude, this paper presented a learning-augmented constrained MPC framework for planning under model mismatch. While dynamics learning enabled the planner to account for directly observ-able but incompletely modeled state variables, the main driver of the observed performance gains was the integration of a learned critic into the gradient-based optimization framework. By pro-viding corrective descent directions during the backward recursion, the critic injected long-horizon behavioral information that is inaccessible to a finite-horizon MPC objective alone. In this way, the method mitigated the inherent myopia of MPC by leveraging value information from the underlying infinite-horizon MDP. Across the reported runtime and control experiments, the framework scaled to thousands of parallel optimal control problems and improved performance under biased and in-complete dynamics models. Future work will focus on addressing the aforementioned limitations and on stronger critic pre-training, including representation learning from video demonstrations. 9A Bellman-Residual Form of the Blended Objective

The ablations in Tables 1, 2, and 3 support the findings summarized in Section 5. On this task, the main benefit comes from critic learning with informative observations, whereas residual dynamics learning yields only moderate improvement. 

Effect of planning horizon. Table 1 shows that nominal MPC works best with a medium length optimization horizon. Very short horizons do not result in a sufficiently strong grasp, keeping the robot stuck in the approach phase, while very long horizons degrade due to unsteadiness in the iLQR backward pass when optimizing over the grasp. This supports the main-text claim that the critic gives iLQR consistently a descent direc-tion aligned with the long-horizon stacking objective, leading to more directed behavior and more robust grasping.
