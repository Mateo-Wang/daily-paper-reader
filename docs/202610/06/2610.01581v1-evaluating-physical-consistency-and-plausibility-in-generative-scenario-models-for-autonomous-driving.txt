## Abstract 

Generative AI models are increasingly used for scenario generation in autonomous driving. While they can generate realistic looking scenarios, they often provide limited transparency into learned representations and consistency with real-world vehicle dynamics. This lack of formal assurance limits their use in safety-critical validation and certification workflows. To address this aspect, we introduce a lay-ered evaluation protocol that complements existing methods by assessing models across five layers. The first four layers inspect internal representations and network layers through kinematic alignment, statistical baseline comparison, latent control-lability, and activation analysis. The fifth layer evaluates model outputs against vehicle dynamics constraints such as lateral jerk thresholds. We demonstrate the protocol on a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) based scenario generator. Although standard output-level metrics and visualizations suggest that the generated scenar-ios are realistic, our protocol provides deeper insight into the extent to which the model’s latent space aligns with kinematic features and whether visually plausible trajectories satisfy vehicle-dynamics constraints. We further apply the protocol to additional generative models, demonstrating its applicability beyond the VAE architecture. 

## 1 Introduction 

Recent progress in autonomous driving research, particularly in areas such as deep reinforcement learning and transfer learning, has substantially enhanced end-to-end perception and decision-making capabilities [1, 2]. These advancements reflect the increasing complexity and adaptiveness of modern Advanced Driver Assistance System (ADAS) and autonomous systems, thereby increasing the need for more comprehensive and systematic validation methodologies [3]. Scenario-based testing in simulations plays a crucial role in validating the functionality and safety of Advanced Driving Systems (ADS) [4, 5]. Instead of relying solely on road testing or statistical mileage accumulation, scenario-based approaches evaluate system behavior under structured and reproducible driving situations. These scenarios describe interactions between multiple traffic participants, environmental conditions, infrastructure layouts, and dynamic behaviors that may lead to safety-critical outcomes [6–8]. Traditionally, scenario-based testing is resource-intensive and often relies on mathematical models that oversimplify real-world complexity, failing to capture the full spectrum of traffic scenarios. To address these limitations, the automotive industry is increasingly leveraging Artificial Intelligence (AI) [9–13] to enhance scenario-based testing. AI methods offer the potential to model complex,

real-world scenarios with greater accuracy by learning from large volumes of driving data. By integrating AI, it is possible to create more realistic and varied driving scenarios that better reflect the unpredictability and complexity of actual driving environments. Moreover, AI-powered scenario generation presents significant opportunities to explore rare and extreme events that are crucial for stress-testing ADAS and ensuring their reliability in unpredictable real-world conditions. Despite these opportunities, the industrial transferability of these models remains limited [14]. A key reason is their lack of transparency. Unlike mathematical or rule-based scenario models, where experts can inspect assumptions, constraints, and generation logic directly, AI models often provide limited visibility into how scenarios are produced. Therefore, output-level realism checks alone are insufficient for validation. Test engineers also need evidence that the model has learned driving-relevant structures, can be reliably steered toward targeted scenario variations, and can produce trajectories that remain executable under vehicle dynamics constraints. Current evaluations mainly focus on reconstruction error, visual plausibility, or distributional similarity. However, these criteria do not sufficiently assess whether generated scenarios are structurally meaningful, controllable, or physically executable. As a result, scenarios that appear realistic at the output level may still be unsuitable for industrial testing. Addressing this gap requires interpretability methods. While tools such as probing [15], latent traversal [16], and mechanistic interpretability [17, 18] have been used to study representations in image and language models, their adaptation to generative scenario modeling remains limited. In this paper, we introduce a structured evaluation protocol that directly targets these gaps for genera-tive scenario models in autonomous driving. The protocol defines five complementary evaluation layers covering latent alignment, structure beyond statistical baselines, controllability, layer-wise maneuver organization, and physical executability We instantiate the protocol on a VAE trained on real-world highway maneuvers. The VAE’s explicit latent space z offers the most direct and interpretable unit through which to explain the protocol’s logic. However, the protocol itself is not specific to VAEs. It can be transferred to other generative architectures, as shown in the Appendix A.8 with appropriate adjustments to how representation layers are defined. 

## 2 Evaluation Setup 

The protocol requires three components : the generative model we want to evaluate, the real-world dataset it was trained, and a kinematic parameter extractor to probe for physically meaningful representations. 

Model. The model used for the protocol demonstration is a VAE with a ten-dimensional latent space. The encoder maps a maneuver x ∈ R100 ×3 to an approximate posterior q(z | x), and the decoder reconstructs ˜x. We adopt a guided variant in which one latent dimension, z0, is dedicated to scenario type and trained against a class-conditional Gaussian prior with an auxiliary alignment loss. The remaining dimensions z1 through z9 are free and follow a standard Gaussian prior. The training objective thus combines reconstruction error, the standard KL term, and the alignment loss: 

Ltotal = MSE(˜ x, x) + β D KL (q(z) ∥ p(z)) + Lalign . (1) This design separates what to generate , encoded in z0, from how to generate it , encoded in z1 through 

z9. The model exhibits strong performance across standard output-level evaluations, as reported in Appendix A.4, where further details on the architecture, training configuration, validation results, and initial latent space inspection are also provided. 

Dataset. The model is trained on approximately 44,600 real world highway maneuvers collected on German highways, totalling about 1,735 hours of recording. Maneuvers are labeled into six safety-critical scenario types: cut-in left (CIL), cut-in right (CIR), cut-out left (COL), cut-out right (COR), cut-through left (CTL), and cut-through right (CTR). These scenario types describe how nearby vehicles move with respect to the ego vehicle. In a cut-in maneuver, a nearby vehicle changes lane into the ego vehicle’s lane. This may require the autonomous vehicle to react to a sudden intrusion. In a cut-out maneuver, a vehicle leaves the ego vehicle’s lane. This can create an unexpected gap or reveal a hidden traffic situation ahead, such as slow or stopped traffic. In a cut-through maneuver, a nearby vehicle crosses multiple lanes in one continuous motion. This scenario is more complex as it involves larger lateral movement and interactions across several lanes. More details about the dataset is given in Appendix A.3 2Physical Parameters. The physical parameters are extracted using the three-phase parametric driving model [19, 20]. The full model description, including the equations and phase definitions, is provided in Appendix A.2. The extracted parameter set serves as a representative baseline and can be updated, extended, or replaced according to the requirements of a specific user, dataset, or application. 

## 3 Proposed Evaluation Protocol 

The protocol has five layers, each answering one question about the model. Layers 1-4 inspect internal representations and network layers. Layer 5 inspects generated scenarios against physical constraints. 1. Layer 1. Does the latent space align with interpretable physical motion descriptors? 2. Layer 2. Is the kinematic structure observed in Layer 1, a product of learning or a reflection of the data itself? 3. Layer 3. How does the decoder transform the latent representation into a maneuver and where does scenario-level structure emerge? 4. Layer 4. Can interventions on the latent representation reliably steer generated trajectories toward user-specified physical targets? 5. Layer 5. Are the generated trajectories physically realizable by a real vehicle? 

Formal setup. Let g : Rd → RT ×C denote the decoder mapping a latent vector z to a maneuver of T timesteps and C channels. In this work, d = 10 , T = 100 , and C = 3 (time, lateral position, longitudinal velocity). For a dataset of N maneuvers, {z(i)}Ni=1 are the latent representations and 

{p(i)}Ni=1 the corresponding physical parameters extracted with p(i) ∈ RM containing M = 12 

physical quantities (9 independent and 3 derived). 

3.1 Layer 1: Latent-Physical Probing 

Probing trains a simple regressor on a frozen representation to measure what information it en-codes [15]. We adapt it to physical regression: for each parameter pj in p, a probe fj : Rd → R

predicts pj from z, evaluated by R2 under five fold cross validation. Two probes of different complexity are used. A linear probe fits a Ridge regression [21], ˆpj = w⊤z+b,which tests whether pj is encoded as a linear function of z. A nonlinear probe fits a one hidden layer MLP, ˆpj = v⊤ϕ(Wz + b1) + b2 with ReLU activation, which tests whether pj is encoded in a nonlinear or distributed form. The probing gap ∆R2 = R2

To localise where in the latent space each parameter is encoded, we additionally fit per dimension univariate regressions, ˆpj = wkzk + bk, yielding a d × M matrix of R2 values. This matrix distin-guishes disentangled encoding (a parameter localized to one dimension) from entangled encoding (a parameter distributed across dimensions). As a null model, the same procedure is applied to random vectors drawn from a standard normal, confirming that high R2 values reflect genuine representations rather than artifacts of the probing procedure. 

3.2 Layer 2: Baseline Comparison 

A high probing R2 alone does not show that the model learned anything meaningful, since any compression that preserves the data well could yield recoverable parameters. We compare against Principal Component Analysis (PCA) [22], which provides an optimal linear compression of the maneuver data in the mean squared error sense and captures all variance structure that purely statistical compression can extract. Each maneuver x(i) ∈ RT ×C is flattened and projected onto the first d principal components to obtain score vectors s(i) ∈ Rd. 3.3 Layer 3: Decoder Analysis 

Layers 1 and 2 characterize the features learned by the model and assess their interpretability. Layer 3 now opens the decoder to study how these features are transformed into a driving maneuver by its internal mechanisms. 3.4 Layer 4: Controllability via Latent Intervention 

Probing alone does not establish that the decoder uses the encoded information: a high R2 can coexist with information the decoder ignores. Layer 4 therefore intervenes on the latent space and observes the resulting change in physical parameters. We combine three complementary analyses: latent traversal to test directional consistency, polynomial fitting to characterise the form of the response, and nudge based sensitivity to produce practical control guidance. 

Latent traversal. Following the traversal protocol from disentanglement analysis [23], we vary one latent dimension at a time around the reference point z0 (the training mean): 

z(α) = z0 + α σ k ek, α ∈ [−3, 3] , (3) where σk is the standard deviation of zk across the training set. The traversal is decoded, the physical parameters are extracted, and directional consistency is measured by the Pearson correlation between traversal magnitude and parameter change, ρk,j = corr( α, p j (α)) . A high |ρk,j | indicates that the decoder responds to zk in a coherent and directionally predictable way.

Together these provide a per dimension control guide indicating which latent dimension an engineer should adjust for a target physical change, whether that change can be steered with simple linear rules or requires nonlinear correction, and how large a perturbation is needed to achieve a practically significant effect. 

3.5 Layer 5: Physical Plausibility Evaluation 

The first four layers characterize how the latent space encodes and controls maneuver parameters. Layer 5 asks a complementary question: do the trajectories the decoder produces obey the physical constraints of a real vehicle? Existing benchmarks rely on displacement based metrics which measure closeness to a reference distribution rather than executability under tire friction, actuator, and stability limits. Unless these constraints are explicitly used during training, they are difficult for the model to learn from data alone. We therefore want to test whether the decoder has implicitly internalized such constraints, or whether it can generate trajectories that appear realistic in displacement space but are physically infeasible. From the generated scenario trajectories, we compute longitudinal and lateral accelerations and lateral jerk by central differences, and check three thresholds: a friction circle constraint

4.3 Layer 3: Decoder Analysis 

The Layer 3 analysis examines how physical structure emerges within the decoder and how this structure relates to the final generated maneuver. 

Activation clustering. PCA projections of decoder activations (Figure 1) show that the six scenario types form distinct clusters from the first dense layer onward, and the discriminative structure is preserved even in the deep layers. A cut-through maneuver which is mechanically a composition of two single lane changes (CTL = CIL + COL; CTR = CIR + COR), at every layer the CTL cluster sits between the CIL and COL clusters in projection. ## 5 Discussion 

The five-layer protocol reveals behaviors that output-based evaluations alone would miss. Although standard evaluations can suggest competitive generation performance, they do not show whether the model learns transparent, controllable, or physically valid representations. Layer 1 shows partial alignment between learned representations and human-defined kinematic descriptors. This is not necessarily a failure, since kinematic descriptors often assume linear relationships while neural networks encode driving behavior nonlinearly and across multiple dimensions. However, partial alignment makes intervention harder, because target quantities may not correspond to clean latent directions. The statistical baseline comparison in Layer 2 suggests that the network learns additional structure, but remains biased by patterns already present in the dataset. Layer 3 further shows that network layers are not complete black boxes. They can be interpreted and that information is reorganized across decoder depth, so interventions may be more effective at the layers where target properties are constructed rather than only at the latent input. Layer 4 builds from Layer 1 and 2 showing some physical quantities are linearly controllable, while others require nonlinear or scenario-dependent mappings, making latent steering unreliable. Layer 5 shows that high output quality does not guarantee physical consistency, motivating physics-informed training in the future. Overall, the protocol improves transparency by showing what the model learns, how information is organized, where interventions should be applied, and whether generated scenarios remain physically executable. 

Note: We provide a detailed comparison of the proposed protocol with two state-of-the-art bench-marks, WOSAC and Waymo E2E, in Section A.9. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We introduced a five-layer protocol for evaluating generative scenario models beyond output quality alone. The protocol examines whether models learn meaningful driving structure, respond control-lably to interventions, and generate physically executable trajectories. When applied to baseline architectures trained on highway maneuver data, it revealed important behaviors that standard metrics miss, including partial kinematic alignment, nonlinear and scenario-dependent control responses, and physically invalid outputs. By making these hidden model behaviors visible, the protocol improves transparency and helps build trust for industrial adoption. These findings suggest that future genera-tive driving models should be evaluated across structural, controllability, and physical dimensions, and should be designed with stronger kinematic supervision, scenario-calibrated control mechanisms, layer-specific interventions, and physics-informed training objectives.

## A Technical appendices and supplementary material 

A.1 Related Work 

Scenario-based testing has traditionally relied on parameterized models that encode kinematic properties through expert-defined parameter sets [26–28]. While interpretable, these approaches are labor-intensive to extend and struggle to cover rare scenarios. Learning-based methods address this limitation by capturing complex interaction patterns directly from data. Examples include Variational Autoencoders [29, 9], Generative Adversarial Networks [30], large language models [10, 11], diffusion models [12, 31], and world models [32]. These methods can produce diverse and realistic scenarios, but they typically encode driving behavior in high-dimensional latent spaces whose individual dimensions do not correspond to recognizable kinematic quantities. This lack of semantic alignment complicates targeted generation, physical verification, and integration with validation pipelines. Because of this emphasis on output realism, current evaluation practice for generative scenario models has largely focused on output-level metrics. Reconstruction error and distributional similarity are widely used to assess fidelity, while displacement-based metrics such as ADE and FDE measure trajectory accuracy [33]. Some recent work further assesses physical plausibility by comparing distributions of acceleration and jerk [34, 35]. However, such comparisons measure closeness to a reference distribution rather than executability under tire friction, actuator, and stability limits. More broadly, evaluation practices remain fragmented, with limited evidence linking generation quality to ADAS validation outcomes [14]. As a result, the internal representations that give rise to generated scenarios are rarely examined, leaving controllability, traceability, and physical grounding largely unaddressed. This gap connects generative scenario testing to the broader literature on model interpretability. Interpretability research on generative models has primarily concentrated on image synthesis and language modeling. Latent traversal [16] and linear probing [15] are standard tools for examining whether latent dimensions encode semantically meaningful attributes, while disentanglement metrics quantify the independence of generative factors [36, 37]. Mechanistic interpretability methods have also begun to expose how specific behaviors emerge from network components [17, 18], although this work has been developed largely for transformer-based language and vision models. Within autonomous driving, interpretability efforts have focused mainly on perception and decision making rather than generative components [38], with some work examining latent representations of maneuver prediction models in discriminative settings [39, 40]. To the best of our knowledge, a systematic interpretability analysis of generative maneuver models that is grounded in physically meaningful kinematic parameters and combined with output-level physical plausibility checks has not yet been undertaken. 12 A.2 Mathematical Model 

A central question in this work is to determine whether the latent space of a data-driven generative model encodes physically meaningful structure. To investigate this, we need a reference represen-tation that assigns concrete physical meaning to maneuver properties something that data-driven representations do not provide by construction. The parametric model defined in this section serves precisely this role: given any maneuver produced by the generative model, the parametric model extracts a compact set of physically interpretable quantities that can then be correlated against the latent representation. We examine three highway interaction scenarios commonly used in ADAS validation: cut-in, cut-out, and cut-through. In each scenario, an actor vehicle executes a lateral maneuver relative to an ego vehicle on a highway as shown in Figure 2. In a cut-in, an actor vehicle merges into the ego lane from an adjacent lane, requiring the AV to respond to a sudden reduction in headway. In a cut-out, a vehicle ahead leaves the ego lane, abruptly exposing slower traffic or a stationary obstacle. In a cut-through, the actor crosses the ego lane entirely before returning to its original lane, introducing more complex lateral dynamics. Together, these they represent common yet safety-critical highway interactions relevant to AV validation. 

Figure 2: Overview of the considered scenario types. These highway maneuvers have an inherent temporal structure that naturally divides into three causally distinct phases, analogous to the preparation, execution, and stabilization structure identified in lane-change maneuver models [19]. The pre-maneuver phase captures approach and preparation. Before initiating a lane change, the actor vehicle establishes its conditions: closing or maintaining a gap, adjusting speed, and positioning laterally. For example, in a cut-in scenario, this is the phase where the actor approaches the ego vehicle from an adjacent lane. Lateral position remains stable during this phase, but longitudinal dynamics may already be changing with intent. The active maneuver phase is the execution window in which lateral displacement occurs. Empirical studies of lane-change kinematics consistently show that drivers apply progressive steering input, producing an S-shaped lateral displacement profile [20]. A sigmoid function captures this shape exactly and is therefore a physically motivated choice rather than a convenience approximation. This phase carries the safety-critical content of the maneuver: lateral rate, time-to-collision, and relative velocity all reach their most critical values here. The post-maneuver phase captures stabilization. After completing the lane change, the actor settles into the new lane and either maintains speed, decelerates, or accelerates depending on the scenario type. Including this phase is important because the post-maneuver longitudinal behavior affects the sustained risk exposure of the ego vehicle after the lateral event has concluded. The model is not used for maneuver generation and its sole function is parameter extraction, so its known limitations such as piecewise constant accelerations and continuity at phase boundaries are irrelevant here. 13 A.2.1 Model Definition

A.3 Dataset- Additional Details 

The dataset used for training the models is derived from real-world driving data. It combines signals from the ego vehicle’s Electronic Control Unit (ECU) with sensor measurements capturing the surrounding traffic environment. All data are recorded at a sampling rate of 50 Hz. The variables considered include the relative longitudinal and lateral positions s(t) and d(t), as well as the absolute speed v(t) of nearby vehicles. The dataset includes six types of driving maneuvers that represent common yet safety-critical traffic situations for autonomous vehicle validation. These include cut-in maneuvers to the left and right (CIL, CIR), cut-out maneuvers to the left and right (COL, COR), and cut-through maneuvers to the left and right (CTL, CTR). Cut-in maneuvers occur when a nearby vehicle merges into the ego lane, requiring the autonomous vehicle to respond to sudden intrusions. Cut-out maneuvers involve a vehicle leaving the ego lane unexpectedly, which may create hazardous gaps in traffic, particularly near congestion. Cut-through maneuvers describe situations where a vehicle crosses multiple lanes in a single movement, resulting in more complex lateral interactions (Reference in Figure 2) To construct a labeled dataset for these maneuvers, a two-stage classification approach is applied. The first stage uses rule-based filtering based on the longitudinal distance s(t) to identify candidate scenarios. The second stage applies a Time-Series Forest (TSF) model, which uses the lateral distance 

d(t) to refine and validate the classification results. Prior to classification, preprocessing steps are performed, including removal of missing data, correction for road curvature, and signal smoothing. The final dataset is organized as refined time-series data consisting of Ns trajectory samples across all six maneuver types, each observed over Nt discrete time steps. Key observations 

• Consistent ranking. Both metrics identify the VAE as the best-performing model, sup-porting that PVR captures a meaningful quality signal rather than an artefact of threshold selection. • PVR reveals per-trajectory failures. Although the GAN achieves lower WOSAC NLL than the Transformer and WorldModel, all three have near-zero PVR, indicating that many individual trajectories violate physical constraints despite matching the overall data distribu-tion. • Safety interpretation. PVR measures the proportion of generated trajectories that satisfy physically executable vehicle dynamics, providing an interpretable percentage. In contrast, WOSAC NLL is a relative distributional score that requires comparison with other models. • Complementary metrics. We recommend using both metrics: WOSAC for distributional realism and PVR for interpretable, safety-oriented validation and diagnosis.
