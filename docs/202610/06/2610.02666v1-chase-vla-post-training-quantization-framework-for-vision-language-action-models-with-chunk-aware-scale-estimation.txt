Abstract. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models map visual obser-vations and language instructions to continuous robot actions, but a diffusion-based action expert (AE) poses a key challenge for low-bit post-training quantization (PTQ). The AE is repeatedly invoked across de-noising steps and policy queries, where fixed calibration scales can be mismatched with activation ranges that vary with denoising progress and intended motion. We propose CHASE-VLA, a chunk-aware PTQ method that exploits a VLA-specific signal readily available from the policy: the generated action chunk, including its unexecuted future suf-fix. Rather than relying only on static scale matching for AE layers, CHASE-VLA combines the previously generated chunk as causal action context with denoising step group information to adapt AE activation scales. This enables W4A4 quantization of both MLP and attention pro-jections in the repeated AE without modifying the pretrained policy. On LIBERO, CHASE-VLA achieves 97.3% average success rate on π0.5

when both MLP and attention projections in the AE are quantized to W4A4, restoring FP16-level performance. CHASE-VLA also reduces the weight storage of the quantized AE linear layers by 73.4% and their single-chunk memory traffic by 70.9% and 71.2% on π0.5 and GR00T N1.6, respectively, with a predictor overhead of at most 1.26% of the saved storage. 

Keywords: Vision-Language-Action model · Post-training quantization 

# 1 Introduction 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have emerged as a foundation for em-bodied intelligence by connecting vision-language understanding with executable robot control [2, 4–6, 14, 15]. By integrating visual perception, language under-standing, high-level reasoning, and action generation within a single policy, these models allow robots to interpret natural language instructions, ground them in visual observations, and produce control commands for manipulation tasks [4, 5, 11, 13–15]. Previous VLA systems typically treat action generation as arXiv:2610.02666v1 [cs.CV] 2 Oct 2026 2 J. Hyun et al.

a lightweight output interface, where robot actions are discretized into language-compatible tokens and predicted through the Vision-Language Model (VLM) output head rather than a dedicated action-generation module [5–7, 14, 23]. In contrast, recent VLA architectures increasingly preserve the perception and reasoning capability of the VLM branch while introducing a specialized diffusion-based action expert (AE) for continuous trajectory generation [4, 9, 11, 15,19,24,26]. This transition makes the AE a central component of modern VLA policies, because it directly determines trajectory smoothness, temporal consis-tency, and control robustness during robot execution [4, 15, 24, 30, 31, 33]. Fig. 1 summarizes this architectural trend: the VLM branch provides vision-language grounding, while a separate diffusion-based AE repeatedly refines continuous action chunks for robot control. Unlike action-token decoders, a diffusion-based AE generates action chunks through iterative refinement, repeatedly processing transformer blocks across denoising steps [9, 15, 21]. This computation recurs at every replanning step throughout a rollout, so weight reads and activation movement accumulate over the entire trajectory [33]. The AE is therefore a primary target for deployment-oriented quantization because it directly affects both memory traffic and control fidelity in repeated action generation [31]. Among compression options, post-training quantization (PTQ) is a practical approach for pretrained policies be-cause it avoids the robot data collection, rollout validation, and iterative action generation required by quantization-aware training [12, 20, 22]. This makes low-bit PTQ attractive for deployment, but the quantized AE must still preserve continuous-control precision across repeated denoising and replanning through-out the executed trajectory [31, 33]. Low-bit PTQ for the AE therefore differs from language-model quantiza-tion in both sensitivity and execution pattern [1, 10, 17, 28, 33]. only the MLP projections [30, 33], leaving a large fraction of the repeated AE cost unquantized. This limitation stems from a mismatch between static cali-bration scales and the dynamic activation behavior of the repeatedly executed AE [8, 27, 33, 35]. As illustrated in Fig. 2(a), the AE is invoked in two nested loops: it refines each action chunk over multiple denoising steps and regenerates a new chunk at every replanning step throughout a rollout [4,9,15]. Across denois-ing steps, the AE produces physical control values through iterative refinement, so its activations change with the denoising step rather than following a fixed forward-pass distribution [33]. The activation range also changes with the action context. Each policy query is conditioned on a different observation, instruction, and robot state, so the generated motion, and therefore the required activation scale, can vary substantially from one chunk to the next [4, 15, 30]. Fig. 2(b) shows this behavior within individual AE linear layers, where the maximum ab-solute activation varies across both denoising steps and action contexts. A single calibration-derived scale cannot accommodate these variations without either clipping broad activations or losing resolution for fine control [33, 36]. Across re-peated denoising and replanning, the resulting local errors can accumulate into trajectory-level control errors [30]. Addressing this mismatch requires activation scales that depend on both the denoising step and the motion being gener-ated [8, 27, 36]. However, the action context of the current chunk is not causally available before the AE produces that chunk, making direct current-chunk scale selection impractical. Chunk-based VLA execution provides a causal source of action context for resolving this causality constraint. Modern VLA policies predict short action chunks at each policy query instead of a single immediate action [2, 4, 9, 15]. 4 J. Hyun et al. 

Each chunk constitutes a short-horizon plan conditioned on the current obser-vation, language instruction, and robot state. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the robot executes only an initial prefix of the chunk and then replans after receiving a new observation [2, 4, 9]. The remaining suffix is not executed because the next policy query generates a new chunk based on the updated observation. How-ever, this suffix is still the policy’s predicted continuation of the same trajectory whose prefix is executed. Thus, it provides an accessible signal of the intended near-future motion without additional rollouts or task-specific annotations. We propose CHASE-VLA, a chunk-aware PTQ method for low-bit quan-tization of diffusion-based VLA AEs. Instead of using fixed calibration scales, CHASE-VLA modulates calibrated activation scales with action-conditioned fac-tors that are shared within groups of denoising steps. The conditioning signal is the previously generated action chunk, which compactly summarizes the in-tended near-future motion and enables scale selection to reflect both action con-text and denoising progress without modifying the AE architecture. Through adaptive scaling, CHASE-VLA supports 4-bit weight and activation quantiza-tion (W4A4) and mitigates the scale mismatch that typically arises from static PTQ. Unlike previous VLA PTQ methods, which keep sensitive attention pro-jections in floating point for stability, CHASE-VLA extends W4A4 coverage beyond MLP layers to the attention projection layers within the repeated AE. By including these repeatedly invoked projections in the low-bit quantization target, CHASE-VLA achieves further reductions in memory traffic compared to MLP-only AE quantization. Our contributions are summarized as follows. 

• We identify activation-scale mismatch across denoising steps and action con-texts as a central barrier to low-bit PTQ for diffusion-based VLA AEs. 

• CHASE-VLA estimates activation scales from the previous action chunk and applies them at the denoising step group level. 

• CHASE-VLA expands the W4A4 AE target from MLP projections to both MLP and attention projections while keeping the pretrained policy and the AE architecture unchanged. 

• Experiments on LIBERO across π0.5, GR00T N1.6, and CogACT show that this design largely preserves task success under the expanded W4A4 target, while an efficiency analysis on π0.5 and GR00T N1.6 quantifies reductions in quantized AE linear weights and single-chunk memory traffic with modest predictor overhead. 

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 Vision-Language-Action Models 

Recent VLA research has shifted from discrete action prediction to continuous trajectories generated by diffusion-based AEs. Diffusion Policy demonstrated that modeling robot actions as a conditional denoising process is effective for gen-erating smooth and multimodal action trajectories [9]. Following this direction, CHASE-VLA: PTQ Framework for VLA Models 5

recent systems such as CogACT, π0.5, GR00T, and RDT employ diffusion-based action generation modules to couple high-level vision-language reasoning with low-level continuous control [2–4, 15, 19]. These AE architectures are attractive because they predict action chunks rather than isolated actions, allowing tempo-rally correlated future controls to be generated as a coherent trajectory [2, 3, 9]. The chunk-based formulation enhances trajectory smoothness and execu-tion reliability, which are critical for contact-rich manipulation and long-horizon robotic tasks [2, 9, 15, 19]. However, this property introduces a substantial de-ployment burden as the AE repeatedly evaluates action chunks during multiple denoising steps. The AE is therefore a major determinant of continuous-control quality and a key target for low-bit deployment [30, 33]. 

2.2 Efficient VLA Inference and Compression 

Recent studies have explored several approaches to reducing the inference cost of VLA models without sacrificing their embodied control capabilities. TinyVLA combines compact multimodal transformers with lightweight diffusion-policy heads, while EfficientVLA accelerates inference by pruning redundant language layers and reusing intermediate representations [25, 32]. VLA-Cache further im-proves runtime efficiency by reusing cached key-value features for static visual tokens, and MoLe-VLA dynamically skips non-essential computation through mixture-of-layers routing in the language backbone [29,34]. Most of these efforts primarily modify the model architecture or inference execution path, while the numerical precision of VLA policies remains less explored. For lightweight de-ployment under tight memory and bandwidth budgets, this leaves quantization as an important requirement beyond architectural or execution-level optimiza-tion. This requirement is particularly relevant to diffusion-based AEs, where low-bit inference must preserve action-chunk-dependent activation distributions and denoising step dynamics. 

2.3 Post-Training Quantization for LLMs, VLMs, DiTs, and VLAs 

For VLA AE quantization, a central distinction is not only quantization scope but also the information used for activation-scale selection during iterative action generation. These methods provide useful building blocks for quan-tizing transformer backbones in VLA systems, where semantic grounding and multimodal reasoning are handled by the VLM branch. Quantization has also been studied for diffusion models and diffusion trans-formers (DiT), whose activation statistics vary across denoising steps [8, 27]. More recently, VLA-specific PTQ methods have begun to address the nu-merical sensitivity of VLA pipelines directly. QuantVLA analyzes scale drift in DiT-based VLA AEs and introduces calibration-based stabilization for VLA PTQ [33]. Its AE quantization uses a selective target: attention projections are kept in floating point while the low-bit target focuses on MLP projections [33]. DA-PTQ addresses a complementary cross-space mismatch by aligning the con-ditioning representation passed from the vision-language backbone to the action decoder [30]. This conditioning-pathway alignment is distinct from adapting ac-tivation scales inside the repeatedly executed AE. Together, these VLA-specific methods improve PTQ stability but still leave open the issue of W4A4 quantization across both MLP and attention projections within the AE. Rather than relying only on static calibration statistics, de-noising step cues, or cross-module representation alignment, CHASE-VLA uses the previously generated action chunk and denoising step groups as condition-ing signals for activation-scale estimation. 3.2 Chunk-Aware Scale Estimation 

To address this variation, activation-scale selection should consider both the de-noising step and the specific action being generated. A fixed calibration scale ignores both factors, while a denoising-step-only rule still assigns identical scales to different action chunks processed at the same denoising step. As illustrated in Fig. 3, CHASE-VLA overcomes these limitations by retaining the quantization operator in Eq. 3 while reusing the previously generated action chunk to precom-pute group-wise activation scales for generating the next action chunk. Instead of predicting an absolute scale independently, CHASE-VLA predicts a multi-plicative adjustment to a calibration-derived base scale, utilizing the generated action context and the denoising step group. CHASE-VLA derives action context from action chunks previously predicted by the policy. For a policy query at time t, the preceding query at t−τ generated a full action chunk At−τ , and the robot executed only its initial τ actions before replanning. Although the remaining suffix is unexecuted, it represents the pol-icy’s predicted continuation of the executed prefix and thus encodes the intended near-future motion. Therefore, as shown in Fig. 3, CHASE-VLA conditions the activation scale for the current chunk At on the entire previous chunk, including both the prefix and the suffix. 3.3 Quantization Targets and Inference Path 

CHASE-VLA focuses W4A4 quantization on repeated linear projections inside the diffusion AE, where the same projections are invoked across denoising steps and policy queries. The adaptive W4A4 target therefore includes feed forward MLP projections and linear projections in the attention modules of the AE. CHASE-VLA: PTQ Framework for VLA Models 11 

These layers lie on the repeated denoising path, where activation-scale mismatch directly affects low-bit action generation. This target coverage is more aggressive than that of previous VLA PTQ methods, which typically quantize only the MLP in the AE while leaving atten-tion projections in floating point. The difference is important because attention projections are also invoked at every denoising step; keeping them in floating point leaves a substantial repeated data-movement cost within the AE. As de-scribed in Sec. 3.2, CHASE-VLA adapts the activation scale to the predicted action context and denoising step group, allowing attention projections to re-main within the W4A4 AE target instead of being left in floating point. As shown in Fig. 3, CHASE-VLA acts as a scale update module for the AE during inference. Since the previous chunk is not available for the first policy query, the target linear layers use the initial scales in Eq. 4. Once the AE gener-ates At, CHASE-VLA immediately maps vec( At) to group-wise scaling factors and updates the activation scales for all target layers and denoising step groups of the next policy query at time t + τ . # 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

CHASE-VLA is evaluated on three VLA models, π0.5, GR00T N1.6, and Co-gACT, which represent diverse AE architectures and model scales [2, 3, 15]. All models are evaluated on the four LIBERO task suites: Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long [18], and we report the task success rate averaged over 20 episodes for each task within a suite. We compare CHASE-VLA with the FP16 reference, QuantVLA [33], and dif-fusion PTQ baselines [8,27] adapted to the AE. All compared PTQ methods use the same calibration set and evaluation protocol within each model. For the dif-fusion PTQ baselines, we apply channel-wise salience balancing and Spearman’s 

ρ-guided salience calibration for PTQ4DiT, and automatic granularity allocation with sample-wise dynamic activation quantization for Q-DiT. QuantVLA is im-plemented by following its calibration and static scale-matching procedure using attention temperature matching and output head balancing. Each evaluation fo-cuses on the AE, while the VLM linear layers are quantized using low-bit PTQ methods with the LLM-oriented PTQ scheme [16], matching the VLM treat-ment in QuantVLA [33], ensuring that the reported differences are due solely to AE quantization. # 5 Conclusion 

We introduced CHASE-VLA, a PTQ method that utilizes the generated ac-tion chunk as a causal signal for activation-scale estimation in diffusion-based VLA AEs. By applying action-conditioned scaling at the denoising step group level, CHASE-VLA enables W4A4 quantization of both MLP and attention pro-jections in the repeated AE without requiring modifications to the pretrained policy. With W4A4 precision for AE linear layers, CHASE-VLA restores the FP16-level average LIBERO success on π0.5 with 97.3%, and maintains 90.5% and 91.5% average success on CogACT and GR00T N1.6. It reduces the weight storage of the quantized AE linear layers by 73.4% on both π0.5 and GR00T N1.6, and decreases single-chunk memory traffic by 70.9% and 71.2%, with predictor overheads of only 1.26% and 0.84% of the saved storage, respectively.

# 4 CHASE-VLA Post-Training Quantization Details 

4.1 Calibration Setup 

In CHASE-VLA, post-training calibration estimates the quantization scales of the target AE linear layers and the activation-scale statistics used to fit the CHASE-VLA: PTQ Framework for VLA Models 21 

predictors. Calibration is performed with the pretrained VLA policy and AE weights kept frozen throughout the procedure. For each LIBERO evaluation suite, we construct a calibration set by randomly sampling two trajectories from each of the other LIBERO suites. The evaluated suite is excluded from this sampling process, which prevents trajectory overlap between the calibration and evaluation sets. The set is used only to estimate activation-scale statistics and to fit the scale predictors. 

4.2 Weight Quantization and Denoising Step Groups 

The quantization configuration is fixed from calibration through inference. For the quantized AE linear layer weights, we apply symmetric per-row 4-bit quan-tization with the round-to-nearest method. The denoising steps are partitioned once for each AE, and the resulting M groups are reused throughout calibration and evaluation. For π0.5 and CogACT, which use K = 10 denoising steps, the groups have sizes 3, 4, and 3 and correspond to step indices {0, 1, 2}, {3, 4, 5, 6},and {7, 8, 9}. For GR00T N1.6, which uses K = 4 denoising steps, the groups have sizes 2 and 2 and correspond to step indices {0, 1} and {2, 3}.
